# Laboratory catalyst leaders are often not the plant-level optimum

## Abstract

Catalysts are ranked by descriptors or laboratory performance, yet a plant selects the catalyst with the lowest production cost at optimized operation. We couple catalyst kinetics to process optimization and catalyst-responsive costing, and invert the chain for the properties that cost parity requires. In ammonia synthesis the Ru price alone turns the intrinsic order Ru > Os > Fe into Fe > Ru > Os. With measured rate laws, supported Ru/C with metal recovery undercuts fused iron whenever its Ru is financed below 4.6–6.5% per year, and the least-cost converter places fused iron upstream of Ru/C, as industrial Ru converters do. An extraction agent runs published catalysts through the same chain: comparing catalysts at their best measured conditions, the space-time-yield leader is not the plant-level optimum in 21 of 63 methanol comparisons between catalysts (33%), nor is the catalyst with the highest rate in 12 of 44 ammonia comparisons (27%).

## Introduction

Catalysts are ranked long before any plant is built. Computational screens rank them by adsorption energies, reactivity descriptors, scaling relations, volcano plots and surrogate models,[1–6] microkinetic and multiscale models link these quantities to rates and operating conditions,[6–8] and experimental studies rank catalysts by activity, selectivity and space-time yield. Both rankings stand in for a decision taken at plant scale, where a catalyst acts through reactor operation, separation, recycle and cost. Descriptors have been linked to reactor design and catalyst selection for ammonia synthesis,[9] catalyst and process design have been posed as coupled problems,[10,11] and laboratory rankings depend on the test environment.[12] A plant, however, acts on the identity of the leader: a screen can stay correlated while the catalyst a plant would select changes, and the price of a catalyst per kilogram is a poor guide to its cost per unit product once stability and scale enter.[13] Process studies of Fe and Ru ammonia catalysts find that the preferred catalyst and pressure depend on plant scale and loop configuration.[14,15] How often the leader of a published catalyst comparison survives candidate-specific process optimization and costing has not been measured.

Here we take catalyst properties forward through kinetics, candidate-specific process optimization and a catalyst-responsive cost model, and backward from an economic target to the catalyst property it requires, testing whether that property lies within reach of the underlying scaling relations.[10,11,16] We distinguish three optima: the atomic optimum, set by a descriptor and the intrinsic turnover frequency; the laboratory optimum, the leader of a published comparison; and the plant-level optimum, the catalyst with the lowest cost once its operation has been optimized under stated process and economic conditions (Methods). Three systems span the steps between them (Fig. 1a). Ammonia synthesis, in which intrinsic activity and metal price set the catalyst inventory and operating conditions,[5,17,18] runs from the atomic to the plant-level optimum; CO₂ hydrogenation to methanol, in which selectivity sets reactant loss, purge, recycle and compression,[19–21] runs from the laboratory to the plant-level optimum; and Au/TiO₂ CO oxidation, in which particle size maps monotonically onto catalyst requirement,[22–25] is the control. An independent reactor study of CO₂ methanation[26] tests the decision criterion outside our own models. Language-model agents can now read the chemical literature and operate computational tools;[27–31] we use one, the Adaptive Catalyst Screening Agent (ACSA), to run the same chain on every catalyst that published papers report and so measure how often the laboratory leader of a published comparison is not the plant-level optimum.

## Results

### Process economics reverse the leading ammonia ranking

The 15-metal ammonia screen ranks Ru, Os and Fe first, second and third in intrinsic activity (Fig. 1c), as established for transition-metal ammonia synthesis.[17,18] When the operating conditions are optimized separately for each catalyst over 14,510 process states and the catalyst-dependent costs are evaluated (Fig. 1a,d,e), the order becomes Fe, Ru and Os, at 11.52, 16.63 and 19.75 US dollars per tonne of NH₃ (Fig. 1b,h). Across all 15 metals the Spearman correlation between the two rankings remains 0.911, whereas among the three leaders ρ = −0.50 (Fig. 1f,g): the screen stays correlated while the selected catalyst changes. Selecting Ru instead of Fe raises the catalyst-dependent cost by 44.4%, the normalized decision regret. The difference of 5.12 US dollars per tonne is a small part of the cost of ammonia from state-of-the-art gas-based plants, about 160 US dollars per tonne,[32] but it is the part that catalyst selection controls: 1.77 million US dollars per year for a 1,000 t/d plant (Supplementary Note 1).

### Published laboratory leaders are often not the plant-cost leaders

Whether such reversals are common depends on the leaders that papers report. ACSA applies the same kinetic, process and economic models to every candidate that publications or databases provide (Fig. 2a). It records every catalyst entry of a paper and its Supporting Information with the location of each value, reproduces the frozen results of all three hand-built cases before scoring a new candidate, and uses a lower bound on cost to allocate the full process optimization. Scored on 50 methanol papers (Supplementary Table 1) against two curated datasets,[33,34] the agent recovers 719 of the 741 curated entries (97%) and transcribes 92–100% of printed values correctly; most remaining errors are plot readings (Extended Data Fig. 1; Supplementary Note 2). In methanol, the lower bound spares the full plant optimization for 394 of 632 candidates (62.3%) and still finds the plant-cost leader of all 81 comparisons, at 38% of the compute (Extended Data Fig. 2).

Methanol papers rank catalysts by laboratory performance, most often by space-time yield. Two extractions with the same pipeline supply 699 operating points with printed conversion, selectivity, temperature, pressure and H₂/CO₂ ratio from 61 papers (Supplementary Tables 1 and 1b; Methods). Entries of one paper tested at the same pressure, H₂/CO₂ ratio and space velocity form the comparison the paper itself makes: 81 comparisons with 632 entries in 50 papers. Each entry enters the methanol recycle–economics model at its own conditions; as in a working loop, CO, methane and N₂ together may make up at most 6.86% of the reactor inlet, the limit of the loop that anchors the cost model,[35] and the purge is optimized within that limit (Methods).

A plant chooses the operating point of each catalyst and how much of it to load. Each catalyst therefore enters the comparison at the best point at which it was measured, and each entry may load more catalyst to raise its single-pass conversion towards equilibrium (at most 95% of it, at the measured selectivity); what counts as a different catalyst (composition, loading of the active component, support or synthesis) was fixed before the comparisons were counted (Methods). In 21 of the 63 comparisons that contain at least two catalysts (33%; 95% confidence interval 22–46% from resampling papers), from 19 papers, the catalyst with the highest space-time yield is not the catalyst with the lowest plant cost (Fig. 2b), and choosing the laboratory leader costs more than 5% extra in 10 of them. With every entry held at its laboratory conversion the count is 26 of the 63 (41%; 19 above 5%), so the two treatments of catalyst amount place the share of comparisons in which the plant selects another catalyst at 33–41%. Over every operating point of every catalyst, the space-time-yield leader is not the plant-cost leader in 50 of 81 comparisons at laboratory conversion (62%; 49–72%), a total that also counts changes of operating point (Supplementary Note 3). These carry their own result: of 112 catalysts measured at two or more temperatures, 82 reach their lowest plant cost at another temperature than their highest space-time yield, 75 of them at a lower one, by a median of 50 °C.

The disagreements are not a sampling or measurement artefact (Extended Data Fig. 3): re-measuring every entry 2,000 times with the measurement uncertainty of the Re/TiO₂ states keeps 42 of the 50 point-level disagreements in at least 90% of re-measurements. They hold without the study that supplies most comparisons (43 of 73), under every by-product closure, CO treatment, catalyst price and plant-term variant tested (41–56 of 81) and for inlet limits of 5–10% (42–50 of 81; Supplementary Note 3). Without any inlet limit, which lets a diluted feed react as in the laboratory, the leaders differ in 25 of 81 comparisons; limiting methane and N₂ alone gives 28 of 81, so the rise from 25 to 50 comes from CO build-up. The plant model reproduces the hydrogen and CO₂ consumption, carbon efficiency and recycle ratio of published plant assessments and the break-even methanol price of one of them to within 2.4% (Extended Data Fig. 4).

The plant ranking follows from what each by-product does in a recycle loop (Fig. 2d,e). At the cost-optimal purge, recycled CO is barely converted beyond what the shift and CO-hydrogenation equilibria require and accumulates: without the inlet limit the reactor feed carries a median 8.8% CO, against 1.3–4.7% in published loop designs. Holding the feed to the limit costs purge, and the purge a catalyst needs grows with the CO and methane it makes: the plant-cost leader is more selective to methanol than the paper leader in 40 of the 50 disagreements and makes less CO in 39. On m-ZrO₂, InNi₃C₀.₅ gives the highest space-time yield of its four supports at 300 °C and 4 MPa (8.5% conversion, 88% methanol selectivity, 12% CO) but needs a 4.0% purge and costs 1,289 euros per tonne, against 1,138 on SiO₂ (2.2% conversion, 95% selectivity, 5% CO) at 0.6% purge. No single laboratory metric replaces the plant model: ranking by conversion disagrees in 54 of 81 comparisons and ranking by methanol selectivity in 30 of 81. A high-pressure Cu/Al₂O₃ study that supplies 8 of the 81 comparisons[36] gives the largest regrets without the limit.

Ammonia papers rank catalysts by rate. ACSA extracted 52 primary ammonia-synthesis papers with the same pipeline (Supplementary Tables 2 and 2b); each catalyst's measured rate is inverted into an effective descriptor on its metal's side of the volcano, and the catalyst is optimized over the full process library with its own metal content (Methods). In 12 of the 44 comparisons in 31 papers (27%; 95% confidence interval 14–42%), the catalyst with the highest rate per gram of catalyst is not the plant-cost leader, 8 with a cost gap above 5% (Fig. 2c). In 3 of them the plant-cost leader is an Fe catalyst on the same support with the lower rate (Fig. 2f), and in 5 a commercial fused-iron catalyst that the paper includes as a reference; without these references the leaders differ in 8 of 44. Across the 73 catalysts tabulated in a review of ammonia-synthesis catalysts,[37] laboratory rate and plant cost are uncorrelated (Spearman ρ = 0.07; Extended Data Fig. 5; Supplementary Note 4).

### Selectivity, recycle and purge reshape the methanol ranking

Four Re/TiO₂ catalyst–temperature states resolve the mechanism behind the methanol result (Fig. 3a,b).[19] By space-time yield per gram of Re, 1 wt% Re at 250 °C leads. After the recycle and separation model at 2% purge, 5 wt% Re at 200 °C, third by space-time yield, is the least-cost state at 943 euros per tonne, against 962 for the space-time-yield winner (Fig. 3c), and two of six pairwise orderings invert (Fig. 3e). Within the 6.86% inlet limit, at each state's optimal purge, the winner stays second at 6.6% regret, and no purge level between 0.5% and 40% makes it the least-cost state (Fig. 3f). Methane suppression has a local economic leverage of 0.376, against 0.0588 for single-pass conversion and 0.00289 for space-time yield (Fig. 3a,g), because methane consumes reactants and accumulates in the loop, raising purge, recycle and compression.[20,21] Moving every state's methane selectivity into methanol reverses the rank correlation to ρ = −0.60, whereas equalizing conversion raises it to +1.00. No increase in space-time yield brings the space-time-yield winner to parity, since unlimited productivity still costs 955 euros per tonne against 943; raising its single-pass conversion from 0.23 to 0.279, inside the range measured on the same catalyst family, or reaching complete methanol selectivity does. Propagating each state's own measurement uncertainty through the plant model keeps the economic winner first in 91.2% of 5,000 draws (Fig. 3d; Supplementary Note 5).

### Alloy surfaces that undercut Fe pair a cheap 3d metal with a group-6 metal

ACSA took the 1,796 bimetallic and pure-metal surfaces of a high-throughput adsorption database[38,39] through the ammonia chain (Methods). Of the 372 surfaces that contain only transition metals outside groups 3–5, 137 satisfy the 90 m³ bed-volume limit. Eight undercut Fe when the database is bridged linearly onto the model's descriptor and nine under an element-anchored bridge, and every one of them pairs Fe, Co, Ni or Cu with Cr, Mo or W (Extended Data Fig. 6), the combination that descriptor interpolation has identified for ammonia synthesis.[40] Surfaces with nitride-forming group 3–5 elements or sp metals, outside the transition-metal scaling relations of the activity model, are scored as separate layers (Supplementary Table 3). The most active surface, Ni₃Re, ranks 12th economically (28.0% regret). The descriptor-only lower bound excludes 1,399 of the 1,695 priced surfaces (82.5%) from the full optimization without excluding any that undercut Fe (Supplementary Note 6).

### What Ru must cost to match fused iron

The ammonia reversal has one cause, the price of Ru. Converting turnover frequency into the metal a plant must hold preserves the intrinsic order; charging that metal at its price over a 10-year life gives Fe < Ru < Os, with annualized metal replacement of 0.204 US dollars per tonne for Fe against 4.09 for Ru, before any reactor, compression, refrigeration or equipment cost is added. Priced as iron, at 8 US dollars per kilogram, and reoptimized, Ru is the least-cost catalyst (10.24 against 11.52 US dollars per tonne), and a price sweep with reoptimization at every price places Fe–Ru parity at 404 US dollars per kilogram (Fig. 4d), 99% below the Ru price of the model. Process optimization does not close the gap but moves it into compression: Ru moves to about 450 °C and 220 bar, where a small bed holds little metal, while Fe operates near 425 °C and 105 bar (Fig. 4b,c), and the 5.12 US dollars per tonne between them comes mainly from fresh-feed compression (+3.988), metal inventory (+1.694) and compressor capital (+1.024) (Fig. 4a,e).

The order holds across the uncertainty of the prices and of the model itself. In 5,000 draws of metal prices, capital cost, electricity price and catalyst lifetime, each with full reoptimization, Fe is cheaper in every draw, with a minimum difference of 1.99 US dollars per tonne (Fig. 4h); descriptor uncertainty matters more, with Fe first in 65.3% of 1,000 descriptor samples, mostly because the Fe bed otherwise exceeds the 90 m³ limit (Fig. 4f,g). Against 64 values from industrial ammonia loops and converters, the model deviates most in converter-inlet NH₃ (typically 63% low), compressor power (42% low) and the bed volume of cost-optimal converter designs (73% low), and lies within tolerance for every flow and bed volume at plant conditions and five of six space velocities (typical deviations 16–30%; Supplementary Table 4). Applying each deviation, in either direction and at its typical or its largest size, to the model component that produces it and reoptimizing both catalysts never puts Ru ahead of Fe, with a smallest margin of 1.34 US dollars per tonne; nor does any combination of directions with all five components at their typical deviation (smallest margin 2.39; Supplementary Note 7).

A real Ru catalyst holds far less metal than the benchmark bed. A promoted Ru/C catalyst exposes 11% of its Ru atoms,[41] against fewer than 1% of the Fe atoms in reduced fused iron;[42] the commercial catalyst carries about 8 wt% Ru,[43] and 90–95.55% of the Ru in spent promoted Ru/C ammonia catalysts is recovered.[44,45] For this pair the descriptor kinetics depart furthest from measurement: at 90 bar, 400 °C and 2% ammonia they give Ru apparent orders of +2.4 in H₂ and −1.9 in NH₃, against +0.3 and −0.4 for promoted Ru/C, and fused iron −0.1 in NH₃ against −0.9 measured. Every comparison of supported Ru/C with fused iron therefore uses measured rate laws: a law fitted to commercial fused-iron data from 1 to 324 bar, with the activity of the industrial magnetite catalyst whose data Dyson and Simon fitted,[46–48] and the kinetics of the promoted Ru/C catalyst, fitted at 50–100 bar,[41] each integrated along the bed and each catalyst optimized only at the pressures its law was fitted on (Methods). Fused iron then costs 15.31 US dollars per tonne at 450 °C and 110 bar and Ru in the benchmark formulation 45.87 at 100 bar; to match fused iron, bulk Ru would need a 16-fold activity gain (16–45-fold across bed-sizing and calibration variants) or a 94% lower price, 3,371 US dollars per kilogram.

Supported Ru/C with 95.55% recovery (8 wt% Ru, 490 kg m⁻³) has its optimum at 100 bar, and its cost relative to fused iron is set by the annual cost of holding its Ru (Fig. 5a). With the metal owned and no charge on it, Ru/C costs 3.83 US dollars per tonne less than fused iron; financed over the 25-year plant life at the 12% per year applied to equipment it costs 2.13 more, and it is the cheaper catalyst at any rate below 6.49% per year. In a KAAP-type loop at 9 MPa with a −20 °C refrigerated condenser,[49] where both rate laws hold at the same operating point, the differences are 1.21 less and 1.95 more, with break-even at 4.57% per year. No Ru lease rate is published. One refiner paid 1.35–5.10% per year, with a median of 2.10%, on 32 fixed-rate platinum and palladium catalyst-metal leases between 2013 and 2022;[50] the annual fee on the metal value is the cost of holding catalyst metal, the role the financing rate plays in our cash flow, so we take 2.10% per year as the reference rate and keep 0% and 12% as bounds. At the reference rate Ru/C costs 1.99 US dollars per tonne less than fused iron at their own optima and 0.65 less in the KAAP-type loop; priced as a supplier lease, the break-even rates are 6.81 and 5.00% per year. Across 16 variants of the price, catalyst and sizing inputs, the break-even rate spans 1.23–15.65% per year at the own optima and 0.71–8.43% per year in the KAAP-type loop (Extended Data Fig. 7). Benchmark deviations of typical size, applied one at a time, shift the difference by at most 2.3 US dollars per tonne without financing and 1.2 at 12% per year and reverse the order at neither rate (Supplementary Table 4).

### Fused iron upstream of Ru/C, as in industrial converters

With both catalysts available to a three-bed adiabatic converter with interbed cooling at 9 MPa, the optimizer chooses the catalyst of each bed. Fused iron in the first, hottest bed with Ru/C downstream is the least-cost design for Ru financing rates from 0 to 5.46% per year, and an all-iron converter above that rate; no design with Ru/C upstream of fused iron is optimal at any rate (Fig. 5b). This is the bed order of the Kellogg Advanced Ammonia Process converter, a magnetite bed followed by Ru/C beds,[49] and of published Fe + Ru multibed designs,[51] reached without being imposed. At the reference rate the converter is fused iron followed by two Ru/C beds, with fused iron 65% of the catalyst volume, against about half in the KAAP converter,[52] and it costs 0.37 US dollars per tonne less than the best all-iron converter (Fig. 5c). Ru/C goes downstream because ammonia inhibits fused iron more strongly (apparent NH₃ orders −0.89 and −0.39): per kilogram, the Ru/C rate exceeds the fused-iron rate 9-fold at 2% NH₃ and 62-fold at 14% (Fig. 5d), while the first bed runs above the 460 °C up to which the Ru/C rate law was measured. The window moves with recovery and Ru price (0–4.92% per year at 90% recovery; 0.12–8.21% and 0–4.03% at a 30% lower and higher price), and Ru/C never moves upstream of fused iron.

### Lifecycle engineering, not activity, brings Ru to parity

Multiplying Ru intrinsic activity by a factor α and reoptimizing the process at each value, Ru reaches the Fe cost only after a 115-fold increase (44–262-fold, 5th–95th percentile under economic uncertainty; Fig. 6a). The scaling relation allows at most 1.02-fold at 673 K and 1.58-fold across all process conditions, and the lowest strict-scaling Ru cost, 16.45 US dollars per tonne at *E*_N = −1.180 eV, remains above the Fe cost (Fig. 6b). Gains of this size come from leaving the scaling relation (Fig. 6a, bottom): against unpromoted Ru, Ba and Cs promotion of Ru/C raise the turnover frequency per surface Ru 75- and 65-fold,[53] Ba promotion of Ru/BN more than 100-fold[54] and Cs promotion of Ru/MgO more than 134-fold,[55] all within the parity band, whereas electride and oxyhydride supports give about tenfold[56,57] and confinement inside carbon nanotubes 0.5-fold.[58] The Ru/C catalyst of the measured kinetics already carries Ba, Cs and K and in the benchmark bed still needs a 16-fold gain; the remaining gap is the cost of the metal, which dispersion and recovery close (Table 1).

Improving Ru recovery and catalyst lifetime together removes the activity requirement: at 99% recovery, a 10-year lifetime needs at most a 1.29-fold gain and a 20-year lifetime none (Fig. 6c). On the strict *E*_N scaling manifold across all 14,510 process states, parity is controlled by the catalyst-loss factor \(q=(1-r)/L\), with \(q^*=8.25\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{y}^{-1}\): at a 20-year lifetime, parity requires 98.35% recovery, and at 99% recovery the best strict-scaling state costs 11.20 US dollars per tonne, 0.32 below Fe (Fig. 6d; Supplementary Note 8). Lifecycle engineering reaches parity inside the scaling-constrained design space, where activity alone cannot.

### Without a price or selectivity channel the ranking holds, and catalyst life sets its limit

In the Au/TiO₂ CO-oxidation control, with the rate anchor of Janssens et al.[24] and the particle-size dependence of Overbury et al.,[25] 2–6 nm particles share one metal, loading, operating condition and process model. The price channel that reverses ammonia is therefore absent, and the required catalyst mass follows the mass activity: 6 nm particles need 8.1 times the catalyst of 2 nm particles. With an independent activity scatter for every particle size (σ = 0.25 in natural-log units) the 2 nm catalyst remains the least-cost catalyst in 99.4% of 10,000 draws and the full order 2 > 3 > 4 > 5 > 6 nm holds in 77.7%; with candidate-specific kinetic and operating variation over 273–293 K it holds in 92.2% (Extended Data Fig. 8). The one channel that can reverse the order is catalyst life. If life grows with particle size as *d*^λ, catalyst cost scales as *d*^(*n*+*m*−λ), where *n* + *m* = 1.9 (1.5–3.2) is the size exponent of the catalyst requirement, so the order inverts above λ = 1.9. The exponent λ is a model parameter: powder Au/TiO₂ at 273–313 K loses activity through carbonate accumulation at the particle perimeter without particle growth,[59,60] which corresponds to λ ≈ 0, whereas where Ostwald ripening controls life, its kinetics and the size-dependent stability of sub-3 nm Au on TiO₂[61] place λ at 3–4.

The three systems span both outcomes of cross-scale propagation. The realized pairwise transfer index χ_AB = ln(J_A/J_B)/ln(s_A/s_B), where s_A > s_B is the higher-is-better upstream property and J the lower-is-better plant objective, puts them on one scale: χ ≈ −1.00 for Au, whose 2 nm particles have 8.1 times the mass activity of 6 nm particles and need 8.1 times less catalyst, against +0.015 for methanol, where a 3.6-fold space-time-yield advantage comes with a 1.9% higher production cost, and +0.057 for ammonia, where Ru has 607 times the intrinsic turnover frequency of Fe and a 44% higher catalyst-dependent cost (Supplementary Note 9).

### An external case: Ru against Ni in CO₂ methanation

The decision structure of ammonia recurs in a study whose reactor model is independent of ours. For CO₂ methanation, Moioli and Züttel[26] found that 0.5 wt% Ru/Al₂O₃ reaches 99.5% conversion in one stage, whereas Ni/Al₂O₃ needs 1.89 times the catalyst volume and an intermediate water-condensation stage, and recommended Ru for small and Ni for large plants. Before taking their volume ratio and catalyst-cost bases through our inventory, reactor and replacement chain, with the metal prices of the ammonia case, we fixed the decision criterion (Methods): Ru is cheaper when *r*_V(1 + φ_proc) > Π, where *r*_V is the Ni-to-Ru catalyst volume ratio, Π the Ru-to-Ni price ratio per kilogram of catalyst and φ_proc the cost of the second Ni stage per unit of Ni catalyst cost. Π = 5.18 exceeds *r*_V = 1.89, so on catalyst inventory alone Ni is cheaper at every scale, as Fe is in ammonia; Ru is cheaper only while the second Ni stage, whose capital has a fixed part, outweighs the Ru price. This holds below 0.60 MW of synthetic natural gas at the central parameters (Monte Carlo median 0.86 MW, 5–95% range 0.18–7.0 MW), with Ru cheaper in 98.3% of draws at 0.1 MW and 0.05% at 100 MW and in every deterministic variant at 0.1 MW and none at 100 MW (Extended Data Fig. 9; Supplementary Note 10).

## Discussion

A catalyst ranking carries from one optimum to the next only as far as the upstream advantage reaches the plant objective. In ammonia, the price of the metal held in the bed intervenes, and process optimization turns it into compression as Ru moves to high pressure and a small bed; in methanol, the reactant lost to purge intervenes; in Au/TiO₂, where particle size maps monotonically onto catalyst requirement, nothing intervenes, and the ranking holds for as long as catalyst life does not grow with particle size. In CO₂ methanation the same criterion keeps the more active Ru where the cheaper Ni needs a second reactor and gives Ni the larger plants.

Changes of leader are common in the literature itself: the catalyst a methanol paper ranks first by space-time yield is not the plant-level optimum in a third of comparisons between catalysts, and the ammonia catalyst with the highest rate is not in 27%. In methanol, CO and methane accumulate in the loop and set purge and recycle, so that ranking by conversion or by selectivity fails as well; in ammonia, iron catalysts tested alongside more active ruthenium catalysts are cheaper to operate. The temperature at which a catalyst performs best in the laboratory is usually not where a plant would run it, and in most cases the plant would run it cooler. A laboratory comparison can report the plant-level consequence of its leader alongside its rate or yield, and screening can target the catalyst property with enough downstream leverage to change the decision.

For ammonia, the decision turns on what holding Ru costs. Bulk Ru would need a 94% lower price, or an activity gain that its scaling relation does not allow; metal economy does what activity cannot. Supported Ru/C with metal recovery undercuts fused iron whenever its Ru is financed below 6.5% per year at the two catalysts' own optima and 4.6% in a KAAP-type loop, and at the 2.1% per year that catalyst-metal leases carry, the least-cost converter puts fused iron in the first bed and Ru/C downstream, the arrangement of the Kellogg Advanced Ammonia Process. Independent process analyses describe the same trade-off between lower-pressure operation, refrigeration and the price, loading, lifetime and recycling of Ru.[15,62,63]

ACSA adds scale, not new science: 97% recall, 92–100% of printed values transcribed correctly and cost bounds that spare most full optimizations without losing a leader. Models of higher fidelity and catalyst physics specific to a formulation slot into the same chain without changing its decision logic, so that every comparison a catalysis paper reports can be taken to its plant-level consequence.

## Methods

### Multiscale decision analysis

Each catalytic system is evaluated with a process-level objective matched to the catalyst-to-process pathway under study. For ammonia, the economic objective contains the terms that respond directly to catalyst identity and catalytic performance: catalyst inventory and replacement, reactor requirements, compression, refrigeration and pressure-dependent equipment. Common upstream H₂/N₂ supply contributions are held fixed so that they do not dilute the catalyst-dependent decision signal. The methanol model evaluates net production cost through its recycle and separation process, while the Au/TiO₂ control maps particle-size-dependent activity to required catalyst mass under a common process model. The plant-level optimum of a set of catalysts is the catalyst with the lowest value of its system's objective under the stated process and economic conditions, each catalyst at the operating point that minimizes the objective: over the full process library for descriptor-based and measured ammonia catalysts, within the fitted pressure range for a measured rate law and over its measured operating points for a published methanol catalyst. The plant-cost leader of a published comparison is its plant-level optimum. Cross-system comparison is made at the level of ranking propagation and coupling topology, not by equating the absolute economic objectives of different reactions.

Within each system, rankings before and after process evaluation are compared using Spearman and Kendall correlations, pairwise inversions, top-rank identity and normalized decision regret. For a lower-is-better downstream objective J, regret is defined as R = (J_upstream-winner - J_downstream-winner) / J_downstream-winner. Analyses of the leading candidates are reported separately from full-set statistics so that changes at the top of the ranking are not obscured by agreement among weaker candidates. Backward design varies a selected catalyst property, reoptimizes the process and identifies the value required for economic parity. This value is then compared with the range accessible under the corresponding catalyst-property relation or scaling relation. Perturbation tests change one predefined input while preserving the other model assumptions and repeating the same downstream optimization.

### Ammonia synthesis

The ammonia calculation includes the 15-metal activity screen, scaling and kinetic relations, catalyst productivity and inventory, candidate-specific process optimization, pressure-dependent equipment capital and seven catalyst-dependent cost components. Gas-phase thermodynamics follow one measured basis throughout: the NH₃ formation free energy is taken at each temperature from the Gillespie–Beattie equilibrium constant,[64] in place of the DFT gas energies of the activity source, which place it 0.064–0.065 eV per NH₃ higher, and N₂, H₂ and NH₃ enter the loop equilibrium and the microkinetic gas activities as fugacities with Newton–Cooper coefficients,[46] so that the reactor and the loop share one equilibrium. The plant calibration of catalyst productivity is fixed by the fused-iron bed at 400 °C, 80 bar and a −35 °C separator. Temperature, pressure and separator temperature are reoptimized for each candidate over the full 14,510-state process grid. Descriptor uncertainty is propagated through 1,000 samples; Fe feasibility is evaluated against the 90 m³ bed-volume limit, and economic winner, preservation of the atomistic winner and top-three membership are recorded separately. The S1 source contains Fe terrace energetics but no Fe step-site row, so the Fe step descriptor is obtained from the 14-metal terrace-to-step scaling relation. Leave-one-out validation and prediction-interval propagation are reported in Supplementary Table 5; the regression residual standard error is 0.2271 eV, which is the Fe descriptor uncertainty used in the descriptor Monte Carlo analysis. The atomistic reference conditions (673 K, 24.5 bar N₂, 74.25 bar H₂ and 1 bar NH₃) and step-site descriptor framework follow the Wang–Abild-Pedersen Dataset S1 source.[65] The reconstruction recovers the high-activity metal set of the source study,[65] with Ru, Os and Fe as its leading three candidates. The Fe process optimum (425 °C, 105 bar) lies within the pressure range of industrial Fe synthesis loops, which have been built from about 80 bar upwards;[49] milder operating conditions are the stated direction of Haber–Bosch development.[66,67] Published Fe/Ru loop studies give independent process-level context for the catalyst-specific optima.[14] Perturbing the compressor, vessel, cost-index and capital-recovery inputs by 10–25% retains the Fe > Ru > Os economic order and the activity-only reachability verdict.

The plant benchmark compares the model with 64 values from industrial ammonia synthesis: loop states of 13 industrial loop records, forward runs and cost-optimal designs of the converter, compressor power, the separator vapour and the Fe optimum (Supplementary Table 4). Point values are compared as (model − industrial)/industrial, temperatures as differences in °C and published ranges by the distance to the nearer edge; an item passes when its deviation lies within the tolerance listed with it, and 33 of the 64 pass. The typical deviation of a quantity is the median absolute deviation of its items and the largest is their maximum. The failing items trace to five model components: separator vapour, approach to equilibrium, compression power, catalyst amount and the effective bed temperature. Each component was perturbed by the typical and by the largest deviation of its items, in both directions, alone and in all 32 sign combinations, with the catalyst-amount perturbation applied to fused iron alone or to every catalyst; Fe, Ru and Os were reoptimized over the perturbed process library within the 90 m³ limit after the canonical costs had been reproduced on the same code path. Converter items and the measured-kinetics comparisons of this perturbation use the measured rate laws of fused iron and Ru/C described below, each inside its fitted pressure range; the two staging diagnostics that need converter designs at several loop pressures keep the scores of the original benchmark campaign.

For the Ru-price test, only the Ru metal purchase price is changed. It is first set equal to the Fe value of 8 US dollars per kilogram, after which Ru is reoptimized over the full process grid. The Ru-price sweep repeats this optimization at 241 logarithmically spaced prices between 1 and 3 × 10^5 US dollars per kilogram together with the two reference prices. Fe–Ru parity is located by bisection in log price. The baseline Fe–Ru cost difference is decomposed into the same seven cost components.

The economic Monte Carlo analysis uses 5,000 samples with seed 20260920. Fe and Ru metal-price multipliers are sampled independently from a log-uniform distribution over 0.5–2.0. A common CAPEX multiplier is sampled uniformly over 0.8–1.2, electricity price uniformly over 20–100 US dollars per megawatt-hour, and catalyst lifetime uniformly over 5–20 years. Fe and Ru are reoptimized for every sample. The Ru activity multiplier required for parity, α*, is recalculated against the sample-specific reoptimized Fe cost. Normalized regret is computed draw-by-draw as (C_Ru − C_Fe)/C_Fe.

For backward design, Ru intrinsic activity is varied directly and the full process is reoptimized at each activity multiplier. Reachability is evaluated separately along the descriptor path defined by the ammonia scaling relation and over the full process grid. For the joint activity–lifecycle target analysis, catalyst lifetime and Ru recovery are represented through the effective replacement-metal price, P_eff = P_Ru(1 − r)(10 y/L). A conservative target region is constructed from the 42 distinct process states already visited by the independently reoptimized Ru price sweep: for each state, catalyst demand is rescaled by a direct activity multiplier and the volume- and pressure-dependent cost terms are recalculated. Restricting the optimization to this state subset can only overestimate the activity improvement required for parity, so the reported multipliers are upper bounds on the backward target.

Physical reachability of that target is then evaluated on the strict *E*_N scaling manifold using all 14,510 process states. At fixed process state, *E*_N affects cost through TOF and therefore the required active-metal inventory and bed volume; the metal-replacement, reactor-volume and pressure-vessel terms are monotone in that requirement, whereas state-specific compression and refrigeration costs are independent of *E*_N. The cost-minimizing descriptor for each state is therefore its TOF-maximizing value on the canonical 0.005-eV scaling grid. Taking the lower envelope across all states gives the lifecycle parity boundary as a function of \(q=(1-r)/L\). The canonical strict-scaling minimum is reproduced before the lifecycle calculation is accepted. The kinetic and scaling relations follow the established transition-metal ammonia-synthesis literature.[5,17,18,65]

Supported Ru/C is compared with fused iron on measured rate laws: the Model 3 rate law of Keestra et al. for commercial fused-iron catalysts, fitted to data from 1 to 324 bar, with the activity factor (0.350) of the Topsoe KM1R catalyst whose data Dyson and Simon fitted,[46–48] and the rate equation of a Ba–Cs–K-promoted Ru/C catalyst fitted at 50–100 bar,[41] each on the thermodynamic basis of the loop. Catalyst requirement is integrated along the bed in plug flow at every process state, and each law is calibrated on its own measured rate; the Ru/C law reproduces the 125 measured points of its source with a mean error of 10%, and the fused-iron law the outlet of a commercial magnetite catalyst at 100 bar within 16%. Each catalyst is optimized only over the process states inside the pressure range its law was fitted on, fused iron 1–324 bar and Ru/C 50–100 bar, so every comparison, including the KAAP-type loop and the staged converter, lies inside both fits. The supported bed holds 8 wt% Ru[43] at 490 kg m⁻³, the centre of the 430–550 kg m⁻³ derived from a promoted Ru/C laboratory bed,[68] with 95.55% Ru recovery; its Ru dispersion, 11% for the Ba–Cs–K-promoted catalyst[41] and up to about 50% for 2 nm Ru particles in Ba-promoted Ru/C,[69] enters through the measured rate per kilogram of catalyst. The KAAP-type comparison restricts both catalysts to the 90 bar, −20 °C separator states.

Metal cash flows keep the process optimization and price every metal purchase and recovered-metal credit at its date over the 25-year plant life (purchases at 0, 10 and 20 years; recovered Ru credited at replacement and at closure), discounted at a metal financing rate of 0–12% per year with Fe on the same convention; in the plant-life and catalyst-life configuration of the straight-line model and with no discounting it reproduces that accounting to 10⁻⁹ US dollars per tonne. The reference financing rate is the median of the 32 fixed annual rates that one refiner disclosed for its platinum and palladium catalyst-metal leases in its annual reports for 2015–2022 (1.35–5.10% per year, terms of 8 months to 3 years).[50] Under these contracts the refiner sold the catalyst metal to banks, leased it back for an annual fee on its value and repurchased it at the end of the term, so the fee is the annual cost of holding catalyst metal, the quantity the financing rate stands for in the cash flow; no Ru lease rate is published, and the rate is applied to Ru by analogy. In the supplier-lease variant the supplier keeps title, the operator pays the annual fee on the Ru value and settles the fine-metal shortfall at each return, and the operator's cash flows are discounted at 12% per year.

The staged converter has three adiabatic beds with interbed cooling, each filled with fused iron (up to 530 °C) or Ru/C (up to 460 °C, the top of its measured range), at 9 MPa with a −20 °C separator and 8% inerts; for every arrangement of the two catalysts, including those with Ru/C upstream of fused iron, bed temperatures and catalyst amounts are optimized, and the least-cost arrangement is read at each financing rate. The 16 robustness cases vary the Fe catalyst price (×0.5, ×2), the Ru/C bed density (430, 550 kg m⁻³), Ru recovery (90%), the Ru price (×0.7, ×1.3), catalyst life (5, 15 years), the effectiveness factor of either catalyst (0.5), the converter pressure premium (×0, ×0.5, ×2) and bed sizing at the inlet rate. Literature activity gains are taken only from comparisons with a reference Ru catalyst in the same study and are placed on the same multiplier axis without entering the cost model. The recycle-boundary check raises the recycle pressure rise from 3 bar to 6.0 and 9.2 bar, the rise between the cold recycle stream and the converter inlet in the 1,500 t/d Krupp–Uhde flowsheet,[70] and reoptimizes all 15 metals over the 14,510 process states. An independent five-component Peng–Robinson loop, with the NH₃ binary interaction parameters of Mace and Vrabec,[71] checks the cost model in the KAAP-type loop, where both rate laws are inside their fitted ranges (Supplementary Note 7).

### Bimetallic surface extension

N adsorption energies (0.5 N₂(g) + * → N*) for the 1,796 surfaces of Mamun et al.[38] were retrieved from Catalysis-Hub;[39] two independent retrievals give identical files of 5,696 adsorption sites. The most stable site of each surface defines its terrace descriptor. A linear fit over the 14 pure metals present in both this database and the source of the model's descriptors (slope 0.845, R² = 0.973, RMS 0.22 eV) maps it onto that source's terrace scale, and the 14-metal terrace-to-step relation already used for Fe (step E_N = 1.1547 × terrace E_N − 0.1701 eV) gives the step-site descriptor. The element-anchored bridge adds to this value each element's offset between its frozen step descriptor and its own bridged value, weighted by atomic fraction. Per-state activity is read from the cached response surface of the 14,510-state library; the cost uses the alloy's mole-weighted molar mass and mass-weighted metal price and is minimized over all states within the 90 m³ bed limit. The 15 model metals keep their frozen prices; 21 other elements take USGS annual-average prices per kilogram of contained metal (2025; Hg 2024),[72] with oxide, ore and ferroalloy quotations divided by the metal mass fraction. Tc has no market price and its 101 surfaces are not costed. Surfaces are labelled transition-metal, group 3–5 (Sc, Y, La, Ti, Zr, Hf, V, Nb, Ta) or sp-metal (Al, Zn, Cd, Hg, Ga, In, Tl, Sn, Pb, Bi) and scored per layer. The descriptor-only lower bound for each candidate combines its best-state activity, the smallest reactor term and the cheapest process state. For the surfaces below Fe and the highest-activity surface of each bridge, the counterfactual sets the metal price to the Fe price, and backward design solves for the activity multiplier and the metal price at which the reoptimized cost equals that of Fe; reachability is the lowest cost at any descriptor on the strict scaling range with the surface's molar mass and price.

### Measured ammonia catalysts

Tables 1–6 of the review[37] were extracted page by page (text and page image, two independent passes); disagreements and values absent from the page text were resolved on the page images. Rates are taken per gram of catalyst as printed, converted from mL h⁻¹ g⁻¹, or derived from the printed outlet NH₃ fraction and space velocity; rows printed twice are counted once. Rows were checked against the primary papers, and ten transcription errors in nine rows were replaced by the papers' values. Catalysts with one model metal and a stated metal content enter the chain: 83 of 161 rows. The model's rate per gram of metal is F_CAL·TOF(E_N)/M, with F_CAL the fused-iron plant calibration; each catalyst's measured rate per gram of metal fixes the effective descriptor E_eff at which the model reproduces it at the laboratory condition (H₂/N₂ = 3), on the side of the volcano where its metal lies, with a residual multiplier where the rate exceeds the volcano top. The laboratory NH₃ fraction (half the outlet value) enters at the same approach to equilibrium as in the experiment. The catalyst is then optimized over the 14,510 process states with descriptor E_eff, the molar mass and price of its metal, its own metal content and a bed density of 1,000 kg m⁻³ (fused iron: 71.51 wt% and 2,500 kg m⁻³), without metal recovery unless stated. The primary set contains catalysts measured at 300–500 °C under steady thermal operation with an outlet below 90% of equilibrium; chemical-looping, field- or microwave-assisted rows are reported separately. Bed densities of 500 and 2,500 kg m⁻³, catalysts measured at 5 MPa or above, and a constant multiplier on the metal's own TOF in place of E_eff were tested. The primary-paper set contains 30 papers on thermal ammonia synthesis that compare at least two catalysts or conditions with a stated metal content, 28 of them with Supporting Information; extraction uses the methanol passes with an ammonia schema, and mapping and process optimization follow the review chain. Comparison groups contain the entries of one paper at the same temperature, pressure, H₂/N₂ ratio and space velocity; entries with the same metal, metal content and rate (three significant figures) are counted once, and a catalyst is the fused-iron reference only when it is a commercial fused or wüstite iron catalyst without a stated support or added component. Only entries whose rate, temperature and outlet NH₃ fraction are printed values enter the primary comparison (182 of 625 entries; 139 of them in the 33 groups with at least two catalysts); all entries, plot readings included, give a sensitivity (40 of 124 groups). A second batch of 25 papers was screened, extracted with Supporting Information and scored under a protocol committed before extraction (22 included; Supplementary Table 2b); the pooled comparisons are the result (179 printed entries in 44 groups; all entries, 50 of 177 groups). The paper leaderboard is the rate per gram of catalyst, and the confidence interval is a paper-cluster bootstrap with 10,000 resamples.

### CO₂-to-methanol

The methanol analysis uses a recycle and separation model, calibrated on a published CO₂-to-methanol loop,[35] applied to four Re/TiO₂ states derived from the 1 and 5 wt% catalysts reported by Gothe et al.[19] The reference comparison uses 2% purge. Upstream rankings are reconstructed from space-time yield per gram of Re, single-pass methanol yield and space-time yield per gram of catalyst; the downstream ranking uses net production cost. Re purchase price and catalyst replacement are not included in the reference objective. Purge is varied from 0.5% to 40% over 396 levels, with an additional optimization in which purge is selected separately for each catalyst state. Local economic leverage for space-time yield, conversion and methane suppression is evaluated at the 5 wt% Re, 250 °C state. The process interpretation follows established CO₂-to-methanol assessments in which recycle, compression and separation materially affect plant performance.[20,21] The plant model was checked against published process assessments and pilot-plant data (Supplementary Table 6; Extended Data Fig. 4): at 22–33% per-pass conversion its hydrogen and CO₂ consumption (0.193–0.208 and 1.41–1.47 tonnes per tonne of methanol), carbon efficiency (0.93–0.98) and recycle ratio (2.6–5.4) lie within the reported ranges, and at the operating point and price assumptions of ref. 20 it reproduces its break-even methanol price, production cost plus capital recovery, to within 2.4%. The catalyst-independent stoichiometric H₂ and CO₂ demand is 583.2 euros per tonne on both sides; the catalyst-sensitive terms (feed above stoichiometry, electricity, catalyst replacement and capital) sum to 98.3 against 116.4 euros per tonne in the reference. Setting the loop pressure drop, recycle flow, compression, capital and prices to reference values leaves the space-time-yield disagreement at 24–26 of 81 comparisons without the inlet limit and 41–50 of 81 with it (Extended Data Fig. 4).

The measurement Monte Carlo samples the CO₂ conversion and selectivities of each catalyst state from that state's own uncertainty and re-solves the full methanol plant model in each of 5,000 draws (seed 20261005), with every cost parameter held at its reference value. The source reports single integer values, so the uncertainty is built from the source data: the reporting resolution (±0.5 percentage points, with '<1%' sampled over 0–1%), a 5.2% relative uncertainty in conversion derived from the consistency between space-time yield and conversion across the 21 reported runs, and a 0.85-percentage-point exchange between methanol and methane selectivity derived from the 11 runs of a single catalyst. Space-time yield follows conversion and methanol selectivity. Halving or doubling every width retains the economic winner in 99.3% or 78.4% of draws. The 1 wt% Re, 250 °C state uses the Table 3 selectivities of 97% methanol, 1% CO and 1% CH₄.

Counterfactuals remove one candidate difference at a time (methane selectivity moved to methanol; conversion set to the four-state mean) and re-solve the plant at the reference purge. Backward design changes one property of the space-time-yield winner until its cost equals the economic optimum.

### Published methanol comparisons

Every extracted record with CO₂ conversion, methanol selectivity, temperature, pressure and H₂/CO₂ ratio is a candidate operating point; the primary analysis uses entries whose conversion and selectivities are printed numbers, and entries with plot-read values are added as a sensitivity. Groups are formed by the same rule in both cohorts: of the 161 groups that include plot-read values, 33 come from 22 papers without printed values and 47 have fewer than two printed entries, which leaves the 81 groups of the primary analysis (Supplementary Table 7). An operating point printed twice in one paper is counted once. Selectivities are closed as in the hand-built case: methanol and methane as reported, entries below the reporting limit set to zero (setting the 14 such entries to their printed bound changes no comparison), and the CO-like residual equal to one minus the methanol and methane selectivities; when methane is not reported, it is one minus the methanol and CO selectivities if CO is reported and zero otherwise, and both bounds of an unreported CO–methane split are tested. Comparison groups contain the entries of one paper at the same pressure, H₂/CO₂ ratio and space velocity. Entries whose feed adds water or CO to CO₂ and H₂ are excluded, because the plant model takes a dry CO₂/H₂ make-up. Space-time yield per gram of catalyst defines the paper leaderboard: the printed value when every entry of a group prints it and the ratio of printed to conversion-derived productivity varies by less than a factor of 3 within the group, otherwise the value derived from the mass space velocity, otherwise from the volumetric space velocity with a bulk density of 1.0 g mL⁻¹ (0.5 and 2.0 g mL⁻¹ tested). The plant leaderboard is the net production cost at each entry's own temperature, pressure and H₂/CO₂ ratio, with catalyst mass from the group's productivity basis. Recycled CO is converted per pass to the smallest extent that keeps the reactor outlet at or below both reverse-water-gas-shift and CO-hydrogenation equilibrium, the single-pass CO₂ conversion is capped at the loop's CO₂-hydrogenation equilibrium, and the purge is optimized over 0.5–40% among the levels at which the reactor-inlet content of species other than H₂ and CO₂ (CO, methane and N₂) stays at or below 6.86%, the cost model's limit for the loop that anchors it (6.46% CO and N₂ in its stream table, 6.78% with methanol and water[35]). CO counts toward this limit because, at the cost-optimal purge, recycled CO is converted little further and dilutes the CO₂ and H₂ feed as N₂ does; it still reacts up to the equilibrium bound. The loop without the limit (purge extended to 10⁻⁴% as a check), limits of 4–30%, spanning the 4.4–9.8% of CO, methane and N₂ (4.9–10.2% of all species other than H₂ and CO₂) in four published loop designs, a limit on methane and N₂ alone, recycled CO treated as inert and recycled CO converted up to CO-hydrogenation equilibrium are reported as sensitivities (Extended Data Fig. 10; Supplementary Note 3). A disagreement is near-infeasible when the paper leader's cost in the limited loop exceeds twice its cost without the limit or the regret exceeds 100%. Variants use inert CO, 2% purge, single-pass yield, conversion or selectivity as the paper leaderboard, all entries including plot readings, entries whose reported product selectivities sum to at least 95%, and the comparisons without the paper that supplies most groups. In the catalyst-inventory variant each entry may load more catalyst, raising its conversion along an approach-to-equilibrium curve calibrated on its measured point to at most 95% of equilibrium at constant selectivity. The lower bound used to allocate the full plant optimization evaluates the same economics at each purge level on lower-bound loop flows that hold for any per-pass conversion of recycled CO between 0 and 1: reactor-inlet CO₂ of at least 1/[X(S_MeOH + (1 − p)S_CO)] per mole of methanol, three moles of H₂ per mole of methanol and four per mole of methane, the loop methane and nitrogen that the purge fraction p sets, and at least two moles of crude liquid; every equipment term rises with its flow, so the minimum over purge levels bounds the plant cost from below. Within each group the paper's leader is evaluated in full, then the remaining candidates in increasing order of the bound until the next bound exceeds the best cost found. Sampling uncertainty is a paper-cluster bootstrap with 10,000 resamples. Measurement uncertainty re-draws every entry 2,000 times with the Re/TiO₂ basis above (5.2% relative conversion error, a 0.85-percentage-point methanol–methane selectivity exchange and, for printed space-time yields, an independent 5.2% relative error), at one, two and four times these widths, and rebuilds both leaderboards from the same draw. Plant costs of perturbed entries come from an additive per-coordinate response surface built from exact model runs, with draws outside its grid run exactly; 7 of 600 group verdicts (4 of 200 at the reported noise level) in the inert-limited loop and 1 of 600 without the limit differ from exact runs of complete replicates. The catalyst-price variant prices each catalyst from its extracted composition, with metal contents at the model and 2025 commodity prices[72] plus a base cost that gives commercial Cu/ZnO/Al₂O₃ 18.1 euros per kilogram,[35] and charges replacement per tonne of inventory.

Catalyst-level comparison. What counts as a different catalyst was fixed before any comparison was counted. Two entries of a group are different catalysts when the authors report them as different samples that differ in at least one of composition (the elements and their ratios, promoters and dopants included), loading of the active component, support (identity or crystal phase) and synthesis (preparation route, precursor or precipitant, calcination or carburization temperature, morphology); entries of one sample that differ only in reaction temperature, time on stream, in-situ activation or reactor operating mode are one catalyst at different operating points. Each catalyst enters the laboratory ranking with its highest space-time yield and the plant ranking with its lowest plant cost over its operating points, and a comparison with at least two catalysts changes catalyst when the two leaders are different catalysts. In the primary count every entry may load more catalyst as in the catalyst-inventory variant above, so that each candidate is costed at the best condition its own measurement permits; the laboratory ranking keeps the measured space-time yield, and counts with every entry at its laboratory conversion are reported alongside. The cost gap is the lowest cost of the laboratory leader over that of the plant-cost leader, minus one. A stricter identity merges samples that differ only in synthesis; these sets were identified from sample names and papers without reference to either ranking. The point-level count, over every operating point of every catalyst, is reported as the total. For each catalyst measured at two or more temperatures, the temperature of its highest space-time yield is compared with that of its lowest plant cost. One paper ranks its catalysts per gram of Re;[19] on that basis its laboratory leader is also its plant-cost leader, which gives 20 of 63 changes of catalyst (9 above 5%; 25 of 63 and 18 above 5% at laboratory conversion) and leaves the point-level total unchanged.

### Au/TiO₂ control and catalyst life

The Au/TiO₂ control compares 2–6 nm particles under a common operating condition and process mapping. The reference rate, Au loading and dispersion are anchored to Janssens et al.,[24] and the particle-size dependence is taken from the 4.5 wt% Au/TiO₂ series reported by Overbury et al.[25] The broader physical basis for low-temperature Au/TiO₂ activity and particle-size effects follows the established literature.[22,23] Mass activity scales as d^−(n+m), with n the particle-size exponent and m the dispersion exponent; the required catalyst mass is its inverse. In the per-candidate Monte Carlo (10,000 draws, seed 20261019) each draw takes shared exponents n ~ U(0.7, 2.0) and m ~ U(0.8, 1.2) and gives every particle size an independent log-normal activity factor with σ = 0.25 in natural-log units, derived from the exponent uncertainty of the Overbury series; σ = 0, 0.10, 0.50 and 0.69 are reported alongside. The operating-freedom analysis optimizes temperature and O₂/CO ratio separately for each candidate within ranges derived from the literature, with candidate-specific kinetic variation (10,000 samples). Catalyst life enters as τ ∝ d^λ, with the annual catalyst cost m_i/τ_i; the adjacent-pair flip thresholds τ_j/τ_i = m_j/m_i, a (λ, n + m) map at σ = 0.25 (2,000 draws per cell, seed 20261020) and the combination with the operating-freedom samples (seed 20260909) were committed before they were computed.

### Adaptive Catalyst Screening Agent

ACSA fetches each paper through the institutional library service, converts it to page-marked text and page images, and extracts every catalyst entry into a fixed schema in which each number carries its value, unit, qualifier, location and page. Extraction uses one strict-JSON language-model call per paper and pass (gpt-5.5, medium reasoning effort): a main pass over text and page images, a figure pass that digitizes every catalyst-performance point in the main-text figures, and passes over the text, tables and figures of the Supporting Information. A printed main-text value outranks a printed Supporting-Information value, which outranks a plot reading. Units are normalized to the model basis. Prompts contain only the paper itself.

Accuracy is scored against two curated methanol datasets[33,34] and the 21 Re/TiO₂ entries of the hand-built case. Entries are matched by paper and catalyst name, or, for the dataset that lists compositions instead of names,[34] by paper, operating conditions and elemental composition, never by the values being scored; a pair with an equally admissible alternative partner counts for recall but not for value accuracy. Strict tolerances are ±1 K for temperature, ±1% for pressure and space-time yield, ±0.05 for the H₂/CO₂ ratio and ±0.5 percentage points for conversion and selectivity. Every disagreement between extracted and curated values was checked against the paper. Curated values that the paper contradicts fall into 26 root causes, one mechanism in one source paper each (45 rules, 169 cells and 8 rows across the two methanol datasets and the ammonia review), with a further 24 space-time-yield cells that one dataset derives from an erroneous input; that dataset computes space-time yield from its yield and space velocity, so a printed space-time yield that differs from the derivation is not counted as an error (Supplementary Table 8). Curated values are corrected to the printed value before scoring, and both raw and corrected scores are reported. Of the extracted entries without a curated partner, 150 of 179 in the first 18 papers and a random sample of 87 in the 31 papers added later were checked against the source. A random sample of 100 extracted values from 10 papers was checked by the authors against the papers and Supporting Information: 91 are printed or equal after unit conversion, 6 are H₂/CO₂ ratios derived from printed feed compositions, and 3 match after a corrected locator, a restored upper-bound qualifier or a stated metric name; none contradicts the source.

Before new candidates are scored, ACSA runs the hand-built inputs of all three systems, and the extracted Re/TiO₂ and Au/TiO₂ inputs, through the same code path and requires the frozen results to be reproduced; the batch runs do not start otherwise. Every extracted Au/TiO₂ input must also equal the hand-built value, because the dispersion enters the chain only as a ratio and cannot be checked through the outputs. A value that the source prints twice with different numbers is accepted in either form: the Au loading appears as 4.41 wt% in the text and 4.40 wt% in Table 1 of the source paper,[24] which changes the required Au mass by 0.2% and no ranking quantity. Candidates are then taken through the forward chain; the descriptor-only lower bound decides which ammonia candidates need the full process optimization, the purge-wise plant bound decides which methanol candidates need the equilibrium-constrained purge optimization, and all candidates were additionally evaluated in full to verify both bounds. The counterfactual and backward calculations are run on the leading candidates only.
