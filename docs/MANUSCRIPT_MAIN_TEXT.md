# Laboratory catalyst leaders are often not the industrial optimum

## Abstract

Catalyst screening ranks materials by descriptors or laboratory performance, whereas industrial selection depends on how catalyst properties propagate through reactor operation and plant economics. We couple catalyst-specific kinetics to candidate-specific process optimization and a catalyst-responsive cost objective, invert that objective into catalyst-property targets, and ask where the leading catalyst changes between the atomic, laboratory and industrial optimum. In ammonia synthesis the intrinsic order Ru > Os > Fe becomes Fe > Ru > Os because the annualized cost of the Ru held in the bed, not its activity, decides: Ru would need a 115-fold activity gain, beyond its scaling relation. The reversal holds for the industrial catalysts themselves: with measured rate laws, a commercial supported Ru catalyst with 95.55% metal recovery and its Ru inventory financed at the 12% rate applied to plant equipment, fused iron is the least-cost catalyst in a conventional and a KAAP-type loop, in an independent real-gas loop and in an optimized three-bed converter, and Ru enters the converter, downstream of fused iron, only when its metal is financed or leased below 6.7% per year. An agent that extracts published data and runs the full chain on every candidate measures this across the field: within the comparisons that published papers make themselves, the laboratory leader is not the plant-cost leader in 50 of 81 methanol comparisons (62%) and 12 of 44 ammonia comparisons (27%). Leaders change where a catalyst property alters a downstream operating requirement and are kept where it maps monotonically onto catalyst requirement.

## Introduction

Computational catalyst discovery ranks candidate materials by adsorption energies, reactivity descriptors, scaling relations, volcano plots and surrogate models,[1–6] and descriptor-based microkinetic and multiscale models connect those quantities to rates and operating conditions.[6–8] Experimental studies rank catalysts by laboratory activity, selectivity and space-time yield. Both rankings stand in for an industrial decision that depends on how catalyst properties propagate through reactor operation, separation, recycle and plant economics. Previous work has linked ammonia descriptors to reactor design and catalyst selection,[9] formulated catalyst and process design as coupled problems[10,11] and shown that laboratory rankings can depend on the test environment.[12] Whether the identity of the leading catalyst survives candidate-specific process optimization and economic evaluation has not been measured across a field.

The leading candidates matter most. A screen can remain well correlated across a large candidate set while changing the material that would actually be selected, and catalyst cost per kilogram can be a poor proxy for cost per unit product once stability and production scale are included.[13] In ammonia synthesis, process-level Fe/Ru studies show that the preferred catalyst and pressure depend on plant scale and loop configuration.[14,15]

Here we propagate catalyst properties forward through kinetics, candidate-specific process optimization and a catalyst-responsive cost objective, and backward from an economic target to the catalyst property it requires, testing whether that property lies within the range allowed by the underlying scaling relations.[10,11,16] A catalyst can be optimal from three viewpoints. The atomic optimum is set by a descriptor and intrinsic turnover; the laboratory optimum is the leader of a published comparison; the industrial optimum is the least-cost catalyst after process optimization and costing. Three systems examine the steps between them (Fig. 1a). Ammonia synthesis, in which intrinsic activity and metal price set catalyst inventory and operating conditions,[5,17,18] runs from the atomic to the industrial optimum. CO₂ hydrogenation to methanol, in which selectivity sets reactant loss, purge, recycle and compression,[19–21] runs from the laboratory to the industrial optimum. Au/TiO₂ CO oxidation, in which particle size maps monotonically to catalyst requirement,[22–25] runs from the laboratory optimum to the catalyst requirement and serves as the control. Language-model agents can now read the chemical literature and operate computational tools.[26–30] We use one, the Adaptive Catalyst Screening Agent (ACSA), to run the same chain on every candidate that publications report, which turns statements about a few hand-selected catalysts into a field-level measurement: within the comparisons that published methanol and ammonia papers make themselves, how often is the laboratory leader not the plant-cost leader?

## Results

### Process economics reverse the leading ammonia ranking

The 15-metal ammonia screen ranks Ru, Os and Fe first, second and third in intrinsic activity (Fig. 1c), consistent with the established activity landscape for transition-metal ammonia synthesis.[17,18] After the operating conditions are optimized separately for each catalyst over 14,510 process states and the catalyst-dependent costs are evaluated (Fig. 1a,d,e), the order becomes Fe, Ru and Os, at 11.52, 16.63 and 19.75 US dollars per tonne of NH₃ (Fig. 1b,h). The Spearman correlation across all 15 metals remains 0.911, whereas among the three leaders ρ = −0.50 (Fig. 1f,g): the screen stays correlated while the selected catalyst changes. Selecting Ru instead of Fe would raise the catalyst-dependent cost by 44.4%, the normalized decision regret. The difference of 5.12 US dollars per tonne is a small part of the cost of ammonia from state-of-the-art gas-based plants, about 160 US dollars per tonne,[31] but it is the part that catalyst selection controls, 1.77 million US dollars per year for a 1,000 t/d plant (Supplementary Note 1).

### Published laboratory leaders are often not the plant-cost leaders

A reversal among three hand-picked metals does not show how often the leader changes in practice. ACSA applies the same analysis to every candidate that publications or databases provide, with the kinetic, process and economic models unchanged (Fig. 2a). It reads each paper and its Supporting Information and records every catalyst entry with the table, figure and page each value comes from; before it scores any new candidate it must reproduce the frozen results of all three hand-built cases, and a cheap lower bound on cost decides which candidates need the full process optimization. Scored on 50 methanol papers (Supplementary Table 1) against two curated datasets,[32,33] the agent recovers 719 of the 741 curated entries (97%). It transcribes printed values almost without error: 44 of 44 CO₂ conversions and 44 of 44 methanol selectivities in main-text tables, and 129 of 130 conversions, 124 of 129 selectivities and 118 of 128 space-time yields in the Supporting Information; most remaining errors are plot readings (Extended Data Fig. 1; Supplementary Note 2). The self-check reproduces the 21 Re/TiO₂ entries to 2.3 × 10⁻¹³ euros per tonne and every pure-metal ammonia cost to 10⁻⁹ US dollars per tonne. In methanol, without the inlet limit, the lower bound spares the full plant optimization for 394 of 632 candidates (62.3%) and still finds the plant-cost leader of all 81 comparisons, at 38% of the compute (Extended Data Fig. 2).

Methanol papers rank catalysts by laboratory performance, most often by space-time yield. The methanol comparisons come from two extractions with the same pipeline: of the 50 papers above, 33 print the conversion, selectivity, temperature, pressure and H₂/CO₂ ratio of 351 operating points, and of 40 later papers, published from November 2025 to October 2026 and taken in the rank order of a fixed literature search, 28 print those of 348 (Supplementary Tables 1 and 1b; Methods). Within each paper, entries tested at the same pressure, H₂/CO₂ ratio and space velocity form the comparison the paper itself makes: 81 comparisons with 632 entries in 50 papers. Each entry was taken through the methanol recycle–economics model at its own conditions, with its single-pass conversion held at the laboratory value and capped at the loop's CO₂-hydrogenation equilibrium, and recycled CO converted per pass by the least amount that keeps the reactor outlet at or below reverse-water-gas-shift and CO-hydrogenation equilibrium. The loop must then keep the reactor feed as undiluted as a working loop does: CO, methane and N₂ together are limited to 6.86% of the reactor inlet, the cost model's limit for the loop that anchors it (6.46% CO and N₂ in that loop's stream table[34]), and the purge is optimized within that limit. CO counts toward the limit because recycled CO dilutes the CO₂ and H₂ feed as N₂ does; it still reacts up to the equilibrium bound. The space-time-yield leader is not the plant-cost leader in 50 of 81 comparisons (62%; 95% confidence interval 49–72% from resampling papers), 42 of them with a cost gap above 5%, spread over 32 of the 50 papers, and 1,992 of 5,283 pairwise orderings (38%) invert (Fig. 2b). The plant-cost leader is another catalyst in 26 of the 64 comparisons that contain at least two catalysts, 20 of them above 5%; in 17 it runs at the paper leader's temperature, 12 of these above 5% and from 11 papers. It is the same catalyst at another temperature in 23 of the 43 comparisons that contain a temperature series, 21 above 5%.

The disagreement is neither a sampling nor a measurement artefact (Extended Data Fig. 3). Re-measuring every entry 2,000 times with the conversion and selectivity uncertainty derived for the Re/TiO₂ states keeps 42 of the 50 disagreements in at least 90% of re-measurements, whereas measurement noise alone changes a paper's space-time-yield leader in 9 comparisons (95% range 5–14). In 26 of the 50 the paper leader meets the inlet limit only with a large purge and costs more than twice its cost without the limit; the other 24, 17 of them above 5%, involve no such jump (Supplementary Note 3). The disagreement holds without the study that supplies most comparisons (43 of 73, 37 above 5%), when plot-read entries are added (101 of 160 comparisons), for entries whose reported products close to methanol, CO and methane (30 of 50), with unreported CO and methane selectivities set to either bound (48–55 of 81), with recycled CO treated as inert (56 of 81) or converted per pass up to CO-hydrogenation equilibrium (50 of 81, 35 above 5%), when each catalyst is priced from its composition (47 of 81) and when each catalyst may load more catalyst and raise its conversion towards equilibrium (46 of 81, 36 above 5%, from 22 papers). It holds for inlet limits of 5–10% (42–50 of 81, 32–46 above 5%) and weakens towards 15% (41 and 34 of 81 at 12% and 15%, 26 and 24 above 5%; Supplementary Note 3). Without any inlet limit, which implicitly lets a diluted feed react as in the laboratory, the leaders differ in 25 of 81 comparisons (31%), 10 of them above 5%; limiting methane and N₂ alone and leaving CO unlimited gives 28 of 81, 11 above 5%, so the rise from 25 to 50 comes from CO build-up. The plant model reproduces the hydrogen and CO₂ consumption, carbon efficiency and recycle ratio of published plant assessments and, on the same basis, the break-even methanol price of one of them to within 2.4% (Extended Data Fig. 4).

The plant ranking follows from what each by-product does in a recycle loop (Fig. 2d,e). Recycled CO is converted per pass only as far as the shift and CO-hydrogenation equilibria require; at the cost-optimal purge it is barely converted further and accumulates, so that without the inlet limit the reactor feed carries a median 8.8% CO (90th percentile 26%), against 1.3–4.7% in published loop designs. Holding the feed to the limit costs purge, and the purge a catalyst needs grows with the CO and methane it makes. Under the limit the plant-cost leader is more selective to methanol than the paper leader in 40 of the 50 disagreements and makes less CO in 39, whereas without the limit it runs at higher single-pass conversion in 11 of 25. The selectivity rule also predicts: selected on 40 of the comparisons and committed before the plant model costed the other 41, it names the plant-cost leader in 28 of those 41, against 28% for a random choice and 19 for the papers' own space-time-yield leaders. For InNi₃C₀.₅ on different supports at 300 °C and 4 MPa (Supplementary Table 1), the space-time-yield leader on m-ZrO₂ (8.5% conversion, 88% methanol selectivity, 12% CO) needs a 4.0% purge and costs 1,289 euros per tonne, against 1,138 for the catalyst on SiO₂ (2.2% conversion, 95% selectivity, 5% CO) at 0.6% purge; without the limit the order is reversed, 919 against 1,101. No single laboratory metric replaces the plant model: ranking by conversion disagrees more often than ranking by space-time yield (54 of 81) and ranking by methanol selectivity in 30 of 81; without the limit selectivity is the worst proxy (61 of 81). A high-pressure Cu/Al₂O₃ study that supplies 8 of the 81 comparisons[35] gives the largest regrets without the limit.

Ammonia papers rank catalysts by rate. ACSA extracted 52 primary ammonia-synthesis papers with the same pipeline (Supplementary Tables 2 and 2b; Methods). Each catalyst's measured rate is inverted into an effective descriptor on its metal's side of the volcano, and the catalyst is optimized over the full process library with its own metal content. Within each paper, catalysts with printed rate, temperature and outlet that were tested at the same temperature, pressure, H₂/N₂ ratio and space velocity form 44 comparisons in 31 papers. The catalyst with the highest rate per gram of catalyst is not the plant-cost leader in 12 of them (27%; 95% confidence interval 14–42% from resampling papers), in 10 of 31 papers, 8 with a cost gap above 5% and a median regret of 13% where the leaders differ (Fig. 2c). In 3 comparisons a catalyst of another metal is the plant-cost leader, in all 3 an Fe catalyst on the same support with the lower rate (Fig. 2f), and in 5 a commercial fused-iron catalyst that the paper includes as a reference is the plant-cost leader; without these references the leaders differ in 8 of 44. Across the 73 catalysts tabulated in a review of ammonia-synthesis catalysts,[36] laboratory rate and plant cost are uncorrelated (Spearman ρ = 0.07), and only two of them, a Cs- and Ba-promoted Ru catalyst and a BaH₂-promoted Fe catalyst, undercut the fused-iron benchmark without metal recovery, by 4% and 3% (Extended Data Fig. 5; Supplementary Note 4).

### Selectivity, recycle and purge reshape the methanol ranking

The mechanism behind the methanol field result is visible in four Re/TiO₂ catalyst–temperature states (Fig. 3a,b).[19] By space-time yield per gram of Re the order is 1 wt% Re at 250 °C, 1 wt% Re at 200 °C, 5 wt% Re at 200 °C and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C. After the recycle and separation model at 2% purge, the economic order becomes 5 wt% Re at 200 °C, 1 wt% Re at 250 °C, 1 wt% Re at 200 °C and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C, with net production costs of 943, 962, 967 and 1,258 euros per tonne (Fig. 3c). Within the 6.86% inlet limit, at each state's optimal purge, the order becomes 1 wt% Re at 200 °C, 1 wt% Re at 250 °C, 5 wt% Re at 200 °C and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C (921, 982, 997 and 1,765 euros per tonne), and the space-time-yield winner stays second, now at 6.6% regret. At 2% purge, selecting the space-time-yield winner costs 1.93% more, and two of six pairwise orderings invert (Fig. 3e). Methane suppression has a local economic leverage of 0.376, against 0.0588 for single-pass conversion and 0.00289 for space-time yield (Fig. 3a,g), because methane consumes reactants and accumulates in the loop, raising purge, recycle and compression.[20,21] No purge level between 0.5% and 40% makes the space-time-yield winner the least-cost state (Fig. 3f).

Counterfactual and backward calculations locate the cause. Moving every state's methane selectivity into methanol reverses the correlation to ρ = −0.60, whereas equalizing conversion raises it to +1.00. For the space-time-yield winner, no increase in space-time yield reaches parity: unlimited productivity still costs 955 euros per tonne against 943. Raising its single-pass conversion from 0.23 to 0.279, inside the range measured on the same catalyst family, or reaching complete methanol selectivity does. When each state's own measurement uncertainty is propagated through the plant model, the economic winner keeps first place in 91.2% of 5,000 draws (Fig. 3d; Supplementary Note 5).

### Outside nitride formers and sp metals, surfaces that undercut Fe pair a cheap 3d metal with a group-6 metal

To test whether the Fe preference survives a much larger candidate space, ACSA took the 1,796 bimetallic and pure-metal surfaces of a high-throughput adsorption database[37,38] through the ammonia chain (Methods). Among the 372 surfaces that contain only transition metals outside groups 3–5, 137 satisfy the 90 m³ bed-volume limit. Eight undercut Fe when the database is bridged linearly onto the model's descriptor and nine under an element-anchored bridge; every one of them pairs Fe, Co, Ni or Cu with Cr, Mo or W (Extended Data Fig. 6), the combination that descriptor interpolation has identified for ammonia synthesis.[39] Surfaces with group 3–5 elements, which form stable bulk nitrides, and with sp metals, which lie outside the transition-metal scaling relations of the activity model, are reported as separate layers (Supplementary Table 3). The most active surface ranks 12th economically (Ni₃Re, 28.0% regret) and at its own price would need a 5.5-fold activity increase to reach Fe. The descriptor-only lower bound excludes 1,399 of the 1,695 priced surfaces (82.5%) from the full optimization without excluding any that undercut Fe (Supplementary Note 6).

### Metal price and process optimization decide the Fe–Ru ranking

The ammonia reversal is a metal-capital effect. Converting turnover frequency into the metal required for plant throughput preserves the intrinsic order; charging that metal at its price over a 10-year life gives Fe < Ru < Os, with annualized metal replacement of 0.204 US dollars per tonne for Fe against 4.09 for Ru, before any reactor, compression, refrigeration or equipment cost is added. At the Fe price of 8 US dollars per kilogram, reoptimized Ru is the cheaper catalyst (10.24 against 11.52 US dollars per tonne), and a price sweep with reoptimization at every price places Fe–Ru parity at 404 US dollars per kilogram (Fig. 4d). Process optimization does not close the gap but moves it into compression: Ru moves to about 450 °C and 220 bar, where a small bed holds little metal, while Fe operates near 425 °C and 105 bar (Fig. 4b,c). The 5.12 US dollars per tonne at the two optima comes mainly from fresh-feed compression (+3.988), metal inventory (+1.694) and compressor capital (+1.024), partly offset by vessel, recycle-compression and reactor terms (Fig. 4a,e). In 5,000 preregistered draws of metal prices, capital cost, electricity price and catalyst lifetime, each with full reoptimization, Fe is cheaper in every draw, with a minimum difference of 1.99 US dollars per tonne (Fig. 4h); descriptor uncertainty matters more, with Fe first in 65.3% of 1,000 descriptor samples and Ru in 34.7%, in most of the latter because the Fe bed exceeds the 90 m³ limit (Fig. 4f,g; Supplementary Note 7).

Every route that brings Ru to the Fe cost acts on the same quantity, the annualized cost of the Ru held in the bed (Table 1; Supplementary Note 7). The benchmark charges Ru as the bed of the industrial fused-iron catalyst. A promoted Ru/C catalyst exposes 11% of its Ru atoms,[40] against fewer than 1% of the Fe atoms in reduced fused iron;[41] the commercial catalyst carries about 8 wt% Ru,[42] and 90–95.55% of the Ru in spent promoted Ru/C ammonia catalysts is recovered.[43,44] On its own bed with recovery, supported Ru costs 11.46–12.05 US dollars per tonne, from 0.05 below to 0.53 above Fe, and in a KAAP-type loop at about 9 MPa with a −20 °C refrigerated condenser,[45] 16.35–16.44 against 16.98 for fused iron. These recovered-Ru costs are scenarios, since the baseline ranking assumes no recovery, and they charge the Ru inventory, as the benchmark model does, only for the metal lost at each replacement. Financing the Ru inventory over the 25-year plant life at the 12% rate applied to equipment brings Ru to parity with Fe in the KAAP-type loop (16.99 against 17.00 US dollars per tonne), and Ru stays cheaper at any metal financing rate below 12.3% per year. Sampling dispersion, recovery and bed density turns the decision into a probability: Ru is cheaper in 79.3% of draws for a commercial Ru/C bed (5–10 wt% Ru, 430–550 kg m⁻³), whereas Fe stays cheaper in 93.0% with the activities and Ru contents of the 54 measured Ru catalysts (Extended Data Fig. 7).

The descriptor kinetics are furthest from measurement for the two catalysts at this frontier: at 90 bar, 400 °C and 2% ammonia they give Ru apparent orders of +2.4 in H₂ and −1.9 in NH₃, against +0.3 and −0.4 for promoted Ru/C, and fused iron −0.1 in NH₃ against −1.0 measured. Replacing the rate laws of this pair alone with measured ones, the Temkin–Pyzhev form fitted to an industrial magnetite catalyst[46,47] and the kinetics of a promoted Ru/C catalyst,[40] and integrating each along the bed, keeps fused iron ahead in the benchmark formulation: Fe costs 14.93 US dollars per tonne at 450 °C and 140 bar, whereas Ru costs 38.26 at best in the 100–200 bar range and 33.07 at its unconstrained optimum around 735 bar, and would need a 14-fold activity gain for parity (14–25-fold across bed-sizing and calibration variants). For supported Ru/C the measured kinetics place the decision on the financing of the Ru inventory: at its optimum of 105 bar it costs 3.46 US dollars per tonne less than Fe when the Ru inventory carries no financing charge and 1.45 more at 12% per year (in the KAAP-type loop 1.41 less and 1.73 more), and it stays cheaper than Fe up to a financing rate of 7.2% per year, and in the KAAP-type loop up to 5.4% per year, above the disclosed lease rates. With both catalysts available to a three-bed converter at 9 MPa, the least-cost design puts fused iron in the first bed and Ru/C downstream for Ru financing rates of 0.8–6.7% per year, the bed order of the Kellogg Advanced Ammonia Process converter[45] and of published Fe + Ru multibed designs;[48] all-Ru/C wins below that range, all-Fe above it, and a converter with Ru/C first is never optimal. Fused iron takes the first, hottest bed because Ru/C is held to 460 °C, the top of its measured range; Ru/C takes the downstream beds because ammonia inhibits fused iron more strongly, so that the Ru/C rate advantage per kilogram grows from 16-fold at 2% to 52-fold at 14% NH₃. The 1.35–5.10% per year that one refiner disclosed for 32 platinum-group catalyst-metal leases falls inside this window. An independent five-component loop with inert gases, purge, two-stage condensation and real-gas phase equilibria reproduces the financing trade-off, with a break-even of 7.0% per year against 7.2% here (Methods).

The ammonia reversal is therefore an industrial result, not only a property of the pure-metal benchmark bed. A plant that owns its catalyst metal carries the Ru inventory as capital, at the 12% per year it applies to its equipment. On that basis, with the measured rate laws, the commercial Ru/C formulation and 95.55% recovery, fused iron is cheaper than Ru/C by 1.45 US dollars per tonne in the benchmark loop and by 1.73 in the KAAP-type loop, and stays cheaper in 14 and 15 of 16 cases that vary catalyst and metal prices, bed density, recovery, catalyst life, the effectiveness factor of either catalyst, the converter pressure premium and the bed sizing. The independent five-component real-gas loop gives the same order, fused iron 2.24 US dollars per tonne below Ru/C at 425 °C and 220 bar, and the optimized three-bed converter is all fused iron. This is the catalyst choice of nearly all ammonia plants.[45] Ru displaces fused iron only through the metal contract, not through its activity: when the metal is financed or leased below 6.7% per year Ru/C takes the downstream beds, and below 0.8% the whole converter (Supplementary Note 7).

### Backward design separates the required catalyst-property region from physical reachability

Multiplying Ru intrinsic activity by a factor α and reoptimizing the process at each value, Ru reaches the Fe cost only after a 115-fold increase; the 5th–95th percentile requirement under economic uncertainty is 44–262-fold (Fig. 5a). The scaling relation allows much less: at most 1.02-fold at 673 K and 1.58-fold across all process conditions, and the lowest strict-scaling Ru cost, 16.45 US dollars per tonne at *E*_N = −1.180 eV, remains above the Fe cost (Fig. 5b). Improving Ru activity along the present scaling relation cannot make Ru competitive.

Measured catalysts show which modifications supply gains of that size (Fig. 5a, bottom). Ba promotion of Ru/C raises the turnover frequency per surface Ru 75-fold and Cs promotion 65-fold,[49] Ba promotion of Ru/BN more than 100-fold at 5 MPa[50] and Cs promotion of Ru/MgO more than 134-fold;[51] all four lie within the parity band, and the MgO-supported Cs gain exceeds the central requirement, whereas electride and oxyhydride supports give about tenfold[52,53] and confinement inside carbon nanotubes 0.5-fold.[54] Electronic promotion, which changes the energetics beyond a single scaling relation, is the measured route that reaches the parity band.

Lifecycle variables move the target before reachability is tested. Allowing Ru recovery and catalyst lifetime to improve together removes the activity requirement: at 99% recovery, a 10-year lifetime needs at most a 1.29-fold gain and a 20-year lifetime none (Fig. 5c). On the strict *E*_N scaling manifold across all 14,510 process states, parity is controlled by the catalyst-loss factor \(q=(1-r)/L\), with \(q^*=8.25\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{y}^{-1}\). At a 20-year lifetime and 99% recovery the best strict-scaling state costs 11.20 US dollars per tonne, 0.32 below Fe, and parity requires 98.35% recovery at 20 years or a 12.12-year lifetime at 99% recovery (Fig. 5d). Lifecycle engineering thus reaches parity inside the present scaling-constrained design space, where activity alone cannot (Supplementary Note 8).

### A monotonic catalyst-to-process mapping preserves the ranking

Process evaluation does not change every ranking. In Au/TiO₂ CO oxidation, with the rate anchor of Janssens et al.[24] and the particle-size dependence of Overbury et al.,[25] candidates differ only in particle size from 2 to 6 nm under one operating condition and process model. Intrinsic activity and required catalyst mass preserve the order 2 > 3 > 4 > 5 > 6 nm in all 10,000 parameter samples drawn from the literature ranges (Fig. 6b–d), and in 92.2% when candidate-specific kinetic and operating variations are introduced over 273–293 K (Fig. 6e).

These cases reveal three distinct observed outcomes of cross-scale propagation: an upstream advantage is preserved in the Au/TiO₂ control, whereas the selected methanol and ammonia comparisons show cost-ranking inversions (Fig. 6a,f). The contrasts are economically explicit. The 2 nm Au particles have approximately 8.1 times the mass activity of 6 nm particles and require approximately 8.1 times less catalyst under the fixed-condition control mapping. In methanol, a 3.6-fold space-time-yield advantage corresponds to 1.9% *higher* modeled production cost; in ammonia, Ru has approximately 607 times the intrinsic turnover frequency of Fe but 44% *higher* catalyst-dependent cost under the stated process and economic assumptions. For compact comparison we report the **realized pairwise transfer index** χ_AB = ln(J_A/J_B)/ln(s_A/s_B), where s_A > s_B is the higher-is-better upstream property and J is the system-specific lower-is-better downstream objective. Its sign records the direction of the *already evaluated* downstream outcome (Au χ ≈ −1.00, methanol +0.015, ammonia +0.057); χ = −1 occurs in the Au control because catalyst requirement is constructed to scale inversely with activity. Neither χ nor its magnitude predicts the unknown plant cost from activity alone, and χ values across different upstream metrics and downstream objectives are not measures of universal mechanism strength. Instead, the mechanism is identified by the separate process and cost analyses: metal inventory, prices and optimized operation in ammonia, and selectivity-dependent reactant loss, purge and recycle in methanol (Fig. 6a; Supplementary Note 9).

## Discussion

A catalyst ranking carries over from one optimum to the next only to the extent that the upstream advantage reaches the plant objective. Rank agreement across a screen therefore does not guarantee the same industrial choice: in ammonia the full 15-metal ranking remains strongly correlated while the leading order Ru > Os > Fe becomes Fe > Ru > Os. The penalty differs at each step between the optima. From the atomic to the industrial optimum in ammonia it is the capital held in the catalyst bed, which process reoptimization converts into compression as Ru moves to high pressure and a small bed; from the laboratory to the industrial optimum in methanol it is the reactant lost to purge; from the laboratory optimum to the catalyst requirement in Au/TiO₂ there is none, and the ranking is kept.

The field measurement shows that these reversals are not hand-picked exceptions. Within the comparisons that published papers make themselves, the laboratory leader is not the plant-cost leader in 62% of methanol comparisons, 42 of 81 with a cost gap above 5% (31%, 10 of 81, when the loop places no limit on the species that build up at the reactor inlet), and in 27% of ammonia comparisons. The causes are those of the hand-built cases: in methanol, CO and methane accumulate in the loop and set purge and recycle, and ranking by conversion or by selectivity fails as well; in ammonia, iron catalysts tested alongside more active ruthenium catalysts are cheaper to operate. Screening should therefore target the catalyst property that has sufficient downstream leverage to alter the industrial decision, and a laboratory comparison can report the process-level consequence of its leader alongside its rate or yield.

Backward design turns the ammonia mechanism into materials and lifecycle targets. Ru would need a 115-fold activity gain, far beyond the 1.6-fold reachable along its scaling relation, whereas recovery with catalyst lifetime reaches parity on the scaling relation itself. The reversal is also the industrial result: with measured kinetics, the commercial Ru/C formulation and recovery, and the Ru inventory financed at the rate the plant applies to its equipment, fused iron is the least-cost catalyst in every loop tested. Ru becomes competitive through the metal contract rather than through activity: supported, recovered Ru undercuts Fe in the KAAP-type loop as long as its inventory is financed below 5–12% per year, depending on the kinetics, and the same economics select the staged converter of the Kellogg Advanced Ammonia Process at metal-financing rates that contain the disclosed lease rates for platinum-group catalyst metal. Independent process analyses describe the same trade-off between lower-pressure operation, ammonia-separation refrigeration and the price, loading, lifetime and recycling of Ru.[15,55,56]

ACSA adds no new scientific model; its contribution is scale, with 97% recall, 92–100% of printed values transcribed correctly and cost bounds that spare most full optimizations without losing a leader. The framework is modular: formulation-specific catalyst physics and higher-fidelity kinetic, process and economic models can replace the present ones without changing the decision logic, so every comparison a catalysis paper reports can be taken to its plant-level consequence.

## Methods

### Multiscale decision analysis

Each catalytic system is evaluated with a process-level objective matched to the catalyst-to-process pathway under study. For ammonia, the economic objective contains the terms that respond directly to catalyst identity and catalytic performance: catalyst inventory and replacement, reactor requirements, compression, refrigeration and pressure-dependent equipment. Common upstream H₂/N₂ supply contributions are held fixed so that they do not dilute the catalyst-dependent decision signal. The methanol model evaluates net production cost through its recycle and separation process, while the Au/TiO₂ control maps particle-size-dependent activity to required catalyst mass under a common process model. Cross-system comparison is made at the level of ranking propagation and coupling topology rather than by equating the absolute economic objectives of different reactions.

Within each system, rankings before and after process evaluation are compared using Spearman and Kendall correlations, pairwise inversions, top-rank identity and normalized decision regret. For a lower-is-better downstream objective J, regret is defined as R = (J_upstream-winner - J_downstream-winner) / J_downstream-winner. Analyses of the leading candidates are reported separately from full-set statistics so that changes at the top of the ranking are not obscured by agreement among weaker candidates. Backward design varies a selected catalyst property, reoptimizes the process and identifies the value required for economic parity. This value is then compared with the range accessible under the corresponding catalyst-property relation or scaling relation. Perturbation tests change one predefined input while preserving the other model assumptions and repeating the same downstream optimization.

### Ammonia synthesis

The ammonia calculation includes the 15-metal activity screen, scaling and kinetic relations, catalyst productivity and inventory, candidate-specific process optimization, pressure-dependent equipment capital and seven catalyst-dependent cost components. Gas-phase thermodynamics follow one measured basis throughout: the NH₃ formation free energy is taken at each temperature from the Gillespie–Beattie equilibrium constant,[57] in place of the DFT gas energies of the activity source, which place it 0.064–0.065 eV per NH₃ higher, and N₂, H₂ and NH₃ enter the loop equilibrium and the microkinetic gas activities as fugacities with Newton–Cooper coefficients,[46] so that the reactor and the loop share one equilibrium. The plant calibration of catalyst productivity is fixed by the fused-iron bed at 400 °C, 80 bar and a −35 °C separator. Temperature, pressure and separator temperature are reoptimized for each candidate over the full 14,510-state process grid. Descriptor uncertainty is propagated through 1,000 samples; Fe feasibility is evaluated against the 90 m³ bed-volume limit, and economic winner, preservation of the atomistic winner and top-three membership are recorded separately. The S1 source contains Fe terrace energetics but no Fe step-site row, so the Fe step descriptor is obtained from the 14-metal terrace-to-step scaling relation. Leave-one-out validation and prediction-interval propagation are reported in Supplementary Table 4; the regression residual standard error is 0.2271 eV, which is the Fe descriptor uncertainty used in the descriptor Monte Carlo analysis. The atomistic reference conditions (673 K, 24.5 bar N₂, 74.25 bar H₂ and 1 bar NH₃) and step-site descriptor framework follow the Wang–Abild-Pedersen Dataset S1 source.[58] Under those conditions, the source study places Fe and Ru close to the activity plateau and undercoordinated Os close to the volcano maximum.[58] The descriptor-level reconstruction recovers the same high-activity metal set, with Ru, Os and Fe as its leading three candidates, providing the ranking fidelity required for the downstream decision analysis. The Fe process optimum (425 °C, 105 bar) lies within the pressure range of industrial Fe synthesis loops, which have been built from about 80 bar upwards;[45] milder operating conditions are the stated direction of Haber–Bosch development.[59,60] The resulting catalyst-specific optima quantify how intrinsic energetics propagate into operating severity and economic consequence within a common, controlled process model. Published Fe/Ru loop studies provide independent process-level context for these trends despite differences in catalyst formulation, plant scale and flowsheet design.[14] Pressure-CAPEX and equipment-cost diagnostics perturb the compressor, vessel, cost-index and capital-recovery assumptions by approximately 10–25%; the Fe > Ru > Os economic order and the activity-only reachability verdict are retained, demonstrating that the central decision is robust to these model-form perturbations.

For the Ru-price test, only the Ru metal purchase price is changed. It is first set equal to the Fe value of 8 US dollars per kilogram, after which Ru is reoptimized over the full process grid. The Ru-price sweep repeats this optimization at 241 logarithmically spaced prices between 1 and 3 × 10^5 US dollars per kilogram together with the two reference prices. Fe–Ru parity is located by bisection in log price. The baseline Fe–Ru cost difference is decomposed into the same seven cost components.

The preregistered economic Monte Carlo analysis uses 5,000 samples with seed 20260920. Fe and Ru metal-price multipliers are sampled independently from a log-uniform distribution over 0.5–2.0. A common CAPEX multiplier is sampled uniformly over 0.8–1.2, electricity price uniformly over 20–100 US dollars per megawatt-hour, and catalyst lifetime uniformly over 5–20 years. Fe and Ru are reoptimized for every sample. The Ru activity multiplier required for parity, α*, is recalculated against the sample-specific reoptimized Fe cost. The draw sequence is generated from the seed and priors, and normalized regret is computed draw-by-draw as (C_Ru − C_Fe)/C_Fe. The supporting descriptor-correlation sensitivity does not modify this preregistered Monte Carlo. Instead, it preserves the frozen Fe normal marginal and the ±0.15 eV Ru/Os uniform marginals using a Gaussian copula with latent pairwise correlations from 0 to 0.9, and evaluates atomistic ordering on the 673 K one-descriptor volcano. A separate common-bias sweep applies the same additive shift to Ru, Os and Fe descriptors. Neither sensitivity is used to report a new downstream economic probability. The actual-catalyst Monte Carlo adds inputs drawn from a second generator (seed 20261006), so that every preregistered draw keeps its values: the Ru dispersion ratio u = D_Ru/f_Fe, log-uniform over 11–50, with D_Ru from 11% for a Ba–Cs–K-promoted Ru/C catalyst[40] to about 50% for 2 nm Ru particles in Ba-promoted Ru/C[61] and f_Fe = 1% for the exposed Fe atoms of reduced fused iron;[41] Ru recovery uniform over 90–95.55%, the range measured for spent promoted Ru/C ammonia catalysts;[43,44] Ru content of the supported bed uniform over 5–10 wt%,[42] from a third generator (seed 20261007); and bed density uniform over 430–550 kg m⁻³, the range derived from a promoted Ru/C laboratory bed[62]. In the benchmark-bed variant the Ru price is lowered to p(1 − r)/u, as in Fig. 4d; in the supported-bed variant the Ru activity per kilogram of Ru is multiplied by u, the Ru charge is replaced at (1 − r), and the bed volume follows from the Ru content and bed density. The measured-activity variant replaces u by the effective descriptor and residual multiplier of a randomly drawn measured Ru catalyst together with its Ru content.

For backward design, Ru intrinsic activity is varied directly and the full process is reoptimized at each activity multiplier. Reachability is evaluated separately along the descriptor path defined by the ammonia scaling relation and over the full process grid. For the joint activity–lifecycle target analysis, catalyst lifetime and Ru recovery are represented through the effective replacement-metal price, P_eff = P_Ru(1 − r)(10 y/L). A conservative target region is constructed from the 42 distinct process states already visited by the independently reoptimized Ru price sweep: for each state, catalyst demand is rescaled by a direct activity multiplier and the volume- and pressure-dependent cost terms are recalculated. Restricting the optimization to this state subset can only overestimate the activity improvement required for parity, so the reported multipliers are upper bounds on the backward target.

Physical reachability of that target is then evaluated on the strict *E*_N scaling manifold using all 14,510 process states. At fixed process state, *E*_N affects cost through TOF and therefore the required active-metal inventory and bed volume; the metal-replacement, reactor-volume and pressure-vessel terms are monotone in that requirement, whereas state-specific compression and refrigeration costs are independent of *E*_N. The cost-minimizing descriptor for each state is therefore its TOF-maximizing value on the canonical 0.005-eV scaling grid. Taking the lower envelope across all states gives the lifecycle parity boundary as a function of \(q=(1-r)/L\). The canonical strict-scaling minimum is reproduced before the lifecycle calculation is accepted. The kinetic and scaling relations follow the established transition-metal ammonia-synthesis literature.[5,17,18,58]

The actual-catalyst analysis reads the fully reoptimized Ru price sweep at the effective price p(1 − r)/u, where u is the ratio of exposed metal fractions of the supported catalyst and fused iron (u = 0.11/0.01 = 11, a lower bound) and r the recovered fraction (90–95.55%); the activity multiple for parity is recomputed with full reoptimization at every price. The KAAP comparison restricts both catalysts to the 90 bar, −20 °C separator states. A supported bed holding 8 wt% Ru (5–10 wt%) instead of the common formulation changes only the reactor term; recomputing it for bed densities of 430–550 kg m⁻³ adds 0.05–0.07 US dollars per tonne without recovery and 0.30–0.44 with 90–95.55% recovery (0.03–0.10 in the KAAP loop) at 8 wt%. Metal recovery is an optional scenario input, not a condition imposed on the baseline metal comparison. A lifecycle cash-flow variant keeps the process optimization and prices every metal purchase and recovered-metal credit at its date over the 25-year plant life (purchases at 0, 10 and 20 years; recovered Ru credited at replacement and at closure), discounted at a metal-specific rate of 0–12% per year, and above 12% to locate the break-even rate, with Fe on the same convention; in the plant-life and catalyst-life configuration of the straight-line model and with no discounting it reproduces that accounting to 10⁻⁹ US dollars per tonne. For the two frontier catalysts the descriptor rates are replaced by measured rate laws: the Temkin–Pyzhev form with the parameters of Dyson and Simon for an industrial magnetite catalyst[46,47] and the rate equation of a Ba–Cs–K-promoted Ru/C catalyst,[40] each on the thermodynamic basis of the loop. Catalyst requirement is integrated along the bed in plug flow at every process state, and each law is calibrated on its own measured rate; the Ru/C law reproduces the 125 measured points of its source with a mean error of 10%. The fused-iron law was fitted at 149–309 atm and the Ru/C law at 50–100 bar, so the Fe optimum at 140 bar and the 9 MPa loop extrapolate the former, and every Ru optimum above 100 bar the latter. The staged converter has three adiabatic beds with interbed cooling, each filled with fused iron (up to 530 °C) or Ru/C (up to 460 °C, the top of its measured range), at 9 MPa with a −20 °C separator and 8% inerts, and is optimized over bed temperatures and catalyst amounts at every financing rate. Literature activity gains are taken only from comparisons with a reference Ru catalyst in the same study and are placed on the same multiplier axis without entering the cost model. The recycle-boundary check raises the recycle pressure rise from 3 bar to 6.0 and 9.2 bar, the rise between the cold recycle stream and the converter inlet in the 1,500 t/d Krupp–Uhde flowsheet,[63] and reoptimizes all 15 metals over the 14,510 process states. The independent loop check solves a five-component (N₂, H₂, CH₄, Ar, NH₃) Peng–Robinson loop with the temperature-dependent NH₃ binary interaction parameters of Mace and Vrabec,[64] two cold flashes, purge, make-up and a converged recycle; it sizes the bed with the same two measured rate laws at a common 1,500 t/d of net NH₃ and charges excess feed, utilities, catalyst inventory and annualized reactor and compressor capital (Ru recovery 95.55%, H₂ 2 and N₂ 0.10 US dollars per kilogram).

### Bimetallic surface extension

N adsorption energies (0.5 N₂(g) + * → N*) for the 1,796 surfaces of Mamun et al.[37] were retrieved from Catalysis-Hub;[38] two independent retrievals give identical files of 5,696 adsorption sites. The most stable site of each surface defines its terrace descriptor. A linear fit over the 14 pure metals present in both this database and the source of the model's descriptors (slope 0.845, R² = 0.973, RMS 0.22 eV) maps it onto that source's terrace scale, and the 14-metal terrace-to-step relation already used for Fe (step E_N = 1.1547 × terrace E_N − 0.1701 eV) gives the step-site descriptor. The element-anchored bridge adds to this value each element's offset between its frozen step descriptor and its own bridged value, weighted by atomic fraction. Per-state activity is read from the cached response surface of the 14,510-state library; the cost uses the alloy's mole-weighted molar mass and mass-weighted metal price and is minimized over all states within the 90 m³ bed limit. The 15 model metals keep their frozen prices; 21 other elements take USGS annual-average prices per kilogram of contained metal (2025; Hg 2024),[65] with oxide, ore and ferroalloy quotations divided by the metal mass fraction. Tc has no market price and its 101 surfaces are not costed. Surfaces are labelled transition-metal, group 3–5 (Sc, Y, La, Ti, Zr, Hf, V, Nb, Ta) or sp-metal (Al, Zn, Cd, Hg, Ga, In, Tl, Sn, Pb, Bi) and scored per layer. The descriptor-only lower bound for each candidate combines its best-state activity, the smallest reactor term and the cheapest process state. For the surfaces below Fe and the highest-activity surface of each bridge, the counterfactual sets the metal price to the Fe price, and backward design solves for the activity multiplier and the metal price at which the reoptimized cost equals that of Fe; reachability is the lowest cost at any descriptor on the strict scaling range with the surface's molar mass and price.

### Measured ammonia catalysts

Tables 1–6 of the review[36] were extracted page by page (text and page image, two independent passes); disagreements and values absent from the page text were resolved on the page images. Rates are taken per gram of catalyst as printed, converted from mL h⁻¹ g⁻¹, or derived from the printed outlet NH₃ fraction and space velocity; rows printed twice are counted once. Rows were checked against the primary papers, and ten transcription errors in nine rows were replaced by the papers' values. Catalysts with one model metal and a stated metal content enter the chain: 83 of 161 rows. The model's rate per gram of metal is F_CAL·TOF(E_N)/M, with F_CAL the fused-iron plant calibration; each catalyst's measured rate per gram of metal fixes the effective descriptor E_eff at which the model reproduces it at the laboratory condition (H₂/N₂ = 3), on the side of the volcano where its metal lies, with a residual multiplier where the rate exceeds the volcano top. The laboratory NH₃ fraction (half the outlet value) enters at the same approach to equilibrium as in the experiment. The catalyst is then optimized over the 14,510 process states with descriptor E_eff, the molar mass and price of its metal, its own metal content and a bed density of 1,000 kg m⁻³ (fused iron: 71.51 wt% and 2,500 kg m⁻³), without metal recovery unless stated. The primary set contains catalysts measured at 300–500 °C under steady thermal operation with an outlet below 90% of equilibrium; chemical-looping, field- or microwave-assisted rows are reported separately. Bed densities of 500 and 2,500 kg m⁻³, catalysts measured at 5 MPa or above, and a constant multiplier on the metal's own TOF in place of E_eff were tested. The primary-paper set contains 30 papers on thermal ammonia synthesis that compare at least two catalysts or conditions with a stated metal content, 28 of them with Supporting Information; extraction uses the methanol passes with an ammonia schema, and mapping and process optimization follow the review chain. Comparison groups contain the entries of one paper at the same temperature, pressure, H₂/N₂ ratio and space velocity; entries with the same metal, metal content and rate (three significant figures) are counted once, and a catalyst is the fused-iron reference only when it is a commercial fused or wüstite iron catalyst without a stated support or added component. Only entries whose rate, temperature and outlet NH₃ fraction are printed values enter the primary comparison (182 of 625 entries; 139 of them in the 33 groups with at least two catalysts); all entries, plot readings included, give a sensitivity (40 of 124 groups). A second batch of 25 papers was screened, extracted with Supporting Information and scored under a protocol committed before extraction (22 included; Supplementary Table 2b); the pooled comparisons are the primary result (179 printed entries in 44 groups; all entries, 50 of 177 groups), and each batch is also reported alone. The paper leaderboard is the rate per gram of catalyst, and the confidence interval is a paper-cluster bootstrap with 10,000 resamples.

### CO₂-to-methanol

The methanol analysis uses a recycle and separation model, calibrated on a published CO₂-to-methanol loop,[34] applied to four Re/TiO₂ states derived from the 1 and 5 wt% catalysts reported by Gothe et al.[19] The reference comparison uses 2% purge. Upstream rankings are reconstructed from space-time yield per gram of Re, single-pass methanol yield and space-time yield per gram of catalyst; the downstream ranking uses net production cost. Re purchase price and catalyst replacement are not included in the reference objective. Purge is varied from 0.5% to 40% over 396 levels, with an additional optimization in which purge is selected separately for each catalyst state. Local economic leverage for space-time yield, conversion and methane suppression is evaluated at the 5 wt% Re, 250 °C state. The process interpretation follows established CO₂-to-methanol assessments in which recycle, compression and separation materially affect plant performance.[20,21] The plant model was checked against published process assessments and pilot-plant data (Supplementary Table 5; Extended Data Fig. 4): at 22–33% per-pass conversion its hydrogen and CO₂ consumption (0.193–0.208 and 1.41–1.47 tonnes per tonne of methanol), carbon efficiency (0.93–0.98) and recycle ratio (2.6–5.4) lie within the reported ranges, and at the operating point and price assumptions of ref. 20 it reproduces its break-even methanol price, production cost plus capital recovery, to within 2.4%. The catalyst-independent stoichiometric H₂ and CO₂ demand is 583.2 euros per tonne on both sides; the catalyst-sensitive terms (feed above stoichiometry, electricity, catalyst replacement and capital) sum to 98.3 against 116.4 euros per tonne in the reference. Setting the loop pressure drop, recycle flow, compression, capital and prices to reference values leaves the space-time-yield disagreement at 24–26 of 81 comparisons without the inlet limit and 41–50 of 81 with it (Extended Data Fig. 4).

The measurement Monte Carlo samples the CO₂ conversion and selectivities of each catalyst state from that state's own uncertainty and re-solves the full methanol plant model in each of 5,000 draws (seed 20261005), with every cost parameter held at its reference value. The source reports single integer values, so the uncertainty is built from the source data: the reporting resolution (±0.5 percentage points, with '<1%' sampled over 0–1%), a 5.2% relative uncertainty in conversion derived from the consistency between space-time yield and conversion across the 21 reported runs, and a 0.85-percentage-point exchange between methanol and methane selectivity derived from the 11 runs of a single catalyst. Space-time yield follows conversion and methanol selectivity. Halving or doubling every width retains the economic winner in 99.3% or 78.4% of draws. The 1 wt% Re, 250 °C state uses the Table 3 selectivities of 97% methanol, 1% CO and 1% CH₄.

Counterfactuals remove one candidate difference at a time (methane selectivity moved to methanol; conversion set to the four-state mean) and re-solve the plant at the reference purge. Backward design changes one property of the space-time-yield winner until its cost equals the economic optimum.

### Published methanol comparisons

Every extracted record with CO₂ conversion, methanol selectivity, temperature, pressure and H₂/CO₂ ratio is a candidate operating point; the primary analysis uses entries whose conversion and selectivities are printed numbers, and entries with plot-read values are added as a sensitivity. Groups are formed by the same rule in both cohorts: of the 161 groups that include plot-read values, 33 come from 22 papers without printed values and 47 have fewer than two printed entries, which leaves the 81 groups of the primary analysis (Supplementary Table 6). An operating point printed twice in one paper is counted once. Selectivities are closed as in the hand-built case: methanol and methane as reported, entries below the reporting limit set to zero (setting the 14 such entries to their printed bound changes no comparison), and the CO-like residual equal to one minus the methanol and methane selectivities; when methane is not reported, it is one minus the methanol and CO selectivities if CO is reported and zero otherwise, and both bounds of an unreported CO–methane split are tested. Comparison groups contain the entries of one paper at the same pressure, H₂/CO₂ ratio and space velocity. One paper ranks its catalysts per gram of Re;[19] on that basis its disagreement is the same catalyst at another temperature, so the first extraction's other-catalyst and other-temperature counts become 15 of 32 and 13 of 22 in the inert-limited loop (9 of 32 and 6 of 22 without the limit), while the totals are unchanged. A group contains at least two catalysts, a temperature series of one catalyst, or both; a disagreement counts as another catalyst when the plant-cost leader is a different catalyst and as another temperature when it is the paper's leader at a different temperature. Entries whose feed adds water or CO to CO₂ and H₂ are excluded, because the plant model takes a dry CO₂/H₂ make-up. Space-time yield per gram of catalyst defines the paper leaderboard: the printed value when every entry of a group prints it and the ratio of printed to conversion-derived productivity varies by less than a factor of 3 within the group, otherwise the value derived from the mass space velocity, otherwise from the volumetric space velocity with a bulk density of 1.0 g mL⁻¹ (0.5 and 2.0 g mL⁻¹ tested). The plant leaderboard is the net production cost at each entry's own temperature, pressure and H₂/CO₂ ratio, with catalyst mass from the group's productivity basis. Recycled CO is converted per pass to the smallest extent that keeps the reactor outlet at or below both reverse-water-gas-shift and CO-hydrogenation equilibrium, the single-pass CO₂ conversion is capped at the loop's CO₂-hydrogenation equilibrium, and the purge is optimized over 0.5–40% among the levels at which the reactor-inlet content of species other than H₂ and CO₂ (CO, methane and N₂) stays at or below 6.86%, the cost model's limit for the loop that anchors it (6.46% CO and N₂ in its stream table, 6.78% with methanol and water[34]). CO counts toward this limit because, at the cost-optimal purge, recycled CO is converted little further and dilutes the CO₂ and H₂ feed as N₂ does; it still reacts up to the equilibrium bound. The loop without the limit (purge extended to 10⁻⁴% as a check), limits of 4–30%, spanning the 4.4–9.8% of CO, methane and N₂ (4.9–10.2% of all species other than H₂ and CO₂) in four published loop designs, a limit on methane and N₂ alone, recycled CO treated as inert and recycled CO converted up to CO-hydrogenation equilibrium are reported as sensitivities (Extended Data Fig. 8; Supplementary Note 3). A disagreement is near-infeasible when the paper leader's cost in the limited loop exceeds twice its cost without the limit or the regret exceeds 100%. Variants use inert CO, 2% purge, single-pass yield, conversion or selectivity as the paper leaderboard, all entries including plot readings, entries whose reported product selectivities sum to at least 95%, and the comparisons without the paper that supplies most groups. In the catalyst-inventory variant each entry may load more catalyst, raising its conversion along an approach-to-equilibrium curve calibrated on its measured point to at most 95% of equilibrium at constant selectivity. Pressure-dependent condensation enters both leaderboards of a group equally because a group shares one pressure. The lower bound used to allocate the full plant optimization evaluates the same economics at each purge level on lower-bound loop flows that hold for any per-pass conversion of recycled CO between 0 and 1: reactor-inlet CO₂ of at least 1/[X(S_MeOH + (1 − p)S_CO)] per mole of methanol, three moles of H₂ per mole of methanol and four per mole of methane, the loop methane and nitrogen that the purge fraction p sets, and at least two moles of crude liquid; every equipment term rises with its flow, so the minimum over purge levels bounds the plant cost from below. Within each group the paper's leader is evaluated in full, then the remaining candidates in increasing order of the bound until the next bound exceeds the best cost found. Sampling uncertainty is a paper-cluster bootstrap with 10,000 resamples. Measurement uncertainty re-draws every entry 2,000 times with the Re/TiO₂ basis above (5.2% relative conversion error, a 0.85-percentage-point methanol–methane selectivity exchange and, for printed space-time yields, an independent 5.2% relative error), at one, two and four times these widths, and rebuilds both leaderboards from the same draw. Plant costs of perturbed entries come from an additive per-coordinate response surface built from exact model runs, with draws outside its grid run exactly; 7 of 600 group verdicts (4 of 200 at the reported noise level) in the inert-limited loop and 1 of 600 without the limit differ from exact runs of complete replicates. The catalyst-price variant prices each catalyst from its extracted composition, with metal contents at the model and 2025 commodity prices[65] plus a base cost that gives commercial Cu/ZnO/Al₂O₃ 18.1 euros per kilogram,[34] and charges replacement per tonne of inventory.

### Au/TiO₂ rank-preservation control

The Au/TiO₂ control compares 2–6 nm particles under a common operating condition and process mapping. The reference rate, Au loading and dispersion are anchored to Janssens et al.,[24] and the particle-size dependence is taken from the 4.5 wt% Au/TiO₂ series reported by Overbury et al.[25] The broader physical basis for low-temperature Au/TiO₂ activity and particle-size effects follows the established literature.[22,23] Rank preservation is evaluated in 10,000 parameter samples drawn from ranges reported in these studies. An additional analysis introduces candidate-specific kinetic variation and independently optimizes temperature and O₂/CO ratio within the specified ranges derived from the literature.

### Adaptive Catalyst Screening Agent

ACSA fetches each paper through the institutional library service, converts it to page-marked text and page images, and extracts every catalyst entry into a fixed schema in which each number carries its value, unit, qualifier, location and page. Extraction uses one strict-JSON language-model call per paper and pass (gpt-5.5, medium reasoning effort): a main pass over text and page images, a figure pass that digitizes every catalyst-performance point in the main-text figures, and passes over the text, tables and figures of the Supporting Information. A printed main-text value outranks a printed Supporting-Information value, which outranks a plot reading. Units are normalized to the model basis. Prompts contain only the paper itself.

Accuracy is scored against two curated methanol datasets[32,33] and the 21 Re/TiO₂ entries of the hand-built case. Entries are matched by paper and catalyst name, or, for the dataset that lists compositions instead of names,[33] by paper, operating conditions and elemental composition, never by the values being scored; a pair with an equally admissible alternative partner counts for recall but not for value accuracy. Strict tolerances are ±1 K for temperature, ±1% for pressure and space-time yield, ±0.05 for the H₂/CO₂ ratio and ±0.5 percentage points for conversion and selectivity. Every disagreement between extracted and curated values was checked against the paper. Curated values that the paper contradicts fall into 26 root causes, one mechanism in one source paper each (45 rules, 169 cells and 8 rows across the two methanol datasets and the ammonia review), with a further 24 space-time-yield cells that one dataset derives from an erroneous input; that dataset computes space-time yield from its yield and space velocity, so a printed space-time yield that differs from the derivation is not counted as an error (Supplementary Table 7). Curated values are corrected to the printed value before scoring, and both raw and corrected scores are reported. Of the extracted entries without a curated partner, 150 of 179 in the first 18 papers and a random sample of 87 in the 31 papers added later were checked against the source. A random sample of 100 extracted values from 10 papers was checked by the authors against the papers and Supporting Information: 91 are printed or equal after unit conversion, 6 are H₂/CO₂ ratios derived from printed feed compositions, and 3 match after a corrected locator, a restored upper-bound qualifier or a stated metric name; none contradicts the source.

Before new candidates are scored, ACSA runs the hand-built inputs of all three systems, and the extracted Re/TiO₂ and Au/TiO₂ inputs, through the same code path and requires the frozen results to be reproduced; the batch runs do not start otherwise. Every extracted Au/TiO₂ input must also equal the hand-built value, because the dispersion enters the chain only as a ratio and cannot be checked through the outputs. A value that the source prints twice with different numbers is accepted in either form: the Au loading appears as 4.41 wt% in the text and 4.40 wt% in Table 1 of the source paper,[24] which changes the required Au mass by 0.2% and no ranking quantity. Candidates are then taken through the forward chain; the descriptor-only lower bound decides which ammonia candidates need the full process optimization, the purge-wise plant bound decides which methanol candidates need the equilibrium-constrained purge optimization, and all candidates were additionally evaluated in full to verify both bounds. The counterfactual and backward calculations are run on the leading candidates only.
