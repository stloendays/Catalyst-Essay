# Process economics reorder catalyst rankings across computed and published catalysts

## Abstract

Catalyst screening ranks materials by descriptors or laboratory performance, whereas industrial selection depends on how catalyst properties propagate through reactor operation and plant economics. We couple catalyst-specific kinetics to candidate-specific process optimization and a catalyst-responsive cost objective, and invert that objective into catalyst-property targets, and ask where the leading catalyst changes between the atomic, laboratory and industrial optimum. In ammonia synthesis the intrinsic order Ru > Os > Fe becomes Fe > Ru > Os; with descriptor kinetics Ru would need a 201-fold activity gain, outside the scaling-constrained space, whereas promotion supplies gains of that size. Measured rate laws for the two frontier catalysts lower that requirement to 25-fold and make the decision turn on how the Ru inventory is financed: a staged converter, fused iron upstream and supported Ru downstream, is the least-cost design at 0.8–6.7% per year, a range that contains the disclosed lease rates for platinum-group catalyst metal. An agent that extracts published data and runs the full chain on every candidate extends this to 1,695 alloy and metal surfaces, 33 methanol studies and 30 ammonia studies. Among alloys of transition metals outside groups 3–5, only cheap 3d–group-6 pairs undercut Fe. Within published comparisons, the laboratory leader is not the plant-cost leader in 21% of ammonia cases and in 38–70% of methanol cases, depending on whether the recycle loop limits the CO, methane and nitrogen that build up at the reactor inlet (7 and 22 of 40 with a cost gap above 5%).

## Introduction

Computational catalyst discovery ranks candidate materials by adsorption energies, reactivity descriptors, scaling relations, volcano plots and surrogate models,[1–6] and descriptor-based microkinetic and multiscale models connect those quantities to rates and operating conditions.[6–8] Experimental studies rank catalysts by laboratory activity, selectivity and space-time yield. Both rankings stand in for an industrial decision that depends on how catalyst properties propagate through reactor operation, separation, recycle and plant economics. Previous work has linked ammonia descriptors to reactor design and catalyst selection,[9] formulated catalyst and process design as coupled problems[10,11] and shown that laboratory rankings can depend on the test environment.[12] Whether the identity of the leading catalyst survives candidate-specific process optimization and economic evaluation has not been measured across a field.

The leading candidates matter most. A screen can remain well correlated across a large candidate set while changing the material that would actually be selected, and catalyst cost per kilogram can be a poor proxy for cost per unit product once stability and production scale are included.[13] In ammonia synthesis, process-level Fe/Ru studies show that the preferred catalyst and pressure depend on plant scale and loop configuration.[14,15]

Here we propagate catalyst properties forward through kinetics, candidate-specific process optimization and a catalyst-responsive cost objective, and backward from an economic target to the catalyst property it requires, testing whether that property lies within the range allowed by the underlying scaling relations.[10,11,16] We study ammonia synthesis, in which intrinsic activity and metal price set catalyst inventory and operating conditions;[5,17,18] CO₂ hydrogenation to methanol, in which selectivity sets reactant loss, purge, recycle and compression;[19–21] and Au/TiO₂ CO oxidation, a control in which particle size maps monotonically to catalyst requirement.[22–25] The three systems test the chain between three optima: the atomic optimum, set by a descriptor and intrinsic turnover; the laboratory optimum, the leader of a published comparison; and the industrial optimum, the least-cost catalyst after process optimization and costing. Ammonia runs from the atomic to the industrial optimum, methanol from the laboratory to the industrial optimum, and Au/TiO₂ from the laboratory optimum to the catalyst requirement (Fig. 1a). Language-model agents can now read the chemical literature and operate computational tools.[26–30] We use one, the Adaptive Catalyst Screening Agent (ACSA), as the automation layer that extracts catalytic data from publications, reproduces the hand-built cases and runs the same chain on every candidate. This turns statements about a few hand-selected catalysts into a field-level measurement: within the comparisons that published methanol and ammonia papers make themselves, how often is the laboratory leader not the plant-cost leader?

## Results

### Process economics reverse the leading ammonia ranking

The 15-metal ammonia screen ranks Ru, Os and Fe first, second and third in intrinsic activity (Fig. 1c), consistent with the established activity landscape for transition-metal ammonia synthesis.[17,18] After the operating conditions are optimized separately for each catalyst over 14,136 process states and the catalyst-dependent costs are evaluated (Fig. 1a,d,e), the order becomes Fe, Ru and Os, at 15.292, 22.031 and 25.832 US dollars per tonne of NH₃ (Fig. 1b,h). The Spearman correlation across all 15 metals remains 0.929, whereas among the three leaders Spearman rho is -0.50 (Fig. 1f,g): the screen stays correlated while the selected catalyst changes. Selecting Ru instead of Fe would raise the catalyst-dependent cost by 44.1%, the normalized decision regret. The 6.739 US dollars per tonne difference is a small part of the cost of ammonia from state-of-the-art gas-based plants, about 160 US dollars per tonne,[31] but it is the part that catalyst selection controls, 2.34 million US dollars per year for a 1,000 t/d plant (Supplementary Note 1).

### Published laboratory leaders are often not the plant-cost leaders

The hand-built analyses compare three ammonia metals, four methanol states and five Au particle sizes. ACSA scales the same analysis to every candidate that publications or databases provide, while the kinetic, process and economic models remain unchanged (Fig. 2a). It reads each paper and its Supporting Information and records every catalyst entry with its composition, operating conditions, conversion, selectivities and rates, together with the table, figure and page each value comes from. Before it scores any new candidate it must reproduce the frozen results of all three hand-built cases, and a cheap lower bound on cost decides which candidates need the full process optimization. Scored on 50 methanol papers (Supplementary Table 1) against two curated datasets,[32,33] the agent recovers 719 of the 741 curated entries (97%). It transcribes printed values almost without error: 44 of 44 CO₂ conversions and 44 of 44 methanol selectivities in main-text tables, and 129 of 130 conversions, 124 of 129 selectivities and 118 of 128 space-time yields in the Supporting Information; most remaining errors are plot readings (Extended Data Fig. 1; Supplementary Note 2). The self-check reproduces the 21 Re/TiO₂ entries to 2.3 × 10⁻¹³ euros per tonne and every pure-metal ammonia cost to 10⁻⁹ US dollars per tonne. In methanol, without the inlet limit, the lower bound spares the full plant optimization for 199 of 310 candidates (64.2%) and still finds the plant-cost leader of all 40 comparisons, at 36% of the compute (Extended Data Fig. 2).

Methanol papers rank catalysts by laboratory performance, most often by space-time yield. ACSA extracted 351 operating points from 33 studies with printed conversion, selectivity, temperature, pressure and H₂/CO₂ ratio. Within each paper, entries tested at the same pressure, H₂/CO₂ ratio and space velocity form the comparison the paper itself makes, giving 40 comparisons with 310 entries in 28 papers; with plot-read values the same rule gives 83 comparisons in 44 papers (Methods). Each entry was taken through the methanol recycle–economics model at its own conditions, with recycled CO converted per pass by the least amount that keeps the reactor outlet at or below reverse-water-gas-shift and CO-hydrogenation equilibrium and single-pass conversion capped at the loop's CO₂-hydrogenation equilibrium. Because the model holds each entry's single-pass conversion at its laboratory value, the loop must also keep the reactor feed as undiluted as a working loop does: CO, methane and N₂ together are limited to 6.86% of the reactor inlet, the cost model's limit for the loop that anchors it (6.46% CO and N₂ in that loop's stream table[34]), and the purge is optimized within that limit. CO counts toward the limit because recycled CO dilutes the CO₂ and H₂ feed as N₂ does; it still reacts up to the equilibrium bound. In this inert-limited loop the space-time-yield leader is not the plant-cost leader in 28 of 40 comparisons (70%; 95% confidence interval 54–83% from resampling papers), 22 of them with a cost gap above 5%, spread over 21 of the 28 papers, and 707 of 1,997 pairwise orderings (35%) invert (Fig. 2b). Without the inlet limit, which implicitly lets a diluted feed react as in the laboratory, the leaders differ in 15 of 40 comparisons (38%), 7 of them above 5%: depending on how the loop handles the species that build up at the reactor inlet, the laboratory leader is not the plant-cost leader in 38–70% of comparisons. Limiting methane and N₂ alone and leaving CO unlimited gives 18 of 40, 8 above 5%, close to the unlimited loop, so the increase comes from CO build-up.

The disagreement holds for inlet limits of 5–10% (24–28 of 40, 18–25 above 5%) and weakens towards 15% (24 and 18 of 40 at 12% and 15%, 13 and 12 above 5%; Extended Data Fig. 8; Supplementary Note 3). The plant-cost leader is another catalyst in 16 of the 32 comparisons that contain at least two catalysts, 11 of them above 5%; in 12 it runs at the paper leader's temperature, 8 of these above 5% and from 8 papers. It is the same catalyst at another temperature in 12 of the 22 comparisons that contain a temperature series, 11 above 5%.

The disagreement is neither a sampling nor a measurement artefact (Extended Data Fig. 3). Re-measuring every entry 2,000 times with the conversion and selectivity uncertainty derived for the Re/TiO₂ states keeps 24 of the 28 disagreements in at least 90% of re-measurements, whereas measurement noise alone changes a paper's space-time-yield leader in 4 comparisons (95% range 1–8). In 11 of the 28 the paper leader meets the inlet limit only with a large purge and costs more than twice its cost without the limit; the other 17, 12 of them above 5%, involve no such jump (Supplementary Note 3). The disagreement holds without the study that supplies most comparisons (21 of 32, 17 above 5%), when plot-read entries are added (54 of 82 comparisons), for entries whose reported products close to methanol, CO and methane (17 of 28), with unreported CO and methane selectivities set to either bound (26–29 of 40), with recycled CO treated as inert (28 of 40), when each catalyst is priced from its composition (25 of 40) and when each catalyst may load more catalyst and raise its conversion towards equilibrium (23 of 40, 16 above 5%, from 12 papers). The plant model reproduces the hydrogen and CO₂ consumption, carbon efficiency and recycle ratio of published plant assessments and, on the same basis, the break-even methanol price of one of them to within 2.4% (Extended Data Fig. 4).

The plant ranking follows from what each by-product does in a recycle loop (Fig. 2d,e). Recycled CO is converted per pass only as far as the shift and CO-hydrogenation equilibria require; at the cost-optimal purge it is barely converted further and accumulates, so that without the inlet limit the reactor feed carries a median 7.1% CO (90th percentile 22%), against 1.3–4.7% in published loop designs. Holding the feed to the limit costs purge, and the purge a catalyst needs grows with the CO and methane it makes. Under the limit the plant-cost leader is more selective to methanol than the paper leader in 19 of the 28 disagreements and makes less CO in 18, whereas without the limit it more often runs at higher single-pass conversion (9 of 15). For InNi₃C₀.₅ on different supports at 300 °C and 4 MPa (Supplementary Table 1), the space-time-yield leader on m-ZrO₂ (8.5% conversion, 88% methanol selectivity, 12% CO) needs a 4.0% purge and costs 1,289 euros per tonne, against 1,138 for the catalyst on SiO₂ (2.2% conversion, 95% selectivity, 5% CO) at 0.6% purge; without the limit the order is reversed, 919 against 1,101. No single laboratory metric replaces the plant model: in the inert-limited loop ranking by conversion disagrees almost as often as ranking by space-time yield (27 of 40) and ranking by methanol selectivity in 17 of 40, with larger misses (median regret 51%); without the limit selectivity is the worst proxy (31 of 40). A high-pressure Cu/Al₂O₃ study that supplies 8 of the 40 comparisons[35] gives the largest regrets in both loops.

Ammonia papers rank catalysts by rate. ACSA extracted 30 primary ammonia-synthesis papers (Supplementary Table 2), with the Supporting Information of 28 of them. Each catalyst's measured rate is inverted into an effective descriptor on its metal's side of the volcano, and the catalyst is optimized over the full process library with its own metal content. Within each paper, catalysts with printed rate, temperature and outlet that were tested at the same temperature, pressure, H₂/N₂ ratio and space velocity form 33 comparisons in 23 papers. The catalyst with the highest rate per gram of catalyst is not the plant-cost leader in 7 of them (21%; 95% confidence interval 7–37% from resampling papers), in 6 of 23 papers, with a median regret of 10% where the leaders differ (Fig. 2c). In 3 comparisons a catalyst of another metal is the plant-cost leader, in all 3 an Fe catalyst on the same support with the lower rate (Fig. 2f), and in 2 a commercial fused-iron catalyst that the paper includes as a reference is the plant-cost leader. Across the 73 catalysts tabulated in a review of ammonia-synthesis catalysts,[36] laboratory rate and plant cost are nearly uncorrelated (Spearman ρ = 0.16), and none of these catalysts undercuts the fused-iron benchmark without metal recovery (Extended Data Fig. 5; Supplementary Note 4).

### Outside nitride formers and sp metals, surfaces that undercut Fe pair a cheap 3d metal with a group-6 metal

To test whether the Fe preference survives a much larger candidate space, ACSA took the 1,796 bimetallic and pure-metal surfaces of a high-throughput adsorption database[37,38] through the ammonia chain (Methods). Among the 372 surfaces that contain only transition metals outside groups 3–5, 138 satisfy the 90 m³ bed-volume limit. Five undercut Fe when the database is bridged linearly onto the model's descriptor and six under an element-anchored bridge; every one of them pairs Fe, Co, Ni or Cu with Cr, Mo or W (Extended Data Fig. 6), the combination that descriptor interpolation has identified for ammonia synthesis.[39] Surfaces with group 3–5 elements, which form stable bulk nitrides, and with sp metals, which lie outside the transition-metal scaling relations of the activity model, are reported as separate layers (Supplementary Table 3). The most active surface ranks 43rd economically (Os₃Pt, 70.9% regret) and at its own price would need a 689-fold activity increase to reach Fe. The descriptor-only lower bound excludes 1,397 of the 1,695 priced surfaces (82.4%) from the full optimization without excluding any that undercut Fe (Supplementary Note 5).

### Metal price and process optimization decide the Fe–Ru ranking

A layer-wise check locates the ammonia reversal. Converting turnover frequency into the active-metal requirement for plant throughput preserves the intrinsic order; charging that metal at its price over a 10-year life gives Fe < Ru < Os, with annualized metal replacement of 0.199 US dollars per tonne for Fe against 33.57 for Ru, before any reactor, compression, refrigeration or equipment cost is added. Setting the Ru price equal to the Fe value of 8 US dollars per kilogram and reoptimizing makes Ru the cheaper catalyst (14.712 against 15.292 US dollars per tonne), and a price sweep with reoptimization at every price places Fe–Ru parity at 163.76 US dollars per kilogram (Fig. 3d). Process optimization then narrows the gap: Ru moves to about 450 °C and 425 bar, where a small bed holds little metal, while Fe operates near 425 °C and 180 bar (Fig. 3b,c). The remaining 6.739 US dollars per tonne comes mainly from fresh-feed compression (+4.632), metal inventory (+1.763), compressor capital (+1.181) and refrigeration (+0.629), partly offset by vessel, recycle-compression and reactor terms (Fig. 3a,e).

The Fe preference holds across the economic range examined. In 5,000 preregistered draws of metal prices, capital cost, electricity price and catalyst lifetime, each with full reoptimization, Fe is cheaper in every draw, P(C_Fe < C_Ru) = 1.000, with a minimum difference of 2.382 US dollars per tonne (Fig. 3h); the regret has 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles of 30.48%, 40.89% and 54.25%. Descriptor uncertainty matters more: across 1,000 descriptor samples Fe ranks first economically in 68.1% and Ru in 31.9%, in most of the latter because the Fe bed exceeds the 90 m³ limit (Fig. 3f,g; Supplementary Note 6).

The benchmark formulation charges Ru as the bed of the industrial fused-iron catalyst. A promoted Ru/C ammonia catalyst exposes 11% of its Ru atoms,[40] against fewer than 1% of the Fe atoms in reduced fused iron;[41] the commercial catalyst carries about 8 wt% Ru,[42] and 90–95.55% of the Ru in spent promoted Ru/C ammonia catalysts is recovered.[43,44] Read at the effective Ru price p(1 − r)/u, 218–490 US dollars per kilogram, Ru costs 15.41–15.67 US dollars per tonne of NH₃, 0.12–0.38 above Fe, and would reach parity with a 1.2–2.2-fold activity gain (Fig. 3d). With the supported catalyst's own bed (8 wt% Ru at 430–550 kg m⁻³), it costs 15.69–16.01, 0.40–0.72 above Fe. In a KAAP-type loop at about 9 MPa with a −20 °C refrigerated condenser,[45] it costs 18.34–18.91 in the benchmark reading and 18.67–19.08 on its own bed, against 19.07 for fused iron in that loop: below Fe at 95.55% recovery in every bed and at 90% recovery in the 490 and 550 kg m⁻³ beds. With the descriptor kinetics, supported and recovered Ru on its own bed thus reaches the Fe cost in the low-pressure loop for which it was developed and stays above it in the high-pressure Fe loop. These recovered-Ru costs are scenarios, since the baseline ranking assumes no recovery, and they charge the Ru inventory, as the benchmark model does, only for the metal lost at each replacement. Financing that inventory over the 25-year plant life at the 12% rate applied to equipment raises Ru to 21.79 US dollars per tonne against 19.14 for Fe in the KAAP-type loop, and parity requires a metal financing rate below 0.56% per year; at the 1.35–5.10% per year that one refiner disclosed for 32 platinum-group catalyst-metal leases, Ru stays 0.34–1.48 above Fe (0.19–1.43 if leased; Supplementary Note 6). Sampling dispersion, recovery and bed density turns the decision into a probability: Fe is cheaper in 71.9% of draws for a commercial Ru/C bed (5–10 wt% Ru, 430–550 kg m⁻³), and in 94.7% with the activities and Ru contents of the 54 measured Ru catalysts (Extended Data Fig. 7).

The descriptor kinetics matter most for the two catalysts at the decision frontier, and for Ru they are furthest from measurement: at 90 bar, 400 °C and 2% ammonia they give apparent orders of +2.5 in H₂ and −2.0 in NH₃, against +0.3 and −0.4 for promoted Ru/C, whose hydrogen adsorption lowers but does not reverse the hydrogen order, and fused iron −0.4 in NH₃ against −1.0 measured. Replacing the rate laws of this pair alone with measured ones, the Temkin–Pyzhev form fitted to an industrial magnetite catalyst[46,47] and the kinetics of a promoted Ru/C catalyst,[40] and integrating each along the bed, keeps fused iron ahead of Ru in the benchmark formulation. Fe costs 15.58 US dollars per tonne at 425 °C and 170 bar; Ru costs 28.07 at 325 bar, down from 425 bar but not into the 100–200 bar range, where its best cost is 29.08, and would need a 25-fold activity gain for parity (15–37-fold across bed-sizing, calibration and equilibrium variants), against 201-fold with the descriptor kinetics.

For supported Ru/C the measured kinetics move the decision to the financing of the Ru inventory. Its optimum is at 145 bar, and it costs 1.36 US dollars per tonne less than Fe when the Ru inventory carries no financing charge and 1.12 more at 12% per year (in the KAAP-type loop 1.07 less and 1.86 more). With both catalysts available to a three-bed converter at 9 MPa, the least-cost design puts fused iron in the first bed and Ru/C downstream for Ru financing rates of 0.8–6.7% per year, the bed order of the Kellogg Advanced Ammonia Process converter, magnetite first and Ru/C in the following beds,[45] and of published Fe + Ru multibed designs;[48] all-Ru/C wins below that range, all-Fe above it, and a converter with Ru/C first is never optimal. Fused iron takes the first, hottest bed because Ru/C is held to 460 °C, the top of its measured range; Ru/C takes the downstream beds because ammonia inhibits fused iron more strongly, so that the Ru/C rate advantage per kilogram grows from 16-fold at 2% to 52-fold at 14% NH₃. The 1.35–5.10% per year disclosed for platinum-group catalyst-metal leases falls inside this window, where staging saves 0.10–0.78 US dollars per tonne over an all-Fe converter (Supplementary Note 6).

### Backward design separates the required catalyst-property region from physical reachability

Multiplying Ru intrinsic activity by a factor α and reoptimizing the process at each value, Ru reaches the Fe cost only after a 201.22-fold increase; the 5th–95th percentile requirement under economic uncertainty is 70.78–462-fold (Fig. 4a). The scaling relation allows much less: at most 1.090-fold at 673 K and 2.525-fold across all process conditions, and the lowest strict-scaling Ru cost, 21.398 US dollars per tonne at *E*_N = −1.215 eV, remains above the Fe cost (Fig. 4b). Improving Ru activity along the present scaling relation cannot make Ru competitive.

Measured catalysts show which modifications supply gains of that size (Fig. 4a, bottom). Ba promotion of Ru/C raises the turnover frequency per surface Ru 75-fold and Cs promotion 65-fold,[49] Ba promotion of Ru/BN more than 100-fold at 5 MPa[50] and Cs promotion of Ru/MgO more than 134-fold;[51] the Ba and MgO-supported Cs gains lie within the parity band and the Cs–Ru/C gain just below it, whereas electride and oxyhydride supports give about tenfold[52,53] and confinement inside carbon nanotubes 0.5-fold.[54] Electronic promotion, which changes the energetics beyond a single scaling relation, is the measured route that reaches the parity band.

Lifecycle variables move the target before reachability is tested. Allowing Ru recovery and catalyst lifetime to improve together lowers the activity requirement: at 99% recovery, a 20-year lifetime needs at most a 1.401-fold gain (Fig. 4c). On the strict *E*_N scaling manifold across all 14,136 process states, parity is controlled by the catalyst-loss factor \(q=(1-r)/L\), with \(q^*=4.4068\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{y}^{-1}\). At a 20-year lifetime and 99% recovery the best strict-scaling state costs 15.362 US dollars per tonne, 0.071 US dollars per tonne above Fe, and parity requires 99.119% recovery at 20 years or a 22.69-year lifetime at 99% recovery (Fig. 4d). The backward-design result is thus a narrow property region near the edge of the present scaling-constrained design space rather than a single activity threshold (Supplementary Note 7).

### Selectivity, recycle and purge reshape the methanol ranking

The mechanism behind the methanol field result is visible in four Re/TiO₂ catalyst–temperature states (Fig. 5a,b).[19] By space-time yield per gram of Re the order is 1 wt% Re at 250 °C, 1 wt% Re at 200 °C, 5 wt% Re at 200 °C and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C. After the recycle and separation model at 2% purge, the economic order becomes 5 wt% Re at 200 °C, 1 wt% Re at 250 °C, 1 wt% Re at 200 °C and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C, with net production costs of 943, 962, 967 and 1,258 euros per tonne (Fig. 5c). Within the 6.86% inlet limit, at each state's optimal purge, the order becomes 1 wt% Re at 200 °C, 1 wt% Re at 250 °C, 5 wt% Re at 200 °C and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C (921, 982, 997 and 1,765 euros per tonne), and the space-time-yield winner stays second, now at 6.6% regret. Selecting the space-time-yield winner costs 1.93% more, and two of six pairwise orderings invert (Fig. 5e). Methane suppression has a local economic leverage of 0.37579, against 0.05883 for single-pass conversion and 0.00289 for space-time yield (Fig. 5a,g), because methane consumes reactants and accumulates in the loop, raising purge, recycle and compression.[20,21] No purge level between 0.5% and 40% makes the space-time-yield winner the least-cost state (Fig. 5f).

Counterfactual and backward calculations locate the cause. Moving every state's methane selectivity into methanol reverses the correlation to rho = -0.60, whereas equalizing conversion raises it to +1.00. For the space-time-yield winner, no increase in space-time yield reaches parity: unlimited productivity still costs 954.64 euros per tonne against 943.30. Raising its single-pass conversion from 0.23 to 0.279, inside the range measured on the same catalyst family, or reaching complete methanol selectivity does. When each state's own measurement uncertainty is propagated through the plant model, the economic winner keeps first place in 91.2% of 5,000 draws (Fig. 5d; Supplementary Note 8).

### A monotonic catalyst-to-process mapping preserves the ranking

Process evaluation does not change every ranking. In Au/TiO₂ CO oxidation, with the rate anchor of Janssens et al.[24] and the particle-size dependence of Overbury et al.,[25] candidates differ only in particle size from 2 to 6 nm under one operating condition and process model. Intrinsic activity and required catalyst mass preserve the order 2 > 3 > 4 > 5 > 6 nm in all 10,000 parameter samples drawn from the literature ranges (Fig. 6b–d), and in 92.16% when candidate-specific kinetic and operating variations are introduced over 273.15–293.15 K (Fig. 6e). Rankings change when catalyst properties alter downstream operating requirements strongly enough to change relative costs: through catalyst inventory and operating regime in ammonia, and through reactant loss, purge and recycle in methanol (Fig. 6a,f; Supplementary Note 9).

## Discussion

Rank agreement across a screen does not guarantee the same industrial choice. In ammonia the full 15-metal ranking remains strongly correlated while the leading order Ru > Os > Fe becomes Fe > Ru > Os. The reversal occurs where catalyst demand is monetized through metal price and replacement; process reoptimization narrows the Ru penalty by moving Ru to high pressure and a small bed, and shifts the remaining gap into compression and refrigeration. Selecting on the upstream proxy costs 44.1% in the ammonia objective, 1.93% in the methanol case and nothing in the Au/TiO₂ control. A first-order expansion makes the condition explicit. For a higher-is-better upstream metric s and a lower-is-better downstream objective J, Δln J ≈ -ε_s Δln s + Σ_k ε_k Δln z_k, where the z_k are catalyst-dependent process or economic variables; the upstream order inverts when the aggregate downstream penalty exceeds the direct benefit. The transfer index χ_AB = ln(J_A/J_B) / ln(s_A/s_B) is a model-agnostic sign diagnostic of this outcome: +0.085 for Ru versus Fe, +0.015 for the methanol space-time-yield winner versus the cost winner and approximately -1.00 for 2 nm versus 6 nm Au/TiO₂.

The field measurement shows that these reversals are not hand-picked exceptions. Within the comparisons that published papers make themselves, the laboratory leader is not the plant-cost leader in 21% of ammonia comparisons and, depending on whether the recycle loop limits the species that build up at the reactor inlet, in 38–70% of methanol comparisons (7 and 22 of 40 with a cost gap above 5%). The leader changes at both steps: from the atomic to the industrial optimum in ammonia and from the laboratory to the industrial optimum in both reactions, while the Au/TiO₂ control keeps it. The causes are specific to each process and identifiable. In methanol, the by-products that accumulate in the loop, CO and methane, set purge and recycle and outweigh productivity once the feed must stay undiluted, and no single laboratory metric substitutes for the plant model: in the inert-limited loop ranking by conversion disagrees in 27 of 40 comparisons and ranking by selectivity in 17, with larger misses, and without the limit selectivity is the worst proxy (31 of 40). In ammonia, iron catalysts tested alongside more active ruthenium catalysts are cheaper to operate. Screening should therefore target the catalyst property that has sufficient downstream leverage to alter the industrial decision, and a laboratory comparison can report the process-level consequence of its leader alongside its rate or yield.

Backward design converts the ammonia mechanism into materials targets. Ru would need a 201-fold activity gain, far beyond the 2.5-fold reachable along its scaling relation, yet measured electronic promotion supplies gains inside the parity band, and dispersion with metal recovery brings supported Ru to the Fe cost only in a low-pressure loop, at high recovery and with little financing cost on the Ru inventory. With measured rate laws for the two frontier catalysts, the same economics select a staged converter, fused iron upstream and Ru/C downstream, at metal-financing rates that contain the disclosed lease rates for platinum-group catalyst metal, the bed order of the Kellogg Advanced Ammonia Process. Independent process analyses describe the same trade-off between lower-pressure operation, ammonia-separation refrigeration and the price, loading, lifetime and recycling of Ru.[15,55,56]

ACSA adds no new scientific model; its contribution is scale. Extraction reaches 97% recall and transcribes 92–100% of printed values correctly, the agent reproduces the hand-built cases before it scores new candidates, and cost bounds spare most of the full optimizations without losing a leader. The framework is modular: formulation-specific catalyst physics and higher-fidelity kinetic, process and economic models can replace the present ones without changing the decision logic, so every comparison a catalysis paper reports can be taken to its plant-level consequence.

## Methods

### Multiscale decision analysis

Each catalytic system is evaluated with a process-level objective matched to the catalyst-to-process pathway under study. For ammonia, the economic objective contains the terms that respond directly to catalyst identity and catalytic performance: catalyst inventory and replacement, reactor requirements, compression, refrigeration and pressure-dependent equipment. Common upstream H₂/N₂ supply contributions are held fixed so that they do not dilute the catalyst-dependent decision signal. The methanol model evaluates net production cost through its recycle and separation process, while the Au/TiO₂ control maps particle-size-dependent activity to required catalyst mass under a common process model. Cross-system comparison is made at the level of ranking propagation and coupling topology rather than by equating the absolute economic objectives of different reactions.

Within each system, rankings before and after process evaluation are compared using Spearman and Kendall correlations, pairwise inversions, top-rank identity and normalized decision regret. For a lower-is-better downstream objective J, regret is defined as R = (J_upstream-winner - J_downstream-winner) / J_downstream-winner. Analyses of the leading candidates are reported separately from full-set statistics so that changes at the top of the ranking are not obscured by agreement among weaker candidates. Backward design varies a selected catalyst property, reoptimizes the process and identifies the value required for economic parity. This value is then compared with the range accessible under the corresponding catalyst-property relation or scaling relation. Perturbation tests change one predefined input while preserving the other model assumptions and repeating the same downstream optimization.

### Ammonia synthesis

The ammonia calculation includes the 15-metal activity screen, scaling and kinetic relations, catalyst productivity and inventory, candidate-specific process optimization, pressure-dependent equipment capital and seven catalyst-dependent cost components. Temperature, pressure and separator temperature are reoptimized for each candidate over the full 14,136-state process grid. Descriptor uncertainty is propagated through 1,000 samples; Fe feasibility is evaluated against the 90 m³ bed-volume limit, and economic winner, preservation of the atomistic winner and top-three membership are recorded separately. The S1 source contains Fe terrace energetics but no Fe step-site row, so the Fe step descriptor is obtained from the 14-metal terrace-to-step scaling relation. Leave-one-out validation and prediction-interval propagation are reported in Supplementary Table 4; the regression residual standard error is 0.2271 eV, which is the Fe descriptor uncertainty used in the descriptor Monte Carlo analysis. The atomistic reference conditions (673 K, 24.5 bar N₂, 74.25 bar H₂ and 1 bar NH₃) and step-site descriptor framework follow the Wang–Abild-Pedersen Dataset S1 source.[57] Under those conditions, the source study places Fe and Ru close to the activity plateau and undercoordinated Os close to the volcano maximum.[57] The descriptor-level reconstruction recovers the same high-activity metal set, with Ru, Os and Fe as its leading three candidates, providing the ranking fidelity required for the downstream decision analysis. The Fe process optimum (425 °C, 180 bar) lies within the conventional industrial Fe Haber–Bosch operating envelope discussed in the ammonia-catalysis and process literature.[58,59] The resulting catalyst-specific optima quantify how intrinsic energetics propagate into operating severity and economic consequence within a common, controlled process model. Published Fe/Ru loop studies provide independent process-level context for these trends despite differences in catalyst formulation, plant scale and flowsheet design.[14] Pressure-CAPEX and equipment-cost diagnostics perturb the compressor, vessel, cost-index and capital-recovery assumptions by approximately 10–25%; the Fe > Ru > Os economic order and the activity-only reachability verdict are retained, demonstrating that the central decision is robust to these model-form perturbations.

For the Ru-price test, only the Ru metal purchase price is changed. It is first set equal to the Fe value of 8 US dollars per kilogram, after which Ru is reoptimized over the full process grid. The Ru-price sweep repeats this optimization at 241 logarithmically spaced prices between 1 and 3 × 10^5 US dollars per kilogram together with the two reference prices. Fe-Ru parity is located by bisection in log price. The baseline Fe-Ru cost difference is decomposed into the same seven cost components.

The preregistered economic Monte Carlo analysis uses 5,000 samples with seed 20260920. Fe and Ru metal-price multipliers are sampled independently from a log-uniform distribution over 0.5–2.0. A common CAPEX multiplier is sampled uniformly over 0.8–1.2, electricity price uniformly over 20–100 US dollars per megawatt-hour, and catalyst lifetime uniformly over 5–20 years. Fe and Ru are reoptimized for every sample. The Ru activity multiplier required for parity, α*, is recalculated against the sample-specific reoptimized Fe cost. The complete draw sequence has been reconstructed from the frozen seed and priors and reproduces the previously reported Fe/Ru cost and cost-gap quantiles exactly; normalized regret is then computed draw-by-draw as (C_Ru − C_Fe)/C_Fe. The supporting descriptor-correlation sensitivity does not modify this preregistered Monte Carlo. Instead, it preserves the frozen Fe normal marginal and the ±0.15 eV Ru/Os uniform marginals using a Gaussian copula with latent pairwise correlations from 0 to 0.9, and evaluates atomistic ordering on the frozen 673 K one-descriptor volcano. A separate common-bias sweep applies the same additive shift to Ru, Os and Fe descriptors. Neither sensitivity is used to report a new downstream economic probability. The actual-catalyst Monte Carlo adds inputs drawn from a second generator (seed 20261006), so that every preregistered draw keeps its values: the Ru dispersion ratio u = D_Ru/f_Fe, log-uniform over 11–50, with D_Ru from 11% for a Ba–Cs–K-promoted Ru/C catalyst[40] to about 50% for 2 nm Ru particles in Ba-promoted Ru/C[60] and f_Fe = 1% for the exposed Fe atoms of reduced fused iron;[41] Ru recovery uniform over 90–95.55%, the range measured for spent promoted Ru/C ammonia catalysts;[43,44] Ru content of the supported bed uniform over 5–10 wt%,[42] from a third generator (seed 20261007); and bed density uniform over 430–550 kg m⁻³, the range derived from a promoted Ru/C laboratory bed[61]. In the benchmark-bed variant the Ru price is lowered to p(1 − r)/u, as in Fig. 3d; in the supported-bed variant the Ru activity per kilogram of Ru is multiplied by u, the Ru charge is replaced at (1 − r), and the bed volume follows from the Ru content and bed density. The measured-activity variant replaces u by the effective descriptor and residual multiplier of a randomly drawn measured Ru catalyst together with its Ru content.

For backward design, Ru intrinsic activity is varied directly and the full process is reoptimized at each activity multiplier. Reachability is evaluated separately along the descriptor path defined by the ammonia scaling relation and over the full process grid. For the joint activity–lifecycle target analysis, catalyst lifetime and Ru recovery are represented through the effective replacement-metal price, P_eff = P_Ru(1 − r)(10 y/L). A conservative target region is constructed from the 53 distinct process states already visited by the independently reoptimized Ru price sweep: for each state, catalyst demand is rescaled by a direct activity multiplier and the volume- and pressure-dependent cost terms are recalculated. Restricting the optimization to this state subset can only overestimate the activity improvement required for parity, so the reported multipliers are upper bounds on the backward target.

Physical reachability of that target is then evaluated on the strict *E*_N scaling manifold using all 14,136 process states. At fixed process state, *E*_N affects cost through TOF and therefore the required active-metal inventory and bed volume; the metal-replacement, reactor-volume and pressure-vessel terms are monotone in that requirement, whereas state-specific compression and refrigeration costs are independent of *E*_N. The cost-minimizing descriptor for each state is therefore its TOF-maximizing value on the canonical 0.005-eV scaling grid. Taking the lower envelope across all states gives the lifecycle parity boundary as a function of \(q=(1-r)/L\). The canonical strict-scaling minimum is reproduced before the lifecycle calculation is accepted. The kinetic and scaling relations follow the established transition-metal ammonia-synthesis literature.[5,17,18,57]

The actual-catalyst analysis reads the fully reoptimized Ru price sweep at the effective price p(1 − r)/u, where u is the ratio of exposed metal fractions of the supported catalyst and fused iron (u = 0.11/0.01 = 11, a lower bound) and r the recovered fraction (90–95.55%); the activity multiple for parity is recomputed with full reoptimization at every price. The KAAP comparison restricts both catalysts to the 90 bar, −20 °C separator states. A supported bed holding 8 wt% Ru (5–10 wt%) instead of the common formulation changes only the reactor term; recomputing it for bed densities of 430–550 kg m⁻³ adds 0.10–0.13 US dollars per tonne without recovery and 0.25–0.38 with 90–95.55% recovery (0.12–0.47 in the KAAP loop) at 8 wt%. Metal recovery is an optional scenario input, not a condition imposed on the baseline metal comparison. A lifecycle cash-flow variant keeps the process optimization and prices every metal purchase and recovered-metal credit at its date over the 25-year plant life (purchases at 0, 10 and 20 years; recovered Ru credited at replacement and at closure), discounted at a metal-specific rate of 0–12% per year, with Fe on the same convention; in the plant-life and catalyst-life configuration of the straight-line model and with no discounting it reproduces that accounting to 10⁻⁹ US dollars per tonne. For the two frontier catalysts the descriptor rates are replaced by measured rate laws: the Temkin–Pyzhev form with the parameters of Dyson and Simon for an industrial magnetite catalyst[46,47] and the rate equation of a Ba–Cs–K-promoted Ru/C catalyst,[40] each with fugacity corrections and the Gillespie–Beattie equilibrium constant.[62] Catalyst requirement is integrated along the bed in plug flow at every process state, and each law is calibrated on its own measured rate; the Ru/C law reproduces the 125 measured points of its source with a mean error of 10%. The fused-iron law was fitted at 149–309 atm and the Ru/C law at 50–100 bar, so the 9 MPa loop extrapolates the former and the higher-pressure optima the latter. The staged converter has three adiabatic beds with interbed cooling, each filled with fused iron (up to 530 °C) or Ru/C (up to 460 °C, the top of its measured range), at 9 MPa with a −20 °C separator and 8% inerts, and is optimized over bed temperatures and catalyst amounts at every financing rate. Literature activity gains are taken only from comparisons with a reference Ru catalyst in the same study and are placed on the same multiplier axis without entering the cost model.

### Bimetallic surface extension

N adsorption energies (0.5 N₂(g) + * → N*) for the 1,796 surfaces of Mamun et al.[37] were retrieved from Catalysis-Hub;[38] two independent retrievals give identical files of 5,696 adsorption sites. The most stable site of each surface defines its terrace descriptor. A linear fit over the 14 pure metals present in both this database and the source of the model's descriptors (slope 0.845, R² = 0.973, RMS 0.22 eV) maps it onto that source's terrace scale, and the 14-metal terrace-to-step relation already used for Fe (step E_N = 1.1547 × terrace E_N − 0.1701 eV) gives the step-site descriptor. The element-anchored bridge adds to this value each element's offset between its frozen step descriptor and its own bridged value, weighted by atomic fraction. Per-state activity is read from the cached response surface of the frozen 14,136-state library; the cost uses the alloy's mole-weighted molar mass and mass-weighted metal price and is minimized over all states within the 90 m³ bed limit. The 15 model metals keep their frozen prices; 21 other elements take USGS annual-average prices per kilogram of contained metal (2025; Hg 2024),[63] with oxide, ore and ferroalloy quotations divided by the metal mass fraction. Tc has no market price and its 101 surfaces are not costed. Surfaces are labelled transition-metal, group 3–5 (Sc, Y, La, Ti, Zr, Hf, V, Nb, Ta) or sp-metal (Al, Zn, Cd, Hg, Ga, In, Tl, Sn, Pb, Bi) and scored per layer. The descriptor-only lower bound for each candidate combines its best-state activity, the smallest reactor term and the cheapest process state. For the surfaces below Fe and the highest-activity surface of each bridge, the counterfactual sets the metal price to the Fe price, and backward design solves for the activity multiplier and the metal price at which the reoptimized cost equals that of Fe; reachability is the lowest cost at any descriptor on the strict scaling range with the surface's molar mass and price.

### Measured ammonia catalysts

Tables 1–6 of the review[36] were extracted page by page (text and page image, two independent passes); disagreements and values absent from the page text were resolved on the page images. Rates are taken per gram of catalyst as printed, converted from mL h⁻¹ g⁻¹, or derived from the printed outlet NH₃ fraction and space velocity; rows printed twice are counted once. Rows were checked against the primary papers, and ten transcription errors in nine rows were replaced by the papers' values. Catalysts with one model metal and a stated metal content enter the chain: 83 of 161 rows. The model's rate per gram of metal is F_CAL·TOF(E_N)/M, with F_CAL the fused-iron plant calibration; each catalyst's measured rate per gram of metal fixes the effective descriptor E_eff at which the model reproduces it at the laboratory condition (H₂/N₂ = 3), on the side of the volcano where its metal lies, with a residual multiplier where the rate exceeds the volcano top. Because the model's NH₃ formation free energy lies 0.064–0.065 eV per NH₃ above experiment at 573–773 K, the laboratory NH₃ fraction (half the outlet value) is entered at the same approach to equilibrium as in the experiment, using the experimental equilibrium constant.[62] The catalyst is then optimized over the 14,136 process states with descriptor E_eff, the molar mass and price of its metal, its own metal content and a bed density of 1,000 kg m⁻³ (fused iron: 71.51 wt% and 2,500 kg m⁻³), without metal recovery unless stated. The primary set contains catalysts measured at 300–500 °C under steady thermal operation with an outlet below 90% of equilibrium; chemical-looping, field- or microwave-assisted rows are reported separately. Bed densities of 500 and 2,500 kg m⁻³, catalysts measured at 5 MPa or above, and a constant multiplier on the metal's own TOF in place of E_eff were tested. The primary-paper set contains 30 papers on thermal ammonia synthesis that compare at least two catalysts or conditions with a stated metal content, 28 of them with Supporting Information; extraction uses the methanol passes with an ammonia schema, and mapping and process optimization follow the review chain. Comparison groups contain the entries of one paper at the same temperature, pressure, H₂/N₂ ratio and space velocity; entries with the same metal, metal content and rate (three significant figures) are counted once, and a catalyst is the fused-iron reference only when it is a commercial fused or wüstite iron catalyst without a stated support or added component. Only entries whose rate, temperature and outlet NH₃ fraction are printed values enter the primary comparison (182 of 623 entries; 139 of them in the 33 groups with at least two catalysts); all entries, plot readings included, give a sensitivity (44 of 124 groups). The paper leaderboard is the rate per gram of catalyst, and the confidence interval is a paper-cluster bootstrap with 10,000 resamples.

### CO₂-to-methanol

The methanol analysis uses a recycle and separation model, calibrated on a published CO₂-to-methanol loop,[34] applied to four Re/TiO₂ states derived from the 1 and 5 wt% catalysts reported by Gothe et al.[19] The reference comparison uses 2% purge. Upstream rankings are reconstructed from space-time yield per gram of Re, single-pass methanol yield and space-time yield per gram of catalyst; the downstream ranking uses net production cost. Re purchase price and catalyst replacement are not included in the reference objective. Purge is varied from 0.5% to 40% over 396 levels, with an additional optimization in which purge is selected separately for each catalyst state. Local economic leverage for space-time yield, conversion and methane suppression is evaluated at the 5 wt% Re, 250 °C state. The process interpretation follows established CO₂-to-methanol assessments in which recycle, compression and separation materially affect plant performance.[20,21] The plant model was checked against published process assessments and pilot-plant data (Supplementary Table 5; Extended Data Fig. 4): at 22–33% per-pass conversion its hydrogen and CO₂ consumption (0.193–0.208 and 1.41–1.47 tonnes per tonne of methanol), carbon efficiency (0.93–0.98) and recycle ratio (2.6–5.4) lie within the reported ranges, and at the operating point and price assumptions of ref. 20 it reproduces its break-even methanol price, production cost plus capital recovery, to within 2.4%. The catalyst-independent stoichiometric H₂ and CO₂ demand is 583.2 euros per tonne on both sides; the catalyst-sensitive terms (feed above stoichiometry, electricity, catalyst replacement and capital) sum to 98.3 against 116.4 euros per tonne in the reference. Setting the loop pressure drop, recycle flow, compression, capital and prices to reference values leaves the space-time-yield disagreement at 14–16 of 40 comparisons.

The measurement Monte Carlo samples the CO₂ conversion and selectivities of each catalyst state from that state's own uncertainty and re-solves the full methanol plant model in each of 5,000 draws (seed 20261005), with every cost parameter held at its reference value. The source reports single integer values, so the uncertainty is built from the source data: the reporting resolution (±0.5 percentage points, with '<1%' sampled over 0–1%), a 5.2% relative uncertainty in conversion derived from the consistency between space-time yield and conversion across the 21 reported runs, and a 0.85-percentage-point exchange between methanol and methane selectivity derived from the 11 runs of a single catalyst. Space-time yield follows conversion and methanol selectivity. Halving or doubling every width retains the economic winner in 99.3% or 78.4% of draws. The 1 wt% Re, 250 °C state uses the Table 3 selectivities of 97% methanol, 1% CO and 1% CH₄.

Counterfactuals remove one candidate difference at a time (methane selectivity moved to methanol; conversion set to the four-state mean) and re-solve the plant at the reference purge. Backward design changes one property of the space-time-yield winner until its cost equals the economic optimum.

### Published methanol comparisons

Every extracted record with CO₂ conversion, methanol selectivity, temperature, pressure and H₂/CO₂ ratio is a candidate operating point; the primary analysis uses entries whose conversion and selectivities are printed numbers, and entries with plot-read values are added as a sensitivity. Groups are formed by the same rule in both cohorts: of the 83 groups that include plot-read values, 20 come from 11 papers without printed values and 23 have fewer than two printed entries, which leaves the 40 groups of the primary analysis. An operating point printed twice in one paper is counted once. Selectivities are closed as in the hand-built case: methanol and methane as reported, entries below the reporting limit set to zero (setting the 14 such entries to their printed bound changes no comparison), and the CO-like residual equal to one minus the methanol and methane selectivities; when methane is not reported, it is one minus the methanol and CO selectivities if CO is reported and zero otherwise, and both bounds of an unreported CO–methane split are tested. Comparison groups contain the entries of one paper at the same pressure, H₂/CO₂ ratio and space velocity. One paper ranks its catalysts per gram of Re;[19] on that basis its disagreement is the same catalyst at another temperature, so the other-catalyst and other-temperature counts become 15 of 32 and 13 of 22 in the inert-limited loop (9 of 32 and 6 of 22 without the limit), while the totals are unchanged. A group contains at least two catalysts, a temperature series of one catalyst, or both; a disagreement counts as another catalyst when the plant-cost leader is a different catalyst and as another temperature when it is the paper's leader at a different temperature. Entries whose feed adds water or CO to CO₂ and H₂ are excluded, because the plant model takes a dry CO₂/H₂ make-up. Space-time yield per gram of catalyst defines the paper leaderboard: the printed value when every entry of a group prints it and the ratio of printed to conversion-derived productivity varies by less than a factor of 3 within the group, otherwise the value derived from the mass space velocity, otherwise from the volumetric space velocity with a bulk density of 1.0 g mL⁻¹ (0.5 and 2.0 g mL⁻¹ tested). The plant leaderboard is the net production cost at each entry's own temperature, pressure and H₂/CO₂ ratio, with catalyst mass from the group's productivity basis. Recycled CO is converted per pass to the smallest extent that keeps the reactor outlet at or below both reverse-water-gas-shift and CO-hydrogenation equilibrium, the single-pass CO₂ conversion is capped at the loop's CO₂-hydrogenation equilibrium, and the purge is optimized over 0.5–40% among the levels at which the reactor-inlet content of species other than H₂ and CO₂ (CO, methane and N₂) stays at or below 6.86%, the cost model's limit for the loop that anchors it (6.46% CO and N₂ in its stream table, 6.78% with methanol and water[34]). CO counts toward this limit because, at the cost-optimal purge, recycled CO is converted little further and dilutes the CO₂ and H₂ feed as N₂ does; it still reacts up to the equilibrium bound. The loop without the limit (purge extended to 10⁻⁴% as a check), limits of 4–30%, spanning the 4.4–9.8% of CO, methane and N₂ (4.9–10.2% of all species other than H₂ and CO₂) in four published loop designs, a limit on methane and N₂ alone, and recycled CO treated as inert are reported as sensitivities (Extended Data Fig. 8; Supplementary Note 3). A disagreement is near-infeasible when the paper leader's cost in the limited loop exceeds twice its cost without the limit or the regret exceeds 100%. Variants use inert CO, 2% purge, single-pass yield, conversion or selectivity as the paper leaderboard, all entries including plot readings, entries whose reported product selectivities sum to at least 95%, and the comparisons without the paper that supplies most groups. In the catalyst-inventory variant each entry may load more catalyst, raising its conversion along an approach-to-equilibrium curve calibrated on its measured point to at most 95% of equilibrium at constant selectivity. Pressure-dependent condensation enters both leaderboards of a group equally because a group shares one pressure. The lower bound used to allocate the full plant optimization evaluates the same economics at each purge level on lower-bound loop flows that hold for any per-pass conversion of recycled CO between 0 and 1: reactor-inlet CO₂ of at least 1/[X(S_MeOH + (1 − p)S_CO)] per mole of methanol, three moles of H₂ per mole of methanol and four per mole of methane, the loop methane and nitrogen that the purge fraction p sets, and at least two moles of crude liquid; every equipment term rises with its flow, so the minimum over purge levels bounds the plant cost from below. Within each group the paper's leader is evaluated in full, then the remaining candidates in increasing order of the bound until the next bound exceeds the best cost found. Sampling uncertainty is a paper-cluster bootstrap with 10,000 resamples. Measurement uncertainty re-draws every entry 2,000 times with the Re/TiO₂ basis above (5.2% relative conversion error, a 0.85-percentage-point methanol–methane selectivity exchange and, for printed space-time yields, an independent 5.2% relative error), at one, two and four times these widths, and rebuilds both leaderboards from the same draw. Plant costs of perturbed entries come from an additive per-coordinate response surface built from exact model runs, with draws outside its grid run exactly; 7 of 600 group verdicts (4 of 200 at the reported noise level) in the inert-limited loop and 1 of 600 without the limit differ from exact runs of complete replicates. The catalyst-price variant prices each catalyst from its extracted composition, with metal contents at the model and 2025 commodity prices[63] plus a base cost that gives commercial Cu/ZnO/Al₂O₃ 18.1 euros per kilogram,[34] and charges replacement per tonne of inventory.

### Au/TiO₂ rank-preservation control

The Au/TiO₂ control compares 2–6 nm particles under a common operating condition and process mapping. The reference rate, Au loading and dispersion are anchored to Janssens et al.,[24] and the particle-size dependence is taken from the 4.5 wt% Au/TiO₂ series reported by Overbury et al.[25] The broader physical basis for low-temperature Au/TiO₂ activity and particle-size effects follows the established literature.[22,23] Rank preservation is evaluated in 10,000 parameter samples drawn from ranges reported in these studies. An additional analysis introduces candidate-specific kinetic variation and independently optimizes temperature and O₂/CO ratio within the specified ranges derived from the literature.

### Adaptive Catalyst Screening Agent

ACSA fetches each paper through the institutional library service, converts it to page-marked text and page images, and extracts every catalyst entry into a fixed schema in which each number carries its value, unit, qualifier, location and page. Extraction uses one strict-JSON language-model call per paper and pass (gpt-5.5, medium reasoning effort): a main pass over text and page images, a figure pass that digitizes every catalyst-performance point in the main-text figures, and passes over the text, tables and figures of the Supporting Information. A printed main-text value outranks a printed Supporting-Information value, which outranks a plot reading. Units are normalized to the model basis. Prompts contain only the paper itself.

Accuracy is scored against two curated methanol datasets[32,33] and the 21 Re/TiO₂ entries of the hand-built case. Entries are matched by paper and catalyst name, or, for the dataset that lists compositions instead of names,[33] by paper, operating conditions and elemental composition, never by the values being scored; a pair with an equally admissible alternative partner counts for recall but not for value accuracy. Strict tolerances are ±1 K for temperature, ±1% for pressure and space-time yield, ±0.05 for the H₂/CO₂ ratio and ±0.5 percentage points for conversion and selectivity. Every disagreement between extracted and curated values was checked against the paper. Curated values that the paper contradicts fall into 26 root causes, one mechanism in one source paper each (45 rules, 169 cells and 8 rows across the two methanol datasets and the ammonia review), with a further 24 space-time-yield cells that one dataset derives from an erroneous input; that dataset computes space-time yield from its yield and space velocity, so a printed space-time yield that differs from the derivation is not counted as an error (Supplementary Table 6). Curated values are corrected to the printed value before scoring, and both raw and corrected scores are reported. Of the extracted entries without a curated partner, 150 of 179 in the first 18 papers and a random sample of 87 in the 31 papers added later were checked against the source. A random sample of 100 extracted values from 10 papers was checked by the authors against the papers and Supporting Information: 91 are printed or equal after unit conversion, 6 are H₂/CO₂ ratios derived from printed feed compositions, and 3 match after a corrected locator, a restored upper-bound qualifier or a stated metric name; none contradicts the source.

Before new candidates are scored, ACSA runs the hand-built inputs of all three systems, and the extracted Re/TiO₂ and Au/TiO₂ inputs, through the same code path and requires the frozen results to be reproduced; the batch runs do not start otherwise. Every extracted Au/TiO₂ input must also equal the hand-built value, because the dispersion enters the chain only as a ratio and cannot be checked through the outputs. A value that the source prints twice with different numbers is accepted in either form: the Au loading appears as 4.41 wt% in the text and 4.40 wt% in Table 1 of the source paper,[24] which changes the required Au mass by 0.2% and no ranking quantity. Candidates are then taken through the forward chain; the descriptor-only lower bound decides which ammonia candidates need the full process optimization, the purge-wise plant bound decides which methanol candidates need the equilibrium-constrained purge optimization, and all candidates were additionally evaluated in full to verify both bounds. The counterfactual and backward calculations are run on the leading candidates only.
