# Laboratory catalyst leaders are often not the plant-level optimum

## Abstract

Catalysts are ranked by descriptors or laboratory performance, but a plant selects the catalyst with the lowest production cost after optimizing its operation. We couple catalyst-specific kinetics to candidate-specific process optimization and a catalyst-responsive cost model, and invert the model to find the catalyst properties that cost parity requires. In ammonia synthesis the intrinsic order Ru > Os > Fe becomes Fe > Ru > Os through the Ru metal price alone: priced as iron, Ru would remain the least-cost catalyst. With measured rate laws, each used only at the pressures it was fitted on, bulk Ru would need a 94% lower price or a 16-fold activity gain to match fused iron, whereas a commercial supported Ru/C catalyst with metal recovery reaches cost parity with it at 100 bar. An agent that extracts published data and runs every reported catalyst through the same chain shows how often the laboratory leader is displaced: with each catalyst at its best measured operating point, the space-time-yield leader is not the plant-level optimum in 26 of 63 methanol comparisons (41%), nor is the catalyst with the highest rate in 12 of 44 ammonia comparisons (27%). A leader changes when a catalyst property alters a downstream operating requirement, such as purge or metal inventory, and is kept when the property maps monotonically onto catalyst requirement.

## Introduction

Computational catalyst discovery ranks candidate materials by adsorption energies, reactivity descriptors, scaling relations, volcano plots and surrogate models,[1–6] and descriptor-based microkinetic and multiscale models connect these quantities to rates and operating conditions.[6–8] Experimental studies rank catalysts by laboratory activity, selectivity and space-time yield. Both rankings stand in for a decision that is made at the scale of a plant, where catalyst properties act through reactor operation, separation, recycle and cost. Descriptors have been linked to reactor design and catalyst selection for ammonia synthesis,[9] catalyst and process design have been formulated as coupled problems,[10,11] and laboratory rankings are known to depend on the test environment.[12] How often the leading catalyst survives candidate-specific process optimization and costing has not been measured across a field.

The identity of the leader is what a plant acts on. A screen can stay well correlated across many candidates while the material that a plant would select changes, and the cost of a catalyst per kilogram can be a poor guide to its cost per unit product once stability and production scale are included.[13] In ammonia synthesis, process studies of Fe and Ru catalysts find that the preferred catalyst and pressure depend on plant scale and loop configuration.[14,15]

Here we take catalyst properties forward through kinetics, candidate-specific process optimization and a catalyst-responsive cost model, and backward from an economic target to the catalyst property it requires, testing whether that property lies within reach of the underlying scaling relations.[10,11,16] We distinguish three optima. The atomic optimum is set by a descriptor and the intrinsic turnover frequency; the laboratory optimum is the leader of a published comparison; the plant-level optimum is the catalyst with the lowest cost once its operation has been optimized under stated process and economic conditions (Methods). Three systems cover the steps between them (Fig. 1a). Ammonia synthesis, in which intrinsic activity and metal price set the catalyst inventory and operating conditions,[5,17,18] runs from the atomic to the plant-level optimum. CO₂ hydrogenation to methanol, in which selectivity sets reactant loss, purge, recycle and compression,[19–21] runs from the laboratory to the plant-level optimum. Au/TiO₂ CO oxidation, in which particle size maps monotonically onto catalyst requirement,[22–25] runs from the laboratory optimum to the catalyst requirement and serves as the control. Language-model agents can now read the chemical literature and operate computational tools.[26–30] We use one, the Adaptive Catalyst Screening Agent (ACSA), to run the same chain on every catalyst that published papers report. This turns statements about a few chosen catalysts into a measurement over a field: within the comparisons that methanol and ammonia papers make themselves, how often is the catalyst ranked first in the laboratory not the plant-level optimum?

## Results

### Process economics reverse the leading ammonia ranking

The 15-metal ammonia screen ranks Ru, Os and Fe first, second and third in intrinsic activity (Fig. 1c), in line with the established activity landscape of transition-metal ammonia synthesis.[17,18] When the operating conditions are optimized separately for each catalyst over 14,510 process states and the catalyst-dependent costs are evaluated (Fig. 1a,d,e), the order becomes Fe, Ru and Os, at 11.52, 16.63 and 19.75 US dollars per tonne of NH₃ (Fig. 1b,h). Across all 15 metals the Spearman correlation between the two rankings remains 0.911, whereas among the three leaders ρ = −0.50 (Fig. 1f,g): the screen stays correlated while the selected catalyst changes. Selecting Ru instead of Fe would raise the catalyst-dependent cost by 44.4%, the normalized decision regret. The difference of 5.12 US dollars per tonne is a small part of the cost of ammonia from state-of-the-art gas-based plants, about 160 US dollars per tonne,[31] but it is the part that catalyst selection controls, 1.77 million US dollars per year for a 1,000 t/d plant (Supplementary Note 1). The reversal has a single cause, the price of Ru, examined below.

### Published laboratory leaders are often not the plant-cost leaders

A reversal among three chosen metals does not show how often the leader changes in practice. ACSA applies the same analysis, with the kinetic, process and economic models unchanged, to every candidate that publications or databases provide (Fig. 2a). It reads each paper and its Supporting Information and records every catalyst entry with the table, figure and page each value comes from; before it scores a new candidate it must reproduce the frozen results of all three hand-built cases, and a lower bound on cost decides which candidates need the full process optimization. Scored on 50 methanol papers (Supplementary Table 1) against two curated datasets,[32,33] the agent recovers 719 of the 741 curated entries (97%). It transcribes printed values almost without error: 44 of 44 CO₂ conversions and 44 of 44 methanol selectivities in main-text tables, and 129 of 130 conversions, 124 of 129 selectivities and 118 of 128 space-time yields in the Supporting Information; most remaining errors are plot readings (Extended Data Fig. 1; Supplementary Note 2). None of 100 randomly sampled values that the authors checked against the papers contradicts its source (Methods). The self-check reproduces the 21 Re/TiO₂ entries to 2.3 × 10⁻¹³ euros per tonne and every pure-metal ammonia cost to 10⁻⁹ US dollars per tonne. In methanol, without the inlet limit, the lower bound spares the full plant optimization for 394 of 632 candidates (62.3%) and still finds the plant-cost leader of all 81 comparisons, at 38% of the compute (Extended Data Fig. 2).

Methanol papers rank catalysts by laboratory performance, most often by space-time yield. Two extractions with the same pipeline supply the comparisons: of the 50 papers above, 33 print the conversion, selectivity, temperature, pressure and H₂/CO₂ ratio of 351 operating points, and of 40 later papers, published from November 2025 to October 2026 and taken in the rank order of a fixed literature search, 28 print those of 348 (Supplementary Tables 1 and 1b; Methods). Entries of one paper tested at the same pressure, H₂/CO₂ ratio and space velocity form the comparison the paper itself makes: 81 comparisons with 632 entries in 50 papers. Each entry is taken through the methanol recycle–economics model at its own conditions, with its single-pass conversion held at the laboratory value and capped at the loop's CO₂-hydrogenation equilibrium, and with recycled CO converted per pass by the least amount that keeps the reactor outlet at or below reverse-water-gas-shift and CO-hydrogenation equilibrium. As in a working loop, CO, methane and N₂ together may make up at most 6.86% of the reactor inlet, the limit of the loop that anchors the cost model (6.46% CO and N₂ in that loop's stream table[34]), and the purge is optimized within that limit. CO counts toward the limit because recycled CO dilutes the CO₂ and H₂ feed as N₂ does.

A plant chooses the operating point of each catalyst, so each catalyst is compared at the best point at which it was measured: its highest space-time yield for the laboratory ranking and its lowest plant cost for the plant ranking. What counts as a different catalyst (another composition, loading of the active component, support or synthesis) was fixed before the comparisons were counted (Methods). In 26 of the 63 comparisons that contain at least two catalysts (41%; 95% confidence interval 29–53% from resampling papers), the catalyst with the highest space-time yield is not the catalyst with the lowest plant cost (Fig. 2b). These 26 comparisons come from 21 papers; choosing the laboratory leader costs more than 5% extra in 19 of them and more than 10% in 13. Counting samples that differ only in synthesis as one catalyst gives 22 of 52, 15 above 5%. Over every operating point of every catalyst, the space-time-yield leader is not the plant-cost leader in 50 of 81 comparisons (62%; 49–72%), 42 of them above 5% and spread over 32 of the 50 papers, and 1,992 of 5,283 pairwise orderings (38%) invert. Besides the 26 changes of catalyst, these 50 comprise 13 comparisons in which one catalyst leads both rankings at different operating points and 11 comparisons of a single catalyst. The laboratory's best temperature is usually not the plant's: of 112 catalysts measured at two or more temperatures, 82 reach their lowest plant cost at a temperature other than that of their highest space-time yield, 75 of them at a lower one (median 50 °C lower), and 74 of these 82 would cost more than 5% extra at their highest-yield temperature.

The change of catalyst does not hinge on how much catalyst a plant would load. When each catalyst may load more catalyst and raise its conversion towards equilibrium, 21 of the 63 comparisons still change catalyst, 10 of them above 5%, and the total falls from 50 to 46 of 81 (36 above 5%). The 50 disagreements are neither a sampling nor a measurement artefact (Extended Data Fig. 3). Re-measuring every entry 2,000 times with the conversion and selectivity uncertainty derived for the Re/TiO₂ states keeps 42 of them in at least 90% of re-measurements, whereas measurement noise alone changes a paper's space-time-yield leader in 9 comparisons (95% range 5–14). In 26 of the 50 the paper leader meets the inlet limit only with a large purge, at more than twice its cost without the limit; the other 24, 17 of them above 5%, involve no such jump (Supplementary Note 3). The total holds without the study that supplies most comparisons (43 of 73, 37 above 5%), when plot-read entries are added (101 of 160 comparisons), for entries whose reported products close to methanol, CO and methane (30 of 50), with unreported CO and methane selectivities set to either bound (48–55 of 81), with recycled CO treated as inert (56 of 81) or converted per pass up to CO-hydrogenation equilibrium (50 of 81, 35 above 5%) and when each catalyst is priced from its composition (47 of 81). It holds for inlet limits of 5–10% (42–50 of 81, 32–46 above 5%) and weakens towards 15% (41 and 34 of 81 at 12% and 15%, 26 and 24 above 5%; Supplementary Note 3). Without any inlet limit, which lets a diluted feed react as in the laboratory, the leaders differ in 25 of 81 comparisons (31%), 10 of them above 5%; limiting methane and N₂ alone and leaving CO free gives 28 of 81, 11 above 5%, so the rise from 25 to 50 comes from CO build-up. The plant model reproduces the hydrogen and CO₂ consumption, carbon efficiency and recycle ratio of published plant assessments and, on the same basis, the break-even methanol price of one of them to within 2.4% (Extended Data Fig. 4).

The plant ranking follows from what each by-product does in a recycle loop (Fig. 2d,e). Recycled CO is converted per pass only as far as the shift and CO-hydrogenation equilibria require; at the cost-optimal purge it is barely converted further and accumulates, so that without the inlet limit the reactor feed carries a median 8.8% CO (90th percentile 26%), against 1.3–4.7% in published loop designs. Holding the feed to the limit costs purge, and the purge a catalyst needs grows with the CO and methane it makes. Under the limit the plant-cost leader is more selective to methanol than the paper leader in 40 of the 50 disagreements and makes less CO in 39, whereas without the limit it runs at higher single-pass conversion in 11 of 25. InNi₃C₀.₅ on four supports at 300 °C and 4 MPa (Supplementary Table 1) shows the trade: on m-ZrO₂ it gives the highest space-time yield (8.5% conversion, 88% methanol selectivity, 12% CO) but needs a 4.0% purge and costs 1,289 euros per tonne, against 1,138 on SiO₂ (2.2% conversion, 95% selectivity, 5% CO) at 0.6% purge; without the limit the order is reversed, 919 against 1,101. No single laboratory metric replaces the plant model: ranking by conversion disagrees more often than ranking by space-time yield (54 of 81) and ranking by methanol selectivity in 30 of 81; without the limit selectivity is the worst proxy (61 of 81). A high-pressure Cu/Al₂O₃ study that supplies 8 of the 81 comparisons[35] gives the largest regrets without the limit.

Ammonia papers rank catalysts by rate. ACSA extracted 52 primary ammonia-synthesis papers with the same pipeline (Supplementary Tables 2 and 2b; Methods). Each catalyst's measured rate is inverted into an effective descriptor on its metal's side of the volcano, and the catalyst is optimized over the full process library with its own metal content. Catalysts of one paper with printed rate, temperature and outlet, tested at the same temperature, pressure, H₂/N₂ ratio and space velocity, form 44 comparisons in 31 papers. The catalyst with the highest rate per gram of catalyst is not the plant-cost leader in 12 of them (27%; 95% confidence interval 14–42% from resampling papers), in 10 of 31 papers, 8 with a cost gap above 5% and a median regret of 13% where the leaders differ (Fig. 2c). In 3 comparisons a catalyst of another metal is the plant-cost leader, in all 3 an Fe catalyst on the same support with the lower rate (Fig. 2f), and in 5 a commercial fused-iron catalyst that the paper includes as a reference is the plant-cost leader; without these references the leaders differ in 8 of 44. Across the 73 catalysts tabulated in a review of ammonia-synthesis catalysts,[36] laboratory rate and plant cost are uncorrelated (Spearman ρ = 0.07), and only two of them, a Cs- and Ba-promoted Ru catalyst and a BaH₂-promoted Fe catalyst, undercut the fused-iron benchmark without metal recovery, by 4% and 3% (Extended Data Fig. 5; Supplementary Note 4).

### Selectivity, recycle and purge reshape the methanol ranking

Four Re/TiO₂ catalyst–temperature states show the mechanism behind the methanol field result (Fig. 3a,b).[19] By space-time yield per gram of Re the order is 1 wt% Re at 250 °C, 1 wt% Re at 200 °C, 5 wt% Re at 200 °C and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C. After the recycle and separation model at 2% purge, the economic order becomes 5 wt% Re at 200 °C, 1 wt% Re at 250 °C, 1 wt% Re at 200 °C and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C, with net production costs of 943, 962, 967 and 1,258 euros per tonne (Fig. 3c). Within the 6.86% inlet limit, at each state's optimal purge, the order becomes 1 wt% Re at 200 °C, 1 wt% Re at 250 °C, 5 wt% Re at 200 °C and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C (921, 982, 997 and 1,765 euros per tonne), and the space-time-yield winner stays second, now at 6.6% regret. At 2% purge, selecting the space-time-yield winner costs 1.93% more, and two of six pairwise orderings invert (Fig. 3e). Methane suppression has a local economic leverage of 0.376, against 0.0588 for single-pass conversion and 0.00289 for space-time yield (Fig. 3a,g), because methane consumes reactants and accumulates in the loop, raising purge, recycle and compression.[20,21] No purge level between 0.5% and 40% makes the space-time-yield winner the least-cost state (Fig. 3f).

Counterfactual and backward calculations locate the cause. Moving every state's methane selectivity into methanol reverses the correlation to ρ = −0.60, whereas equalizing conversion raises it to +1.00. For the space-time-yield winner, no increase in space-time yield reaches parity: unlimited productivity still costs 955 euros per tonne against 943. Raising its single-pass conversion from 0.23 to 0.279, inside the range measured on the same catalyst family, or reaching complete methanol selectivity does. When each state's own measurement uncertainty is propagated through the plant model, the economic winner keeps first place in 91.2% of 5,000 draws (Fig. 3d; Supplementary Note 5).

### Outside nitride formers and sp metals, surfaces that undercut Fe pair a cheap 3d metal with a group-6 metal

To test the Fe preference on a much larger candidate set, ACSA took the 1,796 bimetallic and pure-metal surfaces of a high-throughput adsorption database[37,38] through the ammonia chain (Methods). Among the 372 surfaces that contain only transition metals outside groups 3–5, 137 satisfy the 90 m³ bed-volume limit. Eight undercut Fe when the database is bridged linearly onto the model's descriptor and nine under an element-anchored bridge; every one of them pairs Fe, Co, Ni or Cu with Cr, Mo or W (Extended Data Fig. 6), the combination that descriptor interpolation has identified for ammonia synthesis.[39] Surfaces with group 3–5 elements, which form stable bulk nitrides, and with sp metals, which lie outside the transition-metal scaling relations of the activity model, are scored as separate layers (Supplementary Table 3). The most active surface ranks 12th economically (Ni₃Re, 28.0% regret) and at its own price would need a 5.5-fold activity increase to reach Fe. The descriptor-only lower bound excludes 1,399 of the 1,695 priced surfaces (82.5%) from the full optimization without excluding any that undercut Fe (Supplementary Note 6).

### What Ru must cost to match fused iron

The ammonia reversal is caused by the price of Ru. Converting turnover frequency into the metal a plant must hold preserves the intrinsic order; charging that metal at its price over a 10-year life gives Fe < Ru < Os, with annualized metal replacement of 0.204 US dollars per tonne for Fe against 4.09 for Ru, before any reactor, compression, refrigeration or equipment cost is added. Priced as iron, at 8 US dollars per kilogram, and reoptimized, Ru is the least-cost catalyst (10.24 against 11.52 US dollars per tonne). A price sweep with reoptimization at every price places Fe–Ru parity at 404 US dollars per kilogram (Fig. 4d), 99% below the Ru price of the model. Process optimization does not close the gap but moves it into compression: Ru moves to about 450 °C and 220 bar, where a small bed holds little metal, while Fe operates near 425 °C and 105 bar (Fig. 4b,c). The 5.12 US dollars per tonne at the two optima comes mainly from fresh-feed compression (+3.988), metal inventory (+1.694) and compressor capital (+1.024), partly offset by vessel, recycle-compression and reactor terms (Fig. 4a,e).

The order holds across the uncertainty of the prices and of the model itself. In 5,000 draws of metal prices, capital cost, electricity price and catalyst lifetime, each with full reoptimization, Fe is cheaper in every draw, with a minimum difference of 1.99 US dollars per tonne (Fig. 4h); descriptor uncertainty matters more, with Fe first in 65.3% of 1,000 descriptor samples and Ru in 34.7%, in most of the latter because the Fe bed exceeds the 90 m³ limit (Fig. 4f,g; Supplementary Note 7). Against 64 values from industrial ammonia loops and converters, the model deviates most in converter-inlet NH₃ (typically 63% low), compressor power (42% low) and the bed volume of cost-optimal converter designs (63% low), and within tolerance for every flow, space velocity and bed volume at plant conditions (typical deviations 16–34%; Supplementary Table 4). Applying each deviation, in either direction and at its typical or its largest size, to the model component that produces it and reoptimizing both catalysts never puts Ru ahead of Fe, with a smallest margin of 1.34 US dollars per tonne; nor does any combination of directions with all five components at their typical deviation (smallest margin 2.21; Supplementary Note 7).

A real Ru catalyst holds far less metal than the benchmark bed. A promoted Ru/C catalyst exposes 11% of its Ru atoms,[40] against fewer than 1% of the Fe atoms in reduced fused iron;[41] the commercial catalyst carries about 8 wt% Ru,[42] and 90–95.55% of the Ru in spent promoted Ru/C ammonia catalysts is recovered.[43,44] Dispersion and recovery divide the metal charge as a lower price would (Fig. 4d): on its own bed with recovery, supported Ru costs 11.46–12.05 US dollars per tonne, from 0.05 below to 0.53 above Fe, and in a KAAP-type loop at 9 MPa with a −20 °C refrigerated condenser,[45] 16.35–16.44 against 16.98 for fused iron (Table 1). Sampling dispersion, recovery and bed density turns the decision into a probability: Ru is cheaper in 79.3% of draws for a commercial Ru/C bed (5–10 wt% Ru, 430–550 kg m⁻³), whereas Fe stays cheaper in 93.0% with the activities and Ru contents of the 54 measured Ru catalysts (Extended Data Fig. 7).

The descriptor kinetics are furthest from measurement for the two catalysts at this frontier: at 90 bar, 400 °C and 2% ammonia they give Ru apparent orders of +2.4 in H₂ and −1.9 in NH₃, against +0.3 and −0.4 for promoted Ru/C, and fused iron −0.1 in NH₃ against −0.9 measured. We therefore replaced the rate laws of this pair with measured ones: a law fitted to commercial fused-iron data from 1 to 324 bar, with the activity of the industrial magnetite catalyst whose data Dyson and Simon fitted,[46,47] and the kinetics of the promoted Ru/C catalyst, fitted at 50–100 bar.[40] Each law is integrated along the bed, and each catalyst is optimized only at the pressures its law was fitted on (Methods). Fused iron then costs 15.31 US dollars per tonne at 450 °C and 110 bar and Ru in the benchmark formulation 45.87 at 100 bar; to match fused iron, bulk Ru would need a 16-fold activity gain (16–45-fold across bed-sizing and calibration variants) or a 94% lower price, 3,371 US dollars per kilogram. Supported Ru/C with 95.55% recovery has its optimum at 100 bar and is at cost parity with fused iron: it costs 3.83 US dollars per tonne less than Fe when its Ru inventory carries no financing charge and 2.13 more when the inventory is financed over the 25-year plant life at the 12% per year applied to equipment, and it is the cheaper catalyst at any financing rate below 6.5% per year. In the KAAP-type loop, where both rate laws are inside their fitted ranges, the differences are 1.21 less and 1.95 more, with break-even at 4.6% per year. Benchmark deviations of typical size shift the difference at 12% per year by −1.6 to +3.0 US dollars per tonne (Supplementary Table 4), so within the accuracy of the model the two catalysts tie.

With both catalysts available to a three-bed converter at 9 MPa, the least-cost design puts fused iron in the first bed and Ru/C downstream for Ru financing rates up to 5.5% per year, the bed order of the Kellogg Advanced Ammonia Process converter[45] and of published Fe + Ru multibed designs;[48] above that rate the converter is all fused iron, and a converter with Ru/C first is never optimal. Fused iron takes the first, hottest bed because Ru/C is held to 460 °C, the top of its measured range; Ru/C takes the downstream beds because ammonia inhibits fused iron more strongly, so that the Ru/C rate advantage per kilogram grows from 9-fold at 2% to 62-fold at 14% NH₃. The 1.35–5.10% per year that one refiner disclosed for 32 platinum-group catalyst-metal leases falls inside this window (Supplementary Note 7).

### Backward design separates the required catalyst-property region from physical reachability

Multiplying Ru intrinsic activity by a factor α and reoptimizing the process at each value, Ru reaches the Fe cost only after a 115-fold increase; the 5th–95th percentile requirement under economic uncertainty is 44–262-fold (Fig. 5a). The scaling relation allows much less: at most 1.02-fold at 673 K and 1.58-fold across all process conditions, and the lowest strict-scaling Ru cost, 16.45 US dollars per tonne at *E*_N = −1.180 eV, remains above the Fe cost (Fig. 5b). Improving Ru activity along the present scaling relation cannot make Ru competitive.

Measured catalysts show where gains of this size come from (Fig. 5a, bottom). Ba promotion of Ru/C raises the turnover frequency per surface Ru 75-fold and Cs promotion 65-fold,[49] Ba promotion of Ru/BN more than 100-fold at 5 MPa[50] and Cs promotion of Ru/MgO more than 134-fold;[51] all four lie within the parity band, and the MgO-supported Cs gain exceeds the central requirement, whereas electride and oxyhydride supports give about tenfold[52,53] and confinement inside carbon nanotubes 0.5-fold.[54] These gains are measured against unpromoted Ru. The Ru/C catalyst of the measured kinetics already carries Ba, Cs and K and in the benchmark bed still needs the 16-fold gain found above; the remaining gap lies in the cost of the metal, which dispersion and recovery close (Table 1).

Lifecycle variables move the target before reachability is tested. Allowing Ru recovery and catalyst lifetime to improve together removes the activity requirement: at 99% recovery, a 10-year lifetime needs at most a 1.29-fold gain and a 20-year lifetime none (Fig. 5c). On the strict *E*_N scaling manifold across all 14,510 process states, parity is controlled by the catalyst-loss factor \(q=(1-r)/L\), with \(q^*=8.25\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{y}^{-1}\). At a 20-year lifetime and 99% recovery the best strict-scaling state costs 11.20 US dollars per tonne, 0.32 below Fe, and parity requires 98.35% recovery at 20 years or a 12.12-year lifetime at 99% recovery (Fig. 5d). Lifecycle engineering reaches parity inside the present scaling-constrained design space, where activity alone cannot (Supplementary Note 8).

### A monotonic catalyst-to-process mapping preserves the ranking

Process evaluation does not change every ranking. In Au/TiO₂ CO oxidation, with the rate anchor of Janssens et al.[24] and the particle-size dependence of Overbury et al.,[25] candidates differ only in particle size, from 2 to 6 nm, under one operating condition and process model. Intrinsic activity and required catalyst mass keep the order 2 > 3 > 4 > 5 > 6 nm in all 10,000 parameter samples drawn from the ranges reported in the literature (Fig. 6b–d), and in 92.2% when candidate-specific kinetic and operating variations are introduced over 273–293 K (Fig. 6e).

The three systems therefore show both outcomes of cross-scale propagation (Fig. 6a,f). The 2 nm Au particles have 8.1 times the mass activity of 6 nm particles and need 8.1 times less catalyst. In methanol, a 3.6-fold space-time-yield advantage comes with a 1.9% higher production cost; in ammonia, Ru has 607 times the intrinsic turnover frequency of Fe and a 44% higher catalyst-dependent cost. The realized pairwise transfer index χ_AB = ln(J_A/J_B)/ln(s_A/s_B), where s_A > s_B is the higher-is-better upstream property and J the lower-is-better plant objective, puts these outcomes on one scale: χ ≈ −1.00 for Au, where catalyst requirement scales inversely with activity, against +0.015 for methanol and +0.057 for ammonia, where the upstream leader costs more. χ records an outcome once the plant model has been evaluated; the mechanism comes from the process and cost analyses above, metal inventory, price and optimized operation in ammonia and selectivity-dependent reactant loss, purge and recycle in methanol (Fig. 6a; Supplementary Note 9).

## Discussion

A catalyst ranking carries over from one optimum to the next only as far as the upstream advantage reaches the plant objective. Agreement across a screen does not settle the choice: in ammonia the 15-metal ranking stays correlated while the leading order Ru > Os > Fe becomes Fe > Ru > Os. What intervenes differs at each step. Between the atomic and the plant-level optimum in ammonia it is the price of the metal held in the bed, which process optimization turns into compression as Ru moves to high pressure and a small bed; between the laboratory and the plant-level optimum in methanol it is the reactant lost to purge; between the laboratory optimum and the catalyst requirement in Au/TiO₂ nothing intervenes, and the ranking is kept.

The field measurement shows that changes of leader are common. With each catalyst at its best measured operating point, the catalyst a methanol paper ranks first by space-time yield is not the plant-level optimum in 41% of comparisons between catalysts, and the ammonia catalyst with the highest rate is not in 27%. The causes are those of the hand-built cases: in methanol, CO and methane accumulate in the loop and set purge and recycle, so that ranking by conversion or by selectivity fails as well; in ammonia, iron catalysts tested alongside more active ruthenium catalysts are cheaper to operate. The operating point carries its own lesson for experimental work: the temperature at which a catalyst performs best in the laboratory is usually not where a plant would run it, and in most cases the plant would run it cooler. A laboratory comparison can report the plant-level consequence of its leader alongside its rate or yield, and screening can target the catalyst property with enough downstream leverage to change the decision.

For ammonia, the decision turns on what Ru costs. Bulk Ru would need a 94% lower price, or an activity gain (16-fold with measured kinetics, 115-fold with the descriptor model) that its scaling relation does not allow. Metal economy does what activity cannot: a supported Ru/C catalyst with metal recovery is at cost parity with fused iron at 100 bar and is the cheaper catalyst whenever its Ru is financed below 6.5% per year, 4.6% in the KAAP-type loop; at the lease rates disclosed for platinum-group catalyst metals, the same economics place Ru/C downstream of fused iron, the arrangement of the Kellogg Advanced Ammonia Process. Recovery combined with a longer catalyst life also reaches parity on the scaling relation itself. Independent process analyses describe the same trade-off between lower-pressure operation, ammonia-separation refrigeration and the price, loading, lifetime and recycling of Ru.[15,55,56]

ACSA runs the same scientific models on every candidate; what it adds is scale, with 97% recall, 92–100% of printed values transcribed correctly and cost bounds that spare most full optimizations without losing a leader. Kinetic, process and economic models of higher fidelity, and catalyst physics specific to a formulation, can replace the present ones without changing the decision logic, so that every comparison a catalysis paper reports can be taken to its plant-level consequence.

## Methods

### Multiscale decision analysis

Each catalytic system is evaluated with a process-level objective matched to the catalyst-to-process pathway under study. For ammonia, the economic objective contains the terms that respond directly to catalyst identity and catalytic performance: catalyst inventory and replacement, reactor requirements, compression, refrigeration and pressure-dependent equipment. Common upstream H₂/N₂ supply contributions are held fixed so that they do not dilute the catalyst-dependent decision signal. The methanol model evaluates net production cost through its recycle and separation process, while the Au/TiO₂ control maps particle-size-dependent activity to required catalyst mass under a common process model. The plant-level optimum of a set of catalysts is the catalyst with the lowest value of its system's objective under the stated process and economic conditions, each catalyst at the operating point that minimizes the objective: over the full process library for descriptor-based and measured ammonia catalysts, within the fitted pressure range for a measured rate law and over its measured operating points for a published methanol catalyst. The plant-cost leader of a published comparison is its plant-level optimum. Cross-system comparison is made at the level of ranking propagation and coupling topology, not by equating the absolute economic objectives of different reactions.

Within each system, rankings before and after process evaluation are compared using Spearman and Kendall correlations, pairwise inversions, top-rank identity and normalized decision regret. For a lower-is-better downstream objective J, regret is defined as R = (J_upstream-winner - J_downstream-winner) / J_downstream-winner. Analyses of the leading candidates are reported separately from full-set statistics so that changes at the top of the ranking are not obscured by agreement among weaker candidates. Backward design varies a selected catalyst property, reoptimizes the process and identifies the value required for economic parity. This value is then compared with the range accessible under the corresponding catalyst-property relation or scaling relation. Perturbation tests change one predefined input while preserving the other model assumptions and repeating the same downstream optimization.

### Ammonia synthesis

The ammonia calculation includes the 15-metal activity screen, scaling and kinetic relations, catalyst productivity and inventory, candidate-specific process optimization, pressure-dependent equipment capital and seven catalyst-dependent cost components. Gas-phase thermodynamics follow one measured basis throughout: the NH₃ formation free energy is taken at each temperature from the Gillespie–Beattie equilibrium constant,[57] in place of the DFT gas energies of the activity source, which place it 0.064–0.065 eV per NH₃ higher, and N₂, H₂ and NH₃ enter the loop equilibrium and the microkinetic gas activities as fugacities with Newton–Cooper coefficients,[46] so that the reactor and the loop share one equilibrium. The plant calibration of catalyst productivity is fixed by the fused-iron bed at 400 °C, 80 bar and a −35 °C separator. Temperature, pressure and separator temperature are reoptimized for each candidate over the full 14,510-state process grid. Descriptor uncertainty is propagated through 1,000 samples; Fe feasibility is evaluated against the 90 m³ bed-volume limit, and economic winner, preservation of the atomistic winner and top-three membership are recorded separately. The S1 source contains Fe terrace energetics but no Fe step-site row, so the Fe step descriptor is obtained from the 14-metal terrace-to-step scaling relation. Leave-one-out validation and prediction-interval propagation are reported in Supplementary Table 5; the regression residual standard error is 0.2271 eV, which is the Fe descriptor uncertainty used in the descriptor Monte Carlo analysis. The atomistic reference conditions (673 K, 24.5 bar N₂, 74.25 bar H₂ and 1 bar NH₃) and step-site descriptor framework follow the Wang–Abild-Pedersen Dataset S1 source.[58] Under those conditions, the source study places Fe and Ru close to the activity plateau and undercoordinated Os close to the volcano maximum.[58] The descriptor-level reconstruction recovers the same high-activity metal set, with Ru, Os and Fe as its leading three candidates, providing the ranking fidelity required for the downstream decision analysis. The Fe process optimum (425 °C, 105 bar) lies within the pressure range of industrial Fe synthesis loops, which have been built from about 80 bar upwards;[45] milder operating conditions are the stated direction of Haber–Bosch development.[59,60] The catalyst-specific optima show how intrinsic energetics propagate into operating severity and cost within a common process model; published Fe/Ru loop studies give independent process-level context for these trends.[14] Perturbing the compressor, vessel, cost-index and capital-recovery inputs by 10–25% retains the Fe > Ru > Os economic order and the activity-only reachability verdict.

The plant benchmark compares the model with 64 values from industrial ammonia synthesis: loop states of 13 industrial loop records, forward runs and cost-optimal designs of the converter, compressor power, the separator vapour and the Fe optimum (Supplementary Table 4). Point values are compared as (model − industrial)/industrial, temperatures as differences in °C and published ranges by the distance to the nearer edge; an item passes when its deviation lies within the tolerance listed with it, and 32 of the 64 pass. The typical deviation of a quantity is the median absolute deviation of its items and the largest is their maximum. The failing items trace to five model components: separator vapour, approach to equilibrium, compression power, catalyst amount and the effective bed temperature. Each component was perturbed by the typical and by the largest deviation of its items, in both directions, alone and in all 32 sign combinations, with the catalyst-amount perturbation applied to fused iron alone or to every catalyst; Fe, Ru and Os were reoptimized over the perturbed process library within the 90 m³ limit after the canonical costs had been reproduced on the same code path. The measured-kinetics comparisons of this perturbation use the fused-iron law of Dyson and Simon[46] over the full pressure range.

For the Ru-price test, only the Ru metal purchase price is changed. It is first set equal to the Fe value of 8 US dollars per kilogram, after which Ru is reoptimized over the full process grid. The Ru-price sweep repeats this optimization at 241 logarithmically spaced prices between 1 and 3 × 10^5 US dollars per kilogram together with the two reference prices. Fe–Ru parity is located by bisection in log price. The baseline Fe–Ru cost difference is decomposed into the same seven cost components.

The economic Monte Carlo analysis uses 5,000 samples with seed 20260920. Fe and Ru metal-price multipliers are sampled independently from a log-uniform distribution over 0.5–2.0. A common CAPEX multiplier is sampled uniformly over 0.8–1.2, electricity price uniformly over 20–100 US dollars per megawatt-hour, and catalyst lifetime uniformly over 5–20 years. Fe and Ru are reoptimized for every sample. The Ru activity multiplier required for parity, α*, is recalculated against the sample-specific reoptimized Fe cost. The draw sequence is generated from the seed and priors, and normalized regret is computed draw-by-draw as (C_Ru − C_Fe)/C_Fe. The descriptor-correlation sensitivity leaves this Monte Carlo unchanged; it preserves the frozen Fe normal marginal and the ±0.15 eV Ru/Os uniform marginals using a Gaussian copula with latent pairwise correlations from 0 to 0.9, and evaluates atomistic ordering on the 673 K one-descriptor volcano. A separate common-bias sweep applies the same additive shift to Ru, Os and Fe descriptors. Neither sensitivity enters a downstream economic probability. The actual-catalyst Monte Carlo adds inputs drawn from a second generator (seed 20261006), so that every draw of the economic Monte Carlo keeps its values: the Ru dispersion ratio u = D_Ru/f_Fe, log-uniform over 11–50, with D_Ru from 11% for a Ba–Cs–K-promoted Ru/C catalyst[40] to about 50% for 2 nm Ru particles in Ba-promoted Ru/C[61] and f_Fe = 1% for the exposed Fe atoms of reduced fused iron;[41] Ru recovery uniform over 90–95.55%, the range measured for spent promoted Ru/C ammonia catalysts;[43,44] Ru content of the supported bed uniform over 5–10 wt%,[42] from a third generator (seed 20261007); and bed density uniform over 430–550 kg m⁻³, the range derived from a promoted Ru/C laboratory bed[62]. In the benchmark-bed variant the Ru price is lowered to p(1 − r)/u, as in Fig. 4d; in the supported-bed variant the Ru activity per kilogram of Ru is multiplied by u, the Ru charge is replaced at (1 − r), and the bed volume follows from the Ru content and bed density. The measured-activity variant replaces u by the effective descriptor and residual multiplier of a randomly drawn measured Ru catalyst together with its Ru content.

For backward design, Ru intrinsic activity is varied directly and the full process is reoptimized at each activity multiplier. Reachability is evaluated separately along the descriptor path defined by the ammonia scaling relation and over the full process grid. For the joint activity–lifecycle target analysis, catalyst lifetime and Ru recovery are represented through the effective replacement-metal price, P_eff = P_Ru(1 − r)(10 y/L). A conservative target region is constructed from the 42 distinct process states already visited by the independently reoptimized Ru price sweep: for each state, catalyst demand is rescaled by a direct activity multiplier and the volume- and pressure-dependent cost terms are recalculated. Restricting the optimization to this state subset can only overestimate the activity improvement required for parity, so the reported multipliers are upper bounds on the backward target.

Physical reachability of that target is then evaluated on the strict *E*_N scaling manifold using all 14,510 process states. At fixed process state, *E*_N affects cost through TOF and therefore the required active-metal inventory and bed volume; the metal-replacement, reactor-volume and pressure-vessel terms are monotone in that requirement, whereas state-specific compression and refrigeration costs are independent of *E*_N. The cost-minimizing descriptor for each state is therefore its TOF-maximizing value on the canonical 0.005-eV scaling grid. Taking the lower envelope across all states gives the lifecycle parity boundary as a function of \(q=(1-r)/L\). The canonical strict-scaling minimum is reproduced before the lifecycle calculation is accepted. The kinetic and scaling relations follow the established transition-metal ammonia-synthesis literature.[5,17,18,58]

The actual-catalyst analysis reads the fully reoptimized Ru price sweep at the effective price p(1 − r)/u, where u is the ratio of exposed metal fractions of the supported catalyst and fused iron (u = 0.11/0.01 = 11, a lower bound) and r the recovered fraction (90–95.55%); the activity multiple for parity is recomputed with full reoptimization at every price. The KAAP comparison restricts both catalysts to the 90 bar, −20 °C separator states. A supported bed holding 8 wt% Ru (5–10 wt%) instead of the common formulation changes only the reactor term; recomputing it for bed densities of 430–550 kg m⁻³ adds 0.05–0.07 US dollars per tonne without recovery and 0.30–0.44 with 90–95.55% recovery (0.03–0.10 in the KAAP loop) at 8 wt%. Metal recovery is a scenario input; the baseline metal comparison assumes none. A lifecycle cash-flow variant keeps the process optimization and prices every metal purchase and recovered-metal credit at its date over the 25-year plant life (purchases at 0, 10 and 20 years; recovered Ru credited at replacement and at closure), discounted at a metal-specific rate of 0–12% per year, and above 12% to locate the break-even rate, with Fe on the same convention; in the plant-life and catalyst-life configuration of the straight-line model and with no discounting it reproduces that accounting to 10⁻⁹ US dollars per tonne. For the two frontier catalysts the descriptor rates are replaced by measured rate laws: the Model 3 rate law of Keestra et al. for commercial fused-iron catalysts, fitted to data from 1 to 324 bar, with the activity factor (0.350) of the Topsoe KM1R catalyst whose data Dyson and Simon fitted,[46,47,63] and the rate equation of a Ba–Cs–K-promoted Ru/C catalyst fitted at 50–100 bar,[40] each on the thermodynamic basis of the loop. Catalyst requirement is integrated along the bed in plug flow at every process state, and each law is calibrated on its own measured rate; the Ru/C law reproduces the 125 measured points of its source with a mean error of 10%, and the fused-iron law the outlet of a commercial magnetite catalyst at 100 bar within 16%. Each catalyst is optimized only over the process states inside the pressure range its law was fitted on, fused iron 1–324 bar and Ru/C 50–100 bar, so every measured-kinetics comparison, including the 9 MPa loop and the staged converter, lies inside both fits. The staged converter has three adiabatic beds with interbed cooling, each filled with fused iron (up to 530 °C) or Ru/C (up to 460 °C, the top of its measured range), at 9 MPa with a −20 °C separator and 8% inerts, and is optimized over bed temperatures and catalyst amounts at every financing rate. Literature activity gains are taken only from comparisons with a reference Ru catalyst in the same study and are placed on the same multiplier axis without entering the cost model. The recycle-boundary check raises the recycle pressure rise from 3 bar to 6.0 and 9.2 bar, the rise between the cold recycle stream and the converter inlet in the 1,500 t/d Krupp–Uhde flowsheet,[64] and reoptimizes all 15 metals over the 14,510 process states. The independent loop check solves a five-component (N₂, H₂, CH₄, Ar, NH₃) Peng–Robinson loop with the temperature-dependent NH₃ binary interaction parameters of Mace and Vrabec,[65] two cold flashes, purge, make-up and a converged recycle; it sizes the bed with the Dyson–Simon and Ru/C rate laws at a common 1,500 t/d of net NH₃ and charges excess feed, utilities, catalyst inventory and annualized reactor and compressor capital (Ru recovery 95.55%, H₂ 2 and N₂ 0.10 US dollars per kilogram).

### Bimetallic surface extension

N adsorption energies (0.5 N₂(g) + * → N*) for the 1,796 surfaces of Mamun et al.[37] were retrieved from Catalysis-Hub;[38] two independent retrievals give identical files of 5,696 adsorption sites. The most stable site of each surface defines its terrace descriptor. A linear fit over the 14 pure metals present in both this database and the source of the model's descriptors (slope 0.845, R² = 0.973, RMS 0.22 eV) maps it onto that source's terrace scale, and the 14-metal terrace-to-step relation already used for Fe (step E_N = 1.1547 × terrace E_N − 0.1701 eV) gives the step-site descriptor. The element-anchored bridge adds to this value each element's offset between its frozen step descriptor and its own bridged value, weighted by atomic fraction. Per-state activity is read from the cached response surface of the 14,510-state library; the cost uses the alloy's mole-weighted molar mass and mass-weighted metal price and is minimized over all states within the 90 m³ bed limit. The 15 model metals keep their frozen prices; 21 other elements take USGS annual-average prices per kilogram of contained metal (2025; Hg 2024),[66] with oxide, ore and ferroalloy quotations divided by the metal mass fraction. Tc has no market price and its 101 surfaces are not costed. Surfaces are labelled transition-metal, group 3–5 (Sc, Y, La, Ti, Zr, Hf, V, Nb, Ta) or sp-metal (Al, Zn, Cd, Hg, Ga, In, Tl, Sn, Pb, Bi) and scored per layer. The descriptor-only lower bound for each candidate combines its best-state activity, the smallest reactor term and the cheapest process state. For the surfaces below Fe and the highest-activity surface of each bridge, the counterfactual sets the metal price to the Fe price, and backward design solves for the activity multiplier and the metal price at which the reoptimized cost equals that of Fe; reachability is the lowest cost at any descriptor on the strict scaling range with the surface's molar mass and price.

### CO₂-to-methanol

The methanol analysis uses a recycle and separation model, calibrated on a published CO₂-to-methanol loop,[34] applied to four Re/TiO₂ states derived from the 1 and 5 wt% catalysts reported by Gothe et al.[19] The reference comparison uses 2% purge. Upstream rankings are reconstructed from space-time yield per gram of Re, single-pass methanol yield and space-time yield per gram of catalyst; the downstream ranking uses net production cost. Re purchase price and catalyst replacement are not included in the reference objective. Purge is varied from 0.5% to 40% over 396 levels, with an additional optimization in which purge is selected separately for each catalyst state. Local economic leverage for space-time yield, conversion and methane suppression is evaluated at the 5 wt% Re, 250 °C state. The process interpretation follows established CO₂-to-methanol assessments in which recycle, compression and separation materially affect plant performance.[20,21] The plant model was checked against published process assessments and pilot-plant data (Supplementary Table 6; Extended Data Fig. 4): at 22–33% per-pass conversion its hydrogen and CO₂ consumption (0.193–0.208 and 1.41–1.47 tonnes per tonne of methanol), carbon efficiency (0.93–0.98) and recycle ratio (2.6–5.4) lie within the reported ranges, and at the operating point and price assumptions of ref. 20 it reproduces its break-even methanol price, production cost plus capital recovery, to within 2.4%. The catalyst-independent stoichiometric H₂ and CO₂ demand is 583.2 euros per tonne on both sides; the catalyst-sensitive terms (feed above stoichiometry, electricity, catalyst replacement and capital) sum to 98.3 against 116.4 euros per tonne in the reference. Setting the loop pressure drop, recycle flow, compression, capital and prices to reference values leaves the space-time-yield disagreement at 24–26 of 81 comparisons without the inlet limit and 41–50 of 81 with it (Extended Data Fig. 4).

The measurement Monte Carlo samples the CO₂ conversion and selectivities of each catalyst state from that state's own uncertainty and re-solves the full methanol plant model in each of 5,000 draws (seed 20261005), with every cost parameter held at its reference value. The source reports single integer values, so the uncertainty is built from the source data: the reporting resolution (±0.5 percentage points, with '<1%' sampled over 0–1%), a 5.2% relative uncertainty in conversion derived from the consistency between space-time yield and conversion across the 21 reported runs, and a 0.85-percentage-point exchange between methanol and methane selectivity derived from the 11 runs of a single catalyst. Space-time yield follows conversion and methanol selectivity. Halving or doubling every width retains the economic winner in 99.3% or 78.4% of draws. The 1 wt% Re, 250 °C state uses the Table 3 selectivities of 97% methanol, 1% CO and 1% CH₄.

Counterfactuals remove one candidate difference at a time (methane selectivity moved to methanol; conversion set to the four-state mean) and re-solve the plant at the reference purge. Backward design changes one property of the space-time-yield winner until its cost equals the economic optimum.

### Published methanol comparisons

Every extracted record with CO₂ conversion, methanol selectivity, temperature, pressure and H₂/CO₂ ratio is a candidate operating point; the primary analysis uses entries whose conversion and selectivities are printed numbers, and entries with plot-read values are added as a sensitivity. Groups are formed by the same rule in both cohorts: of the 161 groups that include plot-read values, 33 come from 22 papers without printed values and 47 have fewer than two printed entries, which leaves the 81 groups of the primary analysis (Supplementary Table 7). An operating point printed twice in one paper is counted once. Selectivities are closed as in the hand-built case: methanol and methane as reported, entries below the reporting limit set to zero (setting the 14 such entries to their printed bound changes no comparison), and the CO-like residual equal to one minus the methanol and methane selectivities; when methane is not reported, it is one minus the methanol and CO selectivities if CO is reported and zero otherwise, and both bounds of an unreported CO–methane split are tested. Comparison groups contain the entries of one paper at the same pressure, H₂/CO₂ ratio and space velocity. Entries whose feed adds water or CO to CO₂ and H₂ are excluded, because the plant model takes a dry CO₂/H₂ make-up. Space-time yield per gram of catalyst defines the paper leaderboard: the printed value when every entry of a group prints it and the ratio of printed to conversion-derived productivity varies by less than a factor of 3 within the group, otherwise the value derived from the mass space velocity, otherwise from the volumetric space velocity with a bulk density of 1.0 g mL⁻¹ (0.5 and 2.0 g mL⁻¹ tested). The plant leaderboard is the net production cost at each entry's own temperature, pressure and H₂/CO₂ ratio, with catalyst mass from the group's productivity basis. Recycled CO is converted per pass to the smallest extent that keeps the reactor outlet at or below both reverse-water-gas-shift and CO-hydrogenation equilibrium, the single-pass CO₂ conversion is capped at the loop's CO₂-hydrogenation equilibrium, and the purge is optimized over 0.5–40% among the levels at which the reactor-inlet content of species other than H₂ and CO₂ (CO, methane and N₂) stays at or below 6.86%, the cost model's limit for the loop that anchors it (6.46% CO and N₂ in its stream table, 6.78% with methanol and water[34]). CO counts toward this limit because, at the cost-optimal purge, recycled CO is converted little further and dilutes the CO₂ and H₂ feed as N₂ does; it still reacts up to the equilibrium bound. The loop without the limit (purge extended to 10⁻⁴% as a check), limits of 4–30%, spanning the 4.4–9.8% of CO, methane and N₂ (4.9–10.2% of all species other than H₂ and CO₂) in four published loop designs, a limit on methane and N₂ alone, recycled CO treated as inert and recycled CO converted up to CO-hydrogenation equilibrium are reported as sensitivities (Extended Data Fig. 8; Supplementary Note 3). A disagreement is near-infeasible when the paper leader's cost in the limited loop exceeds twice its cost without the limit or the regret exceeds 100%. Variants use inert CO, 2% purge, single-pass yield, conversion or selectivity as the paper leaderboard, all entries including plot readings, entries whose reported product selectivities sum to at least 95%, and the comparisons without the paper that supplies most groups. In the catalyst-inventory variant each entry may load more catalyst, raising its conversion along an approach-to-equilibrium curve calibrated on its measured point to at most 95% of equilibrium at constant selectivity. Pressure-dependent condensation enters both leaderboards of a group equally because a group shares one pressure. The lower bound used to allocate the full plant optimization evaluates the same economics at each purge level on lower-bound loop flows that hold for any per-pass conversion of recycled CO between 0 and 1: reactor-inlet CO₂ of at least 1/[X(S_MeOH + (1 − p)S_CO)] per mole of methanol, three moles of H₂ per mole of methanol and four per mole of methane, the loop methane and nitrogen that the purge fraction p sets, and at least two moles of crude liquid; every equipment term rises with its flow, so the minimum over purge levels bounds the plant cost from below. Within each group the paper's leader is evaluated in full, then the remaining candidates in increasing order of the bound until the next bound exceeds the best cost found. Sampling uncertainty is a paper-cluster bootstrap with 10,000 resamples. Measurement uncertainty re-draws every entry 2,000 times with the Re/TiO₂ basis above (5.2% relative conversion error, a 0.85-percentage-point methanol–methane selectivity exchange and, for printed space-time yields, an independent 5.2% relative error), at one, two and four times these widths, and rebuilds both leaderboards from the same draw. Plant costs of perturbed entries come from an additive per-coordinate response surface built from exact model runs, with draws outside its grid run exactly; 7 of 600 group verdicts (4 of 200 at the reported noise level) in the inert-limited loop and 1 of 600 without the limit differ from exact runs of complete replicates. The catalyst-price variant prices each catalyst from its extracted composition, with metal contents at the model and 2025 commodity prices[66] plus a base cost that gives commercial Cu/ZnO/Al₂O₃ 18.1 euros per kilogram,[34] and charges replacement per tonne of inventory.

Catalyst-level comparison. What counts as a different catalyst was fixed before any comparison was counted. ### Adaptive Catalyst Screening Agent

ACSA fetches each paper through the institutional library service, converts it to page-marked text and page images, and extracts every catalyst entry into a fixed schema in which each number carries its value, unit, qualifier, location and page. Extraction uses one strict-JSON language-model call per paper and pass (gpt-5.5, medium reasoning effort): a main pass over text and page images, a figure pass that digitizes every catalyst-performance point in the main-text figures, and passes over the text, tables and figures of the Supporting Information. A printed main-text value outranks a printed Supporting-Information value, which outranks a plot reading. Units are normalized to the model basis. Prompts contain only the paper itself.

Accuracy is scored against two curated methanol datasets[32,33] and the 21 Re/TiO₂ entries of the hand-built case. Entries are matched by paper and catalyst name, or, for the dataset that lists compositions instead of names,[33] by paper, operating conditions and elemental composition, never by the values being scored; a pair with an equally admissible alternative partner counts for recall but not for value accuracy. Strict tolerances are ±1 K for temperature, ±1% for pressure and space-time yield, ±0.05 for the H₂/CO₂ ratio and ±0.5 percentage points for conversion and selectivity. Every disagreement between extracted and curated values was checked against the paper. Curated values that the paper contradicts fall into 26 root causes, one mechanism in one source paper each (45 rules, 169 cells and 8 rows across the two methanol datasets and the ammonia review), with a further 24 space-time-yield cells that one dataset derives from an erroneous input; that dataset computes space-time yield from its yield and space velocity, so a printed space-time yield that differs from the derivation is not counted as an error (Supplementary Table 8). Curated values are corrected to the printed value before scoring, and both raw and corrected scores are reported. Of the extracted entries without a curated partner, 150 of 179 in the first 18 papers and a random sample of 87 in the 31 papers added later were checked against the source. A random sample of 100 extracted values from 10 papers was checked by the authors against the papers and Supporting Information: 91 are printed or equal after unit conversion, 6 are H₂/CO₂ ratios derived from printed feed compositions, and 3 match after a corrected locator, a restored upper-bound qualifier or a stated metric name; none contradicts the source.

Before new candidates are scored, ACSA runs the hand-built inputs of all three systems, and the extracted Re/TiO₂ and Au/TiO₂ inputs, through the same code path and requires the frozen results to be reproduced; the batch runs do not start otherwise. Every extracted Au/TiO₂ input must also equal the hand-built value, because the dispersion enters the chain only as a ratio and cannot be checked through the outputs. A value that the source prints twice with different numbers is accepted in either form: the Au loading appears as 4.41 wt% in the text and 4.40 wt% in Table 1 of the source paper,[24] which changes the required Au mass by 0.2% and no ranking quantity. Candidates are then taken through the forward chain; the descriptor-only lower bound decides which ammonia candidates need the full process optimization, the purge-wise plant bound decides which methanol candidates need the equilibrium-constrained purge optimization, and all candidates were additionally evaluated in full to verify both bounds. The counterfactual and backward calculations are run on the leading candidates only.
