# Process economics reorder catalyst rankings across computed and published catalysts

## Abstract

Catalyst screening ranks materials by descriptors or laboratory performance, whereas industrial selection depends on how catalyst properties propagate through reactor operation and plant economics. We couple catalyst-specific kinetics to candidate-specific process optimization and a catalyst-responsive cost objective, and invert that objective into catalyst-property targets. In ammonia synthesis the intrinsic order Ru > Os > Fe becomes Fe > Ru > Os; Ru would need a 201-fold activity gain, outside the scaling-constrained space, whereas dispersion with metal recovery and alkali promotion reach the parity boundary. An agent that extracts catalytic data from publications and runs the full chain on every candidate extends the analysis to 1,695 bimetallic surfaces and to 443 operating points from 19 methanol studies. Only alloys pairing a cheap 3d metal with a group-6 metal undercut Fe. Within published methanol comparisons, the space-time-yield leader is not the plant-cost leader in 44% of cases, and the selectivity leader in 75%.

## Introduction

Computational catalyst discovery commonly uses adsorption energies, reactivity descriptors, scaling relations, volcano plots and surrogate models to rank candidate materials.[1–6] Descriptor-based microkinetic models have made these relations useful across broad catalyst families, while first-principles multiscale models have connected elementary-step energetics to rates and operating conditions.[6,7,43] Previous work has also linked ammonia descriptors to reactor design and catalyst selection,[32] and formulated catalyst design and process optimization as coupled problems.[33,36] Laboratory reactor studies further show that catalyst rankings can depend on the test environment.[35] What remains less clear is whether the ordering of discrete catalyst candidates is preserved when atomistic predictions or laboratory measurements are propagated through candidate-specific process optimization and economic evaluation.

This question matters most for the leading candidates. A screening model can remain well correlated across a large candidate set while changing the identity of the material that would actually be selected. Global rank correlation therefore does not by itself establish that the top of the ranking is reliable. Early-stage catalyst-economic analysis has likewise shown that catalyst cost per kilogram can be a poor proxy for cost per unit product once catalyst stability and production scale are included.[44] In ammonia synthesis, process-level Fe/Ru studies further show that the preferred catalyst and pressure depend on plant scale and loop configuration.[42,58] These studies motivate a decision-level comparison, but they do not answer whether a broad candidate ranking survives candidate-specific process optimization. The same principle applies to uncertainty: adaptive-sampling and Bayesian approaches show that uncertainty is most useful when interpreted in relation to the quantity or decision of interest.[8–10]

Here we propagate catalyst properties forward through kinetics, candidate-specific process optimization and a catalyst-responsive economic objective, and backward from an economic target to the catalyst property it requires, testing whether that requirement lies within the range allowed by the underlying scaling relations. Integrated material-and-process design has inverted process objectives into molecular targets,[34] and catalyst-design frameworks have optimized descriptors within kinetic models;[33,36] the emphasis here is on candidate ranking, economic parity and physical reachability. Ammonia synthesis provides a 15-metal screen in which intrinsic activity and metal price set catalyst inventory and operating conditions;[5,11,12] CO₂ hydrogenation to methanol, a process in which selectivity sets reactant loss, purge, recycle and compression;[13–15] and Au/TiO₂ CO oxidation, a control in which particle size maps monotonically to catalyst requirement.[16–19] Automated laboratories and AI workflows have accelerated reaction and catalyst optimization,[23–29] and language-model agents can now read the chemical literature and operate computational tools.[20–22,30,31] We use such an agent, the Adaptive Catalyst Screening Agent (ACSA), as the automation layer that extracts catalytic data from publications, runs the forward and backward chain on every candidate and first reproduces the hand-built cases. This extends the analysis from a few hand-selected candidates to 1,695 bimetallic surfaces and to the catalyst comparisons reported in 19 methanol studies.

## Results

### Process and economic evaluation reverse the leading ammonia ranking

The 15-metal ammonia screen ranks Ru, Os and Fe first, second and third in intrinsic activity (Fig. 1c), consistent with the established activity landscape for transition-metal ammonia synthesis.[11,12] After optimizing the operating conditions separately for each catalyst and evaluating catalyst-dependent costs (Fig. 1a,d,e), the order becomes Fe, Ru and Os, with costs of 15.292, 22.031 and 25.832 US dollars per tonne of NH₃, respectively (Fig. 1b,h).

The change is concentrated among the leading candidates. For Ru, Os and Fe, Spearman rho is -0.50 and Kendall tau is -0.33, while the Spearman correlation across all 15 metals remains 0.929 (Fig. 1f,g). The overall screen can therefore remain strongly correlated even when the preferred catalyst changes at the top of the ranking. With three leading candidates these coefficients describe the reordering rather than test it statistically; the decision-relevant quantities are the identity of the winner and the cost of choosing wrongly. Agreement among the leading candidates is thus more relevant to catalyst selection than full-set correlation alone. Agreement can also be expressed in decision terms. If the intrinsic-activity winner Ru were selected instead of the economic winner Fe, the catalyst-dependent cost objective would increase from 15.292 to 22.031 US dollars per tonne of NH₃, corresponding to a normalized decision regret of **44.1%**. The 6.739 US dollars per tonne difference is a small fraction of the cost of ammonia from state-of-the-art gas-based plants, about 160 US dollars per tonne,[49] but it is the part of the cost that catalyst selection controls and amounts to 2.34 million US dollars per year for a 1,000 t/d plant.

### Metal price and process optimization jointly determine the Fe-Ru ranking

The leading metals favor different operating conditions. Fe has an interior optimum near 425 °C and 180 bar with a 30 °C separator, whereas Ru favors approximately 450 °C and 425 bar with a 25 °C separator; Os has a broad, shallow minimum at high pressure (Fig. 2b,c). The economic comparison therefore reflects both catalyst properties and the operating conditions favored by each catalyst.

To isolate the effect of metal price, we repeated the Ru optimization after setting its metal price equal to the Fe value of 8 US dollars per kilogram. All other model inputs, constraints and costing assumptions were unchanged. The Ru optimum shifts from 450 °C and 425 bar to 425 °C and 170 bar, and its optimized cost falls to 14.712 US dollars per tonne of NH₃, 0.580 US dollars per tonne below Fe (Fig. 2b–e). Thus removing the Ru-Fe price difference reverses the economic order after process reoptimization.

A full Ru-price sweep, with process reoptimization at every price, places Fe-Ru parity at 163.76 US dollars per kilogram. At this price, Ru operates at 425 °C and 185 bar with a 6.24 m³ catalyst bed (Fig. 2d). Across the sweep, the optimum moves from 170 bar and a 9.4 m³ bed at 1 US dollar per kilogram to 420–430 bar, a 0 °C separator and a 0.014 m³ bed at 3 × 10^5 US dollars per kilogram. Metal price therefore affects the final ranking directly through catalyst cost and indirectly by changing the preferred process conditions.

The model gives every metal the formulation of the industrial fused-iron catalyst, so the same curve can be read at the cost of a real Ru catalyst (Fig. 2d). A promoted Ru/C ammonia catalyst exposes 11% of its Ru atoms,[45] against fewer than 1% of the Fe atoms in reduced fused iron,[46] and more than 94% of the Ru is recovered from spent catalyst.[47] Dispersion and recovery divide the metal charge in the same way as the metal price, so the catalyst enters at an effective price p(1 − r)/u: 294–490 US dollars per kilogram for 90–94% recovery at a dispersion gain u = 11. At that price Ru costs 15.49–15.67 US dollars per tonne of NH₃, 0.19–0.38 above Fe, at 450 °C and 195–200 bar, and the activity multiple required for parity falls from 201-fold to 1.5–2.2-fold. In a KAAP-type loop at 9.1 MPa with a −20 °C condenser,[48] the same supported and recovered catalyst costs 18.53–18.91 US dollars per tonne against 19.07 for fused iron in that loop. Metal economy through dispersion and recovery is therefore the route that brings Ru to the Fe decision boundary, the route taken industrially by the Kellogg Advanced Ammonia Process.

The baseline Ru-Fe cost difference of 6.739 US dollars per tonne is distributed across several cost components. Fresh-feed compression contributes +4.632 US dollars per tonne, metal inventory +1.763, compressor capital +1.181 and refrigeration +0.629, while vessel pressure, recycle compression and reactor-base terms partly offset these contributions (Fig. 2a,e). This decomposition identifies the main contributions to the baseline difference, whereas the equal-price calculation shows how the optimized process changes when the metal-price difference is removed.

A layer-wise check localizes the reversal more precisely. At the common 673 K atomistic reference, converting turnover frequency into the plant-throughput active-metal requirement preserves the leading order: Ru requires less active metal than Os, and both require far less than Fe. Multiplying those same catalyst demands by the canonical metal prices and the frozen 10-year lifetime changes the order to Fe < Ru < Os before any reactor, compression, refrigeration or equipment cost is added. As an independent causal check, setting the Ru metal price equal to the Fe price and reoptimizing the complete 14,136-state process leaves Ru less costly than Fe (14.712 versus 15.292 US dollars per tonne of NH₃). The first inversion is therefore triggered at the catalyst-demand-to-lifecycle-economics interface; the catalyst-specific process response subsequently amplifies the gap through fresh-feed compression, compressor capital and refrigeration.

Descriptor uncertainty affects feasibility and ranking differently. Across 1,000 descriptor-uncertainty samples, Fe satisfies the 90 m³ bed-volume limit in 79.9% of samples, ranks first economically in 68.1% (681/1000), and remains within the top three in 94.0%. The atomistic winner remains the economic winner in 28.2% of samples (Fig. 2f,g). A supporting error-dependence analysis keeps the same marginal descriptor uncertainties but couples the Ru, Os and Fe errors through a Gaussian copula. The independent case reproduces the frozen atomistic-winner frequencies, whereas increasing positive correlation changes the top-one probabilities rather than simply cancelling uncertainty; at latent pairwise correlation 0.9, Fe is the atomistic winner in 21.7% of draws compared with 10.7% in the matched independent audit. A separate common-offset test shows why: because all three descriptors move across a nonlinear volcano, a shared energetic bias can itself change their ordering. These tests are reported as uncertainty-model sensitivities and are not used to replace the preregistered downstream probabilities.

Economic uncertainty around the baseline case does not overturn the Fe preference. Across 5,000 preregistered samples that vary metal price, the CAPEX coefficient, electricity price and catalyst lifetime, full process reoptimization gives P(C_Fe < C_Ru) = 1.000, with a minimum sampled Ru-Fe cost difference of 2.382 US dollars per tonne (Fig. 2h). Expressed as normalized selection regret, R = (C_Ru - C_Fe)/C_Fe, the 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles are **30.48%, 40.89% and 54.25%**, respectively, and R remains positive in all 5,000 draws. The Ru activity multiplier required for economic parity is 70.78-fold, 174.27-fold and 462.00-fold at the 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles, respectively (Fig. 3b). The equal-price calculation therefore identifies a condition under which the ranking reverses, whereas the uncertainty analysis establishes both the sign and the practical cost consequence of the Fe preference over the economic range examined around the baseline case.

### Backward design separates the required catalyst-property region from physical reachability

The forward analysis identifies the preferred catalyst but does not show how much Ru would need to improve to reach parity with Fe. We therefore multiplied Ru intrinsic activity by a factor alpha and reoptimized the full process at each value. Under the baseline economic assumptions, Ru reaches the Fe cost only after a 201.22-fold activity increase (Fig. 3a,c).

The accessible range defined by the scaling relation is much smaller. At 673 K, the largest activity increase along the scaling relation is 1.090-fold, and the largest increase found across all allowed process conditions is 2.525-fold. Under strict scaling, the lowest Ru cost is 21.398 US dollars per tonne of NH₃ at *E*_N = -1.215 eV, which remains above the Fe cost (Fig. 3d,e). The 201.22-fold requirement therefore lies far outside the range accessible by moving Ru along the present scaling relation. Even the 5th-percentile requirement under economic uncertainty, 70.78-fold, remains well above the 2.525-fold maximum (Fig. 3b).

Within the model and scaling relation used here, improving Ru activity alone cannot make Ru economically competitive with Fe over the economic range considered.

Measured catalysts show which modifications supply gains of the required size (Fig. 3a, bottom). Against unpromoted Ru on the same support, Ba and Cs promotion of Ru/C raise the turnover frequency per surface Ru 75- and 65-fold,[50] Ba promotion of Ru/BN raises the rate more than 100-fold at 5 MPa,[51] and Cs promotion of Ru/MgO more than 134-fold at 350 °C,[52] all within the 70.78–462-fold parity band. Electride and oxyhydride supports give gains of about tenfold (9.8-fold for Ru/C12A7:e⁻ and 8.4-fold for Ru/BaTiO₂.₅H₀.₅ at 5 MPa),[53,54] a Ru–Ba core–shell on calcium amide at least 33.5-fold against Cs–Ru/MgO,[55] and confinement inside carbon nanotubes lowers activity to 0.5-fold.[56] Electronic promotion, which changes the energetics beyond a single scaling relation, is the measured route that reaches the parity band. Most of these gains were measured at 0.1–1 MPa; the Ba–Ru/BN and oxyhydride results show the effect at 5 MPa.

The same parity calculation can be written as a two-property lifecycle target at fixed Ru intrinsic activity. The fully reoptimized Ru price sweep gives an effective Ru parity price of 163.76 US dollars per kilogram. Because metal recovery and catalyst lifetime enter the Ru replacement-cost term multiplicatively with metal price, the corresponding boundary is \(P_{Ru}(1-r)(10\,\mathrm{y}/L) \le 163.76\). At the baseline Ru price of 53,852.5 US dollars per kilogram, this requires approximately 99.70% recovery for a 10-year lifetime, 99.39% for a 20-year lifetime, or a 32.9-year lifetime at 99% recovery. The prespecified 5–20-year lifetime range and 99% optimistic recovery bound therefore do not reach parity at the current Ru activity.

Allowing the backward variables to move together changes the required target before physical reachability is tested. We conservatively evaluated the 53 distinct process states already visited by the fully reoptimized Ru price sweep, rescaling catalyst demand by a direct Ru activity multiplier and recomputing the volume- and pressure-dependent cost terms at each state. Because this search uses only a subset of the 14,136-state library, the resulting activity requirements are conservative upper bounds. With 99% Ru recovery, extending catalyst life from 10 to 15 and 20 years lowers the required direct activity multiplier from at most 2.418-fold to 1.742-fold and 1.401-fold, respectively; a 20-year lifetime with 98% recovery requires at most 2.418-fold.

We then tested this target region directly on the strict *E*_N scaling manifold across all 14,136 process states. The canonical strict-scaling minimum, 21.398 US dollars per tonne at *E*_N = -1.215 eV, is reproduced before lifecycle changes are introduced. The joint parity boundary is controlled by the catalyst-loss factor \(q=(1-r)/L\), with \(q^*=4.4068\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{y}^{-1}\). Within the prespecified range of catalyst lifetimes up to 20 years and Ru recovery up to 99%, the closest point is 20 years and 99% recovery: its best strict-scaling state occurs at *E*_N = -1.230 eV and costs 15.362 US dollars per tonne, 0.071 US dollars per tonne above Fe. Parity therefore lies just outside the tested property range, requiring 99.119% recovery at a 20-year lifetime or, equivalently, a 22.69-year lifetime at 99% recovery. The backward-design result is thus a narrow property region near the edge of the present scaling-constrained design space rather than a single activity threshold.

### Selectivity, recycle and purge reshape the methanol ranking

The same ranking change appears in CO₂ hydrogenation to methanol through a different process pathway. Four literature-derived Re/TiO₂ catalyst-temperature states were compared (Fig. 4a,b).[13] Using space-time yield per gram of Re as the catalyst metric, the order is 1 wt% Re at 250 °C, 1 wt% Re at 200 °C, 5 wt% Re at 200 °C and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C. After propagation through the recycle and separation model at the 2% purge used in the reference case, the economic order becomes 5 wt% Re at 200 °C, 1 wt% Re at 250 °C, 1 wt% Re at 200 °C and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C, with net production costs of 943, 962, 967 and 1,258 euros per tonne, respectively (Fig. 4c).

The state with the highest space-time yield per gram of Re falls from first to second economically, and the state ranked third by space-time yield is the economic optimum. Selecting the upstream winner would give 961.51 euros per tonne instead of the 943.30 euros per tonne economic optimum, a normalized decision regret of **1.93%**. Across the four states, Spearman rho is 0.40, Kendall tau is 0.33 and two of six pairwise comparisons invert (Fig. 4e). Methane formation consumes reactants and increases gas accumulation, purge, recycle and compression requirements. A local sensitivity analysis at the 5 wt% Re, 250 °C state gives economic leverage values of 0.37579 for methane suppression, 0.05883 for single-pass conversion and 0.00289 for space-time yield (Fig. 4a,g). These sensitivities are consistent with previous CO₂-to-methanol process studies in which recycle, compression and separation make substantial contributions to plant performance.[14,15]

The reordering persists across the full purge range considered. Over 396 purge levels from 0.5% to 40%, the state with the highest space-time yield per gram of Re is never the least-cost option; Spearman rho never exceeds 0.80 and at least one of six pairwise comparisons remains inverted. Optimizing purge separately for each catalyst state changes the detailed order but does not restore the space-time-yield ranking (Fig. 4f).

Counterfactual and backward calculations locate the cause. Moving every state's methane selectivity into methanol reverses the correlation to rho = −0.80 and makes the highest-conversion state the least costly, whereas equalizing conversion raises rho to +0.80. For the space-time-yield winner, no increase in space-time yield reaches parity: unlimited productivity still costs 954.64 euros per tonne against 943.30. Raising its single-pass conversion from 0.23 to 0.281, inside the 0.19–0.40 range measured on the same catalyst family, or reaching complete methanol selectivity does. As in ammonia, the catalyst property that ranks the candidates upstream is not the one that can move the industrial decision.

Propagating each state's own measurement uncertainty separates the robust positions from the unresolved one (Fig. 4d). In 5,000 draws in which the CO₂ conversion and selectivities of every state are sampled independently and the full plant model is re-solved, the economic winner keeps first place in 4,559 draws (91.2%) and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C remains last in all 5,000. The second and third places, 5.5 euros per tonne apart, exchange in 1,054 draws (21.1%). The space-time-yield winner becomes the economic optimum in 413 draws (8.3%), and the complete space-time-yield ranking is recovered in 24 draws (0.5%).

### Catalyst effects on process operation determine whether rankings change

The ammonia and methanol results identify different routes by which catalyst properties affect process cost (Fig. 5a). In ammonia, intrinsic activity and metal price determine catalyst inventory and the preferred operating conditions, which in turn change compression, reactor and equipment costs. In methanol, selectivity changes reactant losses, gas accumulation, purge, recycle and compression. Absolute costs are not compared across the two reactions; the comparison concerns how catalyst properties enter each process model.

Au/TiO₂ CO oxidation provides a control in which the relation between particle size and catalyst requirement is monotonic. Low-temperature CO oxidation on supported Au is well known to depend on particle size and support effects.[16,17] The present calculation uses the absolute-rate anchor reported by Janssens et al.[18] and the particle-size dependence measured by Overbury et al.[19] at the closest Au loading. Candidate states differ only in Au particle size from 2 to 6 nm under the same operating condition and process model.

Under this common mapping, intrinsic activity and required catalyst mass preserve the same order, 2 > 3 > 4 > 5 > 6 nm. Spearman rho and Kendall tau are both 1.000, no pairwise comparison inverts, and all 10,000 parameter samples drawn from ranges reported in the literature preserve the complete order (Fig. 5b–d), as required of a control in which catalyst burden is the inverse of activity. The upstream winner is therefore also the minimum-burden downstream choice, giving zero selection regret under this control mapping. When moderate candidate-specific kinetic and operating variations are introduced, the exact order is retained in 92.16% of 10,000 samples over 273.15–293.15 K, with mean rho = 0.99214. Extending the temperature range to 313.15 K reduces exact preservation to 72.62% while retaining mean rho = 0.96802 (Fig. 5e). Process and economic evaluation therefore do not necessarily change a catalyst ranking; changes arise when catalyst properties affect downstream operating requirements strongly enough to alter relative costs (Fig. 5f).

### An automated agent extends the analysis to published and computed catalysts

The hand-built analyses compare three ammonia metals, four methanol states and five Au particle sizes. ACSA scales the same analysis to every candidate that publications or databases provide, while the kinetic, process and economic models remain unchanged (Fig. 6a). It performs three tasks. It reads each paper and its Supporting Information and records every catalyst entry with composition, loading, operating conditions, conversion, selectivities and rates, together with the table, figure and page each value comes from. It takes every candidate through the forward chain, with a cheap lower bound deciding which candidates need the full process optimization. Before scoring any new candidate it must reproduce the frozen numbers of the hand-built cases.

Extraction was scored on 20 methanol papers: the 19 that a manually curated dataset covers (413 curated entries)[62] and the Re/TiO₂ study that supplies the hand-built methanol states.[13] The agent recovers 396 of the 413 curated entries (96%). Values printed in tables are extracted exactly: 58 of 58 CO₂ conversions and 58 of 58 methanol selectivities in main-text tables, and 117 of 117 conversions, 113 of 114 selectivities and 107 of 107 space-time yields in the Supporting Information (Fig. 6b). Values read from plots fall within the strict tolerance for 131 of 153 conversions and 59 of 131 selectivities. After checking every extracted entry that has no curated counterpart against the source, 89% are correct; most of them are entries the curated set does not list. The same comparison identifies 271 curated cells that differ from the values printed in the papers. All 21 Re/TiO₂ entries come out exactly.

The self-check passes in both reaction systems. Taking the extracted Re/TiO₂ entries through the methanol model reproduces the frozen costs of all 21 entries to 2.3 × 10⁻¹³ euros per tonne, including the four hand-built states at 943.30, 961.51, 966.96 and 1,258.17 euros per tonne. In ammonia, the batch code path reproduces the canonical cost of every pure metal to 10⁻⁹ US dollars per tonne.

Cheap calculations decide where the expensive ones are spent. For each ammonia candidate a lower bound on cost, built from the best-state activity at its descriptor, the smallest reactor and the cheapest process state, requires no process optimization. Across the 1,695 priced bimetallic surfaces this bound excludes 1,397 candidates (82.4%) from the full 14,136-state optimization. Optimizing all 1,695 in full confirms that no candidate below the Fe cost is excluded. In methanol, the plant economics evaluated on lower-bound loop flows at each purge level, valid for any per-pass conversion of recycled CO, replace the equilibrium-constrained purge optimization; taking each comparison group in order of this bound, with the paper's leader always evaluated, needs the full optimization for 89 of the 413 candidates (324 excluded, 78.5%) and finds the plant-cost leader of all 36 groups, at 22% of the compute of evaluating every candidate. Extraction takes about 0.15 million language-model tokens per paper across the main-text, figure and Supporting-Information passes.

### Bimetallic surfaces that undercut Fe pair a cheap 3d metal with a group-6 metal

The 15-metal screen contains only three economically feasible candidates. To test whether the Fe preference survives a much larger candidate space, ACSA took the 1,796 bimetallic and pure-metal surfaces of a high-throughput adsorption database[60,61] through the ammonia chain. The most stable N adsorption energy of each surface is placed on the model's step-site descriptor by the terrace-to-step relation that the workbook already uses for Fe, after a linear bridge between the two datasets over the 14 pure metals they share. A second, element-anchored bridge places every pure metal exactly at its frozen descriptor. Metal prices are the frozen model prices for the 15 model metals and 2025 annual averages for the remaining elements.[63]

Among the 372 surfaces that contain only transition metals outside groups 3–5, 138 satisfy the 90 m³ bed-volume limit. Five undercut Fe under the linear bridge: Cu₃Cr (14.36 US dollars per tonne), Fe₃Mo, Cu₃Mo, Fe₃W and Ni₃W. Six do so under the element-anchored bridge: Cu₃Cr, Cu₃Mo, CuMo, MoNi, CoMo and CoW (Fig. 6c). Every one of them pairs Fe, Co, Ni or Cu with Cr, Mo or W, the combination that descriptor interpolation has identified for ammonia synthesis.[64] The individual alloys depend on the bridge; the family does not. The most active surface ranks 43rd economically (Os₃Pt, 70.9% regret) under the linear bridge and 40th (AuW, 62.9%) under the element-anchored bridge, with Spearman correlations of 0.72 and 0.67 between activity and cost. As in the 15-metal screen, the activity leader is not the economic leader. Counterfactual and backward calculations, run only on these leading surfaces, show the same mechanism as for Ru. At the Fe price both activity leaders would undercut Fe (14.96 and 14.99 US dollars per tonne), whereas at their own prices they need 689-fold and 405-fold activity increases to reach Fe parity, and no descriptor on the strict scaling range brings them below 24.54 and 23.20 US dollars per tonne. Cu₃Cr and Cu₃Mo remain below Fe under both bridges, and Cu₃Cr keeps its lead with 24% of its activity. Surfaces containing group 3–5 elements, which form stable bulk nitrides, and surfaces containing sp metals, which lie outside the transition-metal scaling relations of the activity model, are reported as separate layers (Supplementary Information).

### Published methanol leaderboards and plant-cost leaderboards often disagree

Methanol papers rank catalysts by laboratory performance, most often by space-time yield. ACSA extracted 443 operating points with conversion, selectivity, temperature, pressure, H₂/CO₂ ratio and productivity from 19 studies. Within each paper, entries tested at the same pressure, H₂/CO₂ ratio and space velocity form the comparison the paper itself makes, giving 36 comparisons with 413 entries. Each entry was taken through the methanol recycle–economics model at its own conditions, with recycled CO converted to reverse-water-gas-shift equilibrium and the purge optimized for each entry.

The space-time-yield leader is not the plant-cost leader in 16 of 36 comparisons (44%), spread over 7 of the 19 papers, and 492 of 3,801 pairwise orderings (13%) invert (Fig. 6d). Ranking by methanol selectivity disagrees more often and at greater cost: in 27 of 36 comparisons (75%) across 16 papers, with a median regret of 69% where the leaders differ. Ranking by conversion disagrees in 6 of 36. Treating recycled CO as inert instead, the opposite bound, raises the space-time-yield disagreement to 19 of 36 comparisons across 14 papers. The disagreement holds for printed values alone (10 of 26 comparisons) and for entries whose reported products close to methanol, CO and methane (13 of 30).

The plant ranking follows from what each by-product does in a recycle loop (Fig. 6e). Recycled CO is converted further, so CO selectivity costs little, whereas methane leaves only with the purge. In the Re/TiO₂ study, the space-time-yield leader ranks third because its 3% methane selectivity outweighs its productivity. In a high-pressure Cu/Al₂O₃ study,[65] the space-time-yield leader at 10 MPa (Cu–Ba/Al₂O₃ at 473 K, 62% methanol selectivity, 2.8% conversion) costs 1,170 euros per tonne, against 1,002 for Cu–K/Al₂O₃ at 553 K (0.9% selectivity, 20.7% conversion). The largest regrets, up to 182%, occur in that study, whose productivity leaders run at 1.3–9.3% single-pass conversion and would need a very large recycle; in the other 18 papers the regret stays below 4%. Selectivity leaders are costly because the most selective entry of a comparison usually runs at low conversion and needs a large catalyst inventory and recycle.

## Discussion

Atomic-scale rank agreement is not sufficient to guarantee the same industrial choice. In ammonia, the full 15-metal ranking remains strongly correlated while the decision-frontier order Ru > Os > Fe becomes Fe > Ru > Os after process and economic propagation. The layer-wise ablation localizes the first reversal: intrinsic activity and catalyst demand preserve the upstream order, whereas monetizing catalyst inventory through metal price and replacement economics flips Fe and Ru; process reoptimization then amplifies the gap through compression, compressor capital and refrigeration. The same decision consequence is captured by normalized regret: selecting the upstream winner costs 44.1% more than the downstream winner in the baseline ammonia objective, 1.93% more in the methanol objective, and 0% in the Au/TiO₂ preservation control. For ammonia, the preregistered cost-side Monte Carlo keeps regret positive in all 5,000 draws, with a median of 40.89% and a 5th–95th percentile range of 30.48–54.25%.

A local criterion makes the inversion condition explicit. For a higher-is-better upstream metric s and lower-is-better downstream objective J, a first-order log expansion gives Δln J ≈ -ε_s Δln s + Σ_k ε_k Δln z_k, where the z_k are catalyst-dependent process or economic variables. For candidates with Δln s > 0, the upstream advantage is preserved when the direct-performance term dominates, but it inverts when the aggregate downstream penalty exceeds it. A finite-difference transfer index, χ_AB = ln(J_A/J_B) / ln(s_A/s_B), provides a model-agnostic sign diagnostic of the same outcome: χ_AB < 0 indicates preservation and χ_AB > 0 inversion. The canonical values are +0.085 for Ru versus Fe in ammonia, +0.015 for the methanol STY winner versus the NPC winner, and approximately -1.00 for 2 nm versus 6 nm Au/TiO₂. The elasticities and magnitudes are process-specific; the competition between direct catalyst benefit and downstream penalty is the transferable criterion.

The ammonia mechanism is consistent with independent process studies. Aspen-based analyses of high-activity Ru catalysts report that lower-pressure operation can reduce reactant-gas compression while increased ammonia-separation refrigeration, expensive Ru and catalyst lifetime can offset that benefit.[57] Recent Ru reviews likewise emphasize metal price, loading, lifetime and recycling as practical constraints,[59] while comparative low- and high-pressure Haber–Bosch assessments show that pressure materially changes energy and economic performance.[58] These studies do not reproduce the present reduced cost model or the Fe > Ru result, but they support the same multiscale conclusion: a more active catalyst cannot be evaluated independently of the process regime and lifecycle economics it induces. The 1,695-surface alloy screen extends the conclusion beyond the three frontier metals: the surfaces that undercut Fe are not the most active ones but cheap 3d metals paired with group-6 metals.

Backward design converts that mechanism into a materials target. At the baseline, Ru would require a 201.22-fold activity increase to reach Fe parity, and even the 5th-percentile requirement under economic uncertainty is 70.78-fold, far beyond the 2.525-fold maximum state-specific gain found along the present scaling relation. Measured catalysts show which routes deliver that target: dispersion and metal recovery bring supported Ru to the Fe boundary, and alkali or alkaline-earth promotion supplies gains inside the 70.78–462-fold parity band. Allowing catalyst lifetime and Ru recovery to improve jointly changes the target geometry: at 99% recovery, a 20-year lifetime reduces the conservative direct-activity requirement to at most 1.401-fold. The exact 14,136-state calculation places the best point inside the prespecified 20-year/99%-recovery box only 0.071 US dollars per tonne above Fe; parity lies just outside, at 99.119% recovery for 20 years or 22.69 years at 99% recovery. The design output is therefore a narrow property boundary, not a single activity target.

Uncertainty enters at two different levels. Process and economic perturbations leave the Fe–Ru–Os decision and the activity-only reachability verdict unchanged, while atomistic rank probabilities remain conditional on the descriptor-error model. The supporting Gaussian-copula and common-bias tests show that correlated or shared energetic errors can change top-rank probabilities on a nonlinear volcano even when marginal uncertainties are unchanged. Decision robustness should therefore be reported against both parameter ranges and error structure rather than summarized by a single uncertainty amplitude.

The methanol and Au/TiO₂ systems show why the relevant screening variable is reaction- and process-dependent. In ammonia, activity and metal price act mainly through catalyst inventory and the operating regime. In methanol, methane selectivity has much larger local economic leverage than space-time yield because it changes reactant loss, purge, recycle and compression. Across published methanol comparisons the consequence is systematic: the space-time-yield leader is not the plant-cost leader in 44% of comparisons, and ranking by selectivity alone, which favours low-conversion operating points, misleads in 75%. In the Au/TiO₂ control, a common monotonic mapping preserves the upstream order. Screening should therefore target the catalyst property that has sufficient downstream leverage to alter the industrial decision, rather than assuming that one upstream metric transfers across processes.

ACSA is what turns these cases into a screening result. Extraction of published catalytic data reaches 96% recall with exact transcription of printed values, the agent reproduces the hand-built cases before it scores new candidates, and descriptor-only bounds spare the full process optimization for 82.4% of computed candidates without excluding any that undercut the incumbent. The agent adds no new scientific model; its contribution is scale, which converts statements about a few hand-selected candidates into the fraction of published comparisons whose leader would change.

The three systems are constructed to resolve distinct modes of multiscale decision propagation. Each is taken through the same sequence — upstream ranking, candidate-specific process treatment, cost, counterfactual and backward design — with the process degrees of freedom set by its model: temperature, pressure and separator temperature in ammonia, purge in methanol and the operating point in the Au extension. In ammonia, a common descriptor axis connects intrinsic energetics to microkinetics, catalyst demand, process reoptimization and catalyst-responsive economics, making the displacement between atomic and economic optima directly measurable. In methanol, the same decision logic is transferred through a selectivity–purge–recycle pathway, whereas Au/TiO₂ provides the complementary regime in which the upstream ordering is preserved after downstream propagation. Together, these cases establish a general workflow for locating where catalyst rankings change, measuring the consequence of selecting on an upstream proxy, mapping economic parity back to catalyst-property targets, and testing whether those targets are physically reachable. The framework is modular: formulation-specific catalyst physics and higher-fidelity kinetic, process and economic descriptions can be introduced without changing the decision logic.

## Methods

### Multiscale decision analysis

Each catalytic system is evaluated with a process-level objective matched to the catalyst-to-process pathway under study. For ammonia, the economic objective contains the terms that respond directly to catalyst identity and catalytic performance: catalyst inventory and replacement, reactor requirements, compression, refrigeration and pressure-dependent equipment. Common upstream H₂/N₂ supply contributions are held fixed so that they do not dilute the catalyst-dependent decision signal. The methanol model evaluates net production cost through its recycle and separation process, while the Au/TiO₂ control maps particle-size-dependent activity to required catalyst mass under a common process model. Cross-system comparison is made at the level of ranking propagation and coupling topology rather than by equating the absolute economic objectives of different reactions.

Within each system, rankings before and after process evaluation are compared using Spearman and Kendall correlations, pairwise inversions, top-rank identity and normalized decision regret. For a lower-is-better downstream objective J, regret is defined as R = (J_upstream-winner - J_downstream-winner) / J_downstream-winner. Analyses of the leading candidates are reported separately from full-set statistics so that changes at the top of the ranking are not obscured by agreement among weaker candidates. Backward design varies a selected catalyst property, reoptimizes the process and identifies the value required for economic parity. This value is then compared with the range accessible under the corresponding catalyst-property relation or scaling relation. Perturbation tests change one predefined input while preserving the other model assumptions and repeating the same downstream optimization.

### Ammonia synthesis

The ammonia calculation includes the 15-metal activity screen, scaling and kinetic relations, catalyst productivity and inventory, candidate-specific process optimization, pressure-dependent equipment capital and seven catalyst-dependent cost components. Temperature, pressure and separator temperature are reoptimized for each candidate over the full 14,136-state process grid. Descriptor uncertainty is propagated through 1,000 samples; Fe feasibility is evaluated against the 90 m³ bed-volume limit, and economic winner, preservation of the atomistic winner and top-three membership are recorded separately. The S1 source contains Fe terrace energetics but no Fe step-site row, so the Fe step descriptor is obtained from the 14-metal terrace-to-step scaling relation. Leave-one-out validation and prediction-interval propagation are reported in the Supplementary Information; the regression residual standard error is 0.2271 eV, which is the Fe descriptor uncertainty used in the descriptor Monte Carlo analysis. The atomistic reference conditions (673 K, 24.5 bar N₂, 74.25 bar H₂ and 1 bar NH₃) and step-site descriptor framework follow the Wang–Abild-Pedersen Dataset S1 source.[39] Under those conditions, the source study places Fe and Ru close to the activity plateau and undercoordinated Os close to the volcano maximum.[39] The descriptor-level reconstruction recovers the same high-activity metal set, with Ru, Os and Fe as its leading three candidates, providing the ranking fidelity required for the downstream decision analysis. The Fe process optimum (425 °C, 180 bar) lies within the conventional industrial Fe Haber–Bosch operating envelope discussed in the ammonia-catalysis and process literature.[40,41] The resulting catalyst-specific optima quantify how intrinsic energetics propagate into operating severity and economic consequence within a common, controlled process model. Published Fe/Ru loop studies provide independent process-level context for these trends despite differences in catalyst formulation, plant scale and flowsheet design.[42] Pressure-CAPEX and equipment-cost diagnostics perturb the compressor, vessel, cost-index and capital-recovery assumptions by approximately 10–25%; the Fe > Ru > Os economic order and the activity-only reachability verdict are retained, demonstrating that the central decision is robust to these model-form perturbations.

For the Ru-price test, only the Ru metal purchase price is changed. It is first set equal to the Fe value of 8 US dollars per kilogram, after which Ru is reoptimized over the full process grid. The Ru-price sweep repeats this optimization at 241 logarithmically spaced prices between 1 and 3 × 10^5 US dollars per kilogram together with the two reference prices. Fe-Ru parity is located by bisection in log price. The baseline Fe-Ru cost difference is decomposed into the same seven cost components.

The preregistered economic Monte Carlo analysis uses 5,000 samples with seed 20260920. Fe and Ru metal-price multipliers are sampled independently from a log-uniform distribution over 0.5–2.0. A common CAPEX multiplier is sampled uniformly over 0.8–1.2, electricity price uniformly over 20–100 US dollars per megawatt-hour, and catalyst lifetime uniformly over 5–20 years. Fe and Ru are reoptimized for every sample. The Ru activity multiplier required for parity, α*, is recalculated against the sample-specific reoptimized Fe cost. The complete draw sequence has been reconstructed from the frozen seed and priors and reproduces the previously reported Fe/Ru cost and cost-gap quantiles exactly; normalized regret is then computed draw-by-draw as (C_Ru-C_Fe)/C_Fe. The supporting descriptor-correlation sensitivity does not modify this preregistered Monte Carlo. Instead, it preserves the frozen Fe normal marginal and the ±0.15 eV Ru/Os uniform marginals using a Gaussian copula with latent pairwise correlations from 0 to 0.9, and evaluates atomistic ordering on the frozen 673 K one-descriptor volcano. A separate common-bias sweep applies the same additive shift to Ru, Os and Fe descriptors. Neither sensitivity is used to report a new downstream economic probability.

For backward design, Ru intrinsic activity is varied directly and the full process is reoptimized at each activity multiplier. Reachability is evaluated separately along the descriptor path defined by the ammonia scaling relation and over the full process grid. For the joint activity–lifecycle target analysis, catalyst lifetime and Ru recovery are represented through the effective replacement-metal price, P_eff = P_Ru(1-r)(10 y/L). A conservative target region is constructed from the 53 distinct process states already visited by the independently reoptimized Ru price sweep: for each state, catalyst demand is rescaled by a direct activity multiplier and the volume- and pressure-dependent cost terms are recalculated. Restricting the optimization to this state subset can only overestimate the activity improvement required for parity, so the reported multipliers are upper bounds on the backward target.

Physical reachability of that target is then evaluated on the strict *E*_N scaling manifold using all 14,136 process states. At fixed process state, *E*_N affects cost through TOF and therefore the required active-metal inventory and bed volume; the metal-replacement, reactor-volume and pressure-vessel terms are monotone in that requirement, whereas state-specific compression and refrigeration costs are independent of *E*_N. The cost-minimizing descriptor for each state is therefore its TOF-maximizing value on the canonical 0.005-eV scaling grid. Taking the lower envelope across all states gives the lifecycle parity boundary as a function of \(q=(1-r)/L\). The canonical strict-scaling minimum is reproduced before the lifecycle calculation is accepted. The kinetic and scaling relations follow the established transition-metal ammonia-synthesis literature.[5,11,12,39]

The actual-catalyst analysis reads the fully reoptimized Ru price sweep at the effective price p(1 − r)/u, where u is the ratio of exposed metal fractions of the supported catalyst and fused iron (u = 0.11/0.01 = 11, a lower bound) and r the recovered fraction (90–94%); the activity multiple for parity is recomputed with full reoptimization at every price. The KAAP comparison restricts both catalysts to the 90 bar, −20 °C separator states. A supported bed holding 3.2 wt% Ru instead of the common formulation changes only the reactor term; recomputing it for bed densities of 500–1,000 kg m⁻³ adds 0.15–0.83 US dollars per tonne. Literature activity gains are taken only from comparisons with a reference Ru catalyst in the same study and are placed on the same multiplier axis without entering the cost model.

### Bimetallic surface extension

N adsorption energies (0.5 N₂(g) + * → N*) for the 1,796 surfaces of Mamun et al.[60] were retrieved from Catalysis-Hub;[61] two independent retrievals give identical files of 5,696 adsorption sites. The most stable site of each surface defines its terrace descriptor. A linear fit over the 14 pure metals present in both this database and the source of the model's descriptors (slope 0.845, R² = 0.973, RMS 0.22 eV) maps it onto that source's terrace scale, and the 14-metal terrace-to-step relation already used for Fe (step E_N = 1.1547 × terrace E_N − 0.1701 eV) gives the step-site descriptor. The element-anchored bridge adds to this value each element's offset between its frozen step descriptor and its own bridged value, weighted by atomic fraction. Per-state activity is read from the cached response surface of the frozen 14,136-state library; the cost uses the alloy's mole-weighted molar mass and mass-weighted metal price and is minimized over all states within the 90 m³ bed limit. The 15 model metals keep their frozen prices; the 22 other elements take 2025 annual-average prices per kilogram of contained metal,[63] with oxide, ore and ferroalloy quotations divided by the metal mass fraction. Tc has no market price and its 101 surfaces are not costed. Surfaces are labelled transition-metal, group 3–5 (Sc, Y, La, Ti, Zr, Hf, V, Nb, Ta) or sp-metal (Al, Zn, Cd, Hg, Ga, In, Tl, Sn, Pb, Bi) and scored per layer. The descriptor-only lower bound for each candidate combines its best-state activity, the smallest reactor term and the cheapest process state. For the surfaces below Fe and the highest-activity surface of each bridge, the counterfactual sets the metal price to the Fe price, and backward design solves for the activity multiplier and the metal price at which the reoptimized cost equals that of Fe; reachability is the lowest cost at any descriptor on the strict scaling range with the surface's molar mass and price.

### CO₂-to-methanol

The methanol analysis uses a recycle and separation model applied to four Re/TiO₂ states derived from the 1 and 5 wt% catalysts reported by Gothe et al.[13] The reference comparison uses 2% purge. Upstream rankings are reconstructed from space-time yield per gram of Re, single-pass methanol yield and space-time yield per gram of catalyst; the downstream ranking uses net production cost. Re purchase price and catalyst replacement are not included in the reference objective. Purge is varied from 0.5% to 40% over 396 levels, with an additional optimization in which purge is selected separately for each catalyst state. Local economic leverage for space-time yield, conversion and methane suppression is evaluated at the 5 wt% Re, 250 °C state. The process interpretation follows established CO₂-to-methanol assessments in which recycle, compression and separation materially affect plant performance.[14,15]

The measurement Monte Carlo samples the CO₂ conversion and selectivities of each catalyst state from that state's own uncertainty and re-solves the full methanol plant model in each of 5,000 draws (seed 20261005), with every cost parameter held at its reference value. The source reports single integer values, so the uncertainty is built from the source data: the reporting resolution (±0.5 percentage points, with '<1%' sampled over 0–1%), a 5.2% relative uncertainty in conversion derived from the consistency between space-time yield and conversion across the 21 reported runs, and a 0.85-percentage-point exchange between methanol and methane selectivity derived from the 11 runs of a single catalyst. Space-time yield follows conversion and methanol selectivity. Halving or doubling every width retains the economic winner in 99.3% or 78.4% of draws. The 1 wt% Re, 250 °C state uses the Table 3 selectivities of 97% methanol, 1% CO and 1% CH₄.

Counterfactuals remove one candidate difference at a time (methane selectivity moved to methanol; conversion set to the four-state mean) and re-solve the plant at the reference purge. Backward design changes one property of the space-time-yield winner until its cost equals the economic optimum.

### Published methanol comparisons

Every extracted record with CO₂ conversion, methanol selectivity, temperature, pressure and H₂/CO₂ ratio is a candidate operating point. Selectivities are closed as in the hand-built case: methanol and methane as reported, entries below the reporting limit set to zero, and the CO-like residual equal to one minus the methanol and methane selectivities; when methane is not reported, it is one minus the methanol and CO selectivities if CO is reported and zero otherwise. Comparison groups contain the entries of one paper at the same pressure, H₂/CO₂ ratio and space velocity. Space-time yield per gram of catalyst defines the paper leaderboard: the printed value when every entry of a group prints it, otherwise the value derived from the mass space velocity, otherwise from the volumetric space velocity with a bulk density of 1.0 g mL⁻¹ (0.5 and 2.0 g mL⁻¹ tested). The plant leaderboard is the net production cost at each entry's own temperature, pressure and H₂/CO₂ ratio, with catalyst mass from the group's productivity basis, recycled CO converted per pass to the smallest extent that keeps the reactor outlet at or below reverse-water-gas-shift equilibrium, and purge optimized over 0.5–40%. Variants use inert CO, 2% purge, single-pass yield, conversion or selectivity as the paper leaderboard, printed values only, and entries whose reported product selectivities sum to at least 95%. Pressure-dependent condensation enters both leaderboards of a group equally because a group shares one pressure. The lower bound used to allocate the full plant optimization evaluates the same economics at each purge level on lower-bound loop flows that hold for any per-pass conversion of recycled CO between 0 and 1: reactor-inlet CO₂ of at least 1/[X(S_MeOH + (1 − p)S_CO)] per mole of methanol, three moles of H₂ per mole of methanol and four per mole of methane, the loop methane and nitrogen that the purge fraction p sets, and at least two moles of crude liquid; every equipment term rises with its flow, so the minimum over purge levels bounds the plant cost from below. Within each group the paper's leader is evaluated in full, then the remaining candidates in increasing order of the bound until the next bound exceeds the best cost found.

### Au/TiO₂ rank-preservation control

The Au/TiO₂ control compares 2–6 nm particles under a common operating condition and process mapping. The reference rate, Au loading and dispersion are anchored to Janssens et al.,[18] and the particle-size dependence is taken from the 4.5 wt% Au/TiO₂ series reported by Overbury et al.[19] The broader physical basis for low-temperature Au/TiO₂ activity and particle-size effects follows the established literature.[16,17] Rank preservation is evaluated in 10,000 parameter samples drawn from ranges reported in these studies. An additional analysis introduces candidate-specific kinetic variation and independently optimizes temperature and O₂/CO ratio within the specified ranges derived from the literature.

### Adaptive Catalyst Screening Agent

ACSA fetches each paper through the institutional library service, converts it to page-marked text and page images, and extracts every catalyst entry into a fixed schema in which each number carries its value, unit, qualifier, location and page. Extraction uses one strict-JSON language-model call per paper and pass (gpt-5.5, medium reasoning effort): a main pass over text and page images, a figure pass that digitizes every catalyst-performance point in the main-text figures, and passes over the text, tables and figures of the Supporting Information. A printed main-text value outranks a printed Supporting-Information value, which outranks a plot reading. Units are normalized to the model basis. Prompts contain only the paper itself.

Accuracy is scored against a curated methanol dataset[62] and the 21 Re/TiO₂ entries of the hand-built case. Entries are matched by paper and catalyst name; strict tolerances are ±1 K for temperature, ±1% for pressure and space-time yield, ±0.05 for the H₂/CO₂ ratio and ±0.5 percentage points for conversion and selectivity. Every disagreement between an extracted and a curated value was checked against the paper; curated values contradicted by the printed value are corrected before scoring, and both raw and corrected scores are reported. Extracted entries without a curated partner were checked against the source one by one.

Before new candidates are scored, ACSA runs the hand-built inputs, and the extracted Re/TiO₂ inputs, through the same code path and requires the frozen costs to be reproduced. Candidates are then taken through the forward chain; the descriptor-only lower bound decides which ammonia candidates need the full process optimization, the purge-wise plant bound decides which methanol candidates need the equilibrium-constrained purge optimization, and all candidates were additionally evaluated in full to verify both bounds. The counterfactual and backward calculations are run on the leading candidates only.
