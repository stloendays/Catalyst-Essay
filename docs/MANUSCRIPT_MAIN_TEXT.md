# Process economics reorder catalyst rankings across computed and published catalysts

## Abstract

Catalyst screening ranks materials by descriptors or laboratory performance, whereas industrial selection depends on how catalyst properties propagate through reactor operation and plant economics. We couple catalyst-specific kinetics to candidate-specific process optimization and a catalyst-responsive cost objective, and invert that objective into catalyst-property targets. In ammonia synthesis the intrinsic order Ru > Os > Fe becomes Fe > Ru > Os; Ru would need a 201-fold activity gain, outside the scaling-constrained space, whereas promotion supplies gains of that size and dispersion with recovery brings supported Ru close to Fe. An agent that extracts published data and runs the full chain on every candidate extends this to 1,695 alloy and metal surfaces, 45 methanol studies and 30 ammonia studies. Within the 372-surface subset containing only transition metals outside groups 3–5, the alloys that undercut Fe pair Fe, Co, Ni or Cu with Cr, Mo or W. Within published comparisons, the laboratory leader is not the plant-cost leader in 40% of methanol and 35% of ammonia cases.

## Introduction

Computational catalyst discovery ranks candidate materials by adsorption energies, reactivity descriptors, scaling relations, volcano plots and surrogate models,[1–6] and descriptor-based microkinetic and multiscale models connect those quantities to rates and operating conditions.[6,7,43] Experimental studies rank catalysts by laboratory activity, selectivity and space-time yield. Both rankings stand in for an industrial decision that depends on how catalyst properties propagate through reactor operation, separation, recycle and plant economics. Previous work has linked ammonia descriptors to reactor design and catalyst selection,[32] formulated catalyst and process design as coupled problems[33,36] and shown that laboratory rankings can depend on the test environment.[35] Whether a laboratory-leading catalyst or tested operating point remains economically preferred after process optimization has not been measured across a field.

The leading candidates matter most. A screen can remain well correlated across a large candidate set while changing the material that would actually be selected, and catalyst cost per kilogram can be a poor proxy for cost per unit product once stability and production scale are included.[44] In ammonia synthesis, process-level Fe/Ru studies show that the preferred catalyst and pressure depend on plant scale and loop configuration.[42,58]

Here we propagate catalyst properties forward through kinetics, candidate-specific process optimization and a catalyst-responsive cost objective, and backward from an economic target to the catalyst property it requires, testing whether that property lies within the range allowed by the underlying scaling relations.[33,34,36] We study ammonia synthesis, in which intrinsic activity and metal price set catalyst inventory and operating conditions;[5,11,12] CO₂ hydrogenation to methanol, in which selectivity sets reactant loss, purge, recycle and compression;[13–15] and Au/TiO₂ CO oxidation, a control in which particle size maps monotonically to catalyst requirement.[16–19] Language-model agents can now read the chemical literature and operate computational tools.[20–22,30,31] We use one, the Adaptive Catalyst Screening Agent (ACSA), as the automation layer that extracts catalytic data from publications, reproduces the hand-built cases and runs the same chain on every candidate. This turns statements about a few hand-selected catalysts into a field-level measurement: within the comparisons that published methanol and ammonia papers make themselves, how often is the laboratory leader not the plant-cost leader?

## Results

### Process economics reverse the leading ammonia ranking

The 15-metal ammonia screen ranks Ru, Os and Fe first, second and third in intrinsic activity (Fig. 1c), consistent with the established activity landscape for transition-metal ammonia synthesis.[11,12] After the operating conditions are optimized separately for each catalyst over 14,136 process states and the catalyst-dependent costs are evaluated (Fig. 1a,d,e), the order becomes Fe, Ru and Os, at 15.292, 22.031 and 25.832 US dollars per tonne of NH₃ (Fig. 1b,h). The Spearman correlation across all 15 metals remains 0.929, whereas among the three leaders Spearman rho is -0.50 (Fig. 1f,g): the screen stays correlated while the selected catalyst changes. Selecting Ru instead of Fe would raise the catalyst-dependent cost by 44.1%, the normalized decision regret. The 6.739 US dollars per tonne difference is a small part of the cost of ammonia from state-of-the-art gas-based plants, about 160 US dollars per tonne,[49] but it is the part that catalyst selection controls, 2.34 million US dollars per year for a 1,000 t/d plant (Supplementary Note 1).

### Published laboratory leaders are often not the plant-cost leaders

The hand-built analyses compare three ammonia metals, four methanol states and five Au particle sizes. ACSA scales the same analysis to every candidate that publications or databases provide, while the kinetic, process and economic models remain unchanged (Fig. 2a). It reads each paper and its Supporting Information and records every catalyst entry with its composition, operating conditions, conversion, selectivities and rates, together with the table, figure and page each value comes from. Before it scores any new candidate it must reproduce the frozen results of all three hand-built cases, and a cheap lower bound on cost decides which candidates need the full process optimization. Scored on 50 methanol papers (Supplementary Table 1) against two curated datasets,[62,67] the agent recovers 717 of the 741 curated entries (97%) and achieves field-dependent numerical accuracy: 44 of 44 CO₂ conversions in main-text tables, and 128 of 129 CO₂ conversions, 123 of 128 methanol selectivities and 116 of 121 space-time yields from printed Supporting Information entries, satisfy the stated strict tolerances (Extended Data Fig. 1; Supplementary Note 6). The self-check reproduces the 21 Re/TiO₂ entries to 2.3 × 10⁻¹³ euros per tonne and every pure-metal ammonia cost to 10⁻⁹ US dollars per tonne. In methanol the lower bound spares the full plant optimization for 700 of 906 candidates (77.3%) and still finds the plant-cost leader of all 83 comparisons, at 23% of the compute (Extended Data Fig. 2).

Methanol papers rank catalysts by laboratory performance, most often by space-time yield. ACSA extracted 991 operating points from 45 studies. Within each paper, entries tested at the same pressure, H₂/CO₂ ratio and space velocity form the comparison the paper itself makes, giving 83 comparisons with 906 entries. Each entry was taken through the methanol recycle–economics model at its own conditions, with recycled CO converted to reverse-water-gas-shift equilibrium and the purge optimized for each entry. The space-time-yield leader is not the plant-cost leader in 33 of 83 comparisons (40%; 95% confidence interval 24–55% from resampling papers), spread over 19 of the 44 papers, and 1,019 of 8,458 pairwise orderings (12%) invert (Fig. 2b). Ranking by methanol selectivity disagrees in 63 of 83 comparisons (76%) across 40 papers, with a median regret of 15% where the leaders differ; ranking by conversion disagrees in 16 of 83. Treating recycled CO as inert instead, the opposite bound, raises the space-time-yield disagreement to 46 of 83 comparisons across 37 papers (Supplementary Note 9).

The disagreement is neither a sampling nor a measurement artefact (Extended Data Fig. 3). Of the 33 disagreements, 23 cost at least 1%. Re-measuring every entry 2,000 times with the conversion and selectivity uncertainty derived for the Re/TiO₂ states keeps 15 of them in at least 90% of re-measurements, all with a regret of at least 1% (median 5.9%), whereas measurement noise alone changes a paper's space-time-yield leader in 13 comparisons (95% range 8–19). The disagreement holds for printed values alone (17 of 50 comparisons), for entries whose reported products close to methanol, CO and methane (22 of 61), under resampled plot-reading errors (38.5 disagreeing comparisons on average, 95% range 31–45) and when each catalyst is priced from its composition (31 of 83). The plant model reproduces the hydrogen and CO₂ consumption, carbon efficiency and recycle ratio of published plant assessments and, on the same basis, the break-even methanol price of one of them to within 2.4% (Extended Data Fig. 4).

The plant ranking follows from what each by-product does in a recycle loop (Fig. 2d,e). Recycled CO is converted further, so CO selectivity costs little, whereas methane leaves only with the purge. In a high-pressure Cu/Al₂O₃ study,[65] the space-time-yield leader at 10 MPa (Cu–Ba/Al₂O₃ at 473 K, 62% methanol selectivity, 2.8% conversion) costs 1,170 euros per tonne, against 1,002 for Cu–K/Al₂O₃ at 553 K (0.9% selectivity, 20.7% conversion). The largest regrets, up to 182%, occur in that study, whose productivity leaders run at low single-pass conversion and would need a very large recycle; in the other 43 papers the regret stays below 10%. Selectivity leaders are costly because the most selective entry of a comparison usually runs at low conversion and needs a large catalyst inventory and recycle.

Ammonia papers rank catalysts by rate. ACSA extracted 30 primary ammonia-synthesis papers (Supplementary Table 2), with the Supporting Information of 28 of them. Each catalyst's measured rate is inverted into an effective descriptor on its metal's side of the volcano, and the catalyst is optimized over the full process library with its own metal content. Within each paper, catalysts tested at the same temperature, pressure, H₂/N₂ ratio and space velocity form 124 comparisons. The catalyst with the highest rate per gram of catalyst is not the plant-cost leader in 44 of them (35%; 95% confidence interval 18–52%), in 13 of 28 papers, with a median regret of 11% where the leaders differ (Fig. 2c). In 14 comparisons a catalyst of another metal is the plant-cost leader, in 12 of them an Fe catalyst on the same support with the lower rate (Fig. 2f), and in 17 a commercial fused-iron catalyst that the paper includes as a reference is the plant-cost leader. Across the 73 catalysts tabulated in a review of ammonia-synthesis catalysts,[48] laboratory rate and plant cost are nearly uncorrelated (Spearman ρ = 0.16), and none of these catalysts undercuts the fused-iron benchmark without metal recovery (Extended Data Fig. 5; Supplementary Note 8).

### Bimetallic surfaces that undercut Fe pair a cheap 3d metal with a group-6 metal

To test whether the Fe preference survives a much larger candidate space, ACSA took the 1,796 bimetallic and pure-metal surfaces of a high-throughput adsorption database[60,61] through the ammonia chain (Methods). Among the 372 surfaces that contain only transition metals outside groups 3–5, 138 satisfy the 90 m³ bed-volume limit. Five undercut Fe when the database is bridged linearly onto the model's descriptor and six under an element-anchored bridge; every one of them pairs Fe, Co, Ni or Cu with Cr, Mo or W (Extended Data Fig. 6), the combination that descriptor interpolation has identified for ammonia synthesis.[64] Surfaces with group 3–5 elements, which form stable bulk nitrides, and with sp metals, which lie outside the transition-metal scaling relations of the activity model, are reported as separate layers (Supplementary Table 3). The most active surface ranks 43rd economically (Os₃Pt, 70.9% regret) and at its own price would need a 689-fold activity increase to reach Fe. The descriptor-only lower bound excludes 1,397 of the 1,695 priced surfaces (82.4%) from the full optimization without excluding any that undercut Fe (Supplementary Note 7).

### Metal price and process optimization decide the Fe–Ru ranking

A layer-wise check locates the ammonia reversal. Converting turnover frequency into the active-metal requirement for plant throughput preserves the intrinsic order; charging that metal at its price over a 10-year life gives Fe < Ru < Os, with annualized metal replacement of 0.199 US dollars per tonne for Fe against 33.57 for Ru, before any reactor, compression, refrigeration or equipment cost is added. Setting the Ru price equal to the Fe value of 8 US dollars per kilogram and reoptimizing makes Ru the cheaper catalyst (14.712 against 15.292 US dollars per tonne), and a price sweep with reoptimization at every price places Fe–Ru parity at 163.76 US dollars per kilogram (Fig. 3d). Process optimization then narrows the gap: Ru moves to about 450 °C and 425 bar, where a small bed holds little metal, while Fe operates near 425 °C and 180 bar (Fig. 3b,c). The remaining 6.739 US dollars per tonne comes mainly from fresh-feed compression (+4.632), metal inventory (+1.763), compressor capital (+1.181) and refrigeration (+0.629), partly offset by vessel, recycle-compression and reactor terms (Fig. 3a,e).

The Fe preference holds across the economic range examined. In 5,000 preregistered draws of metal prices, capital cost, electricity price and catalyst lifetime, each with full reoptimization, Fe is cheaper in every draw, P(C_Fe < C_Ru) = 1.000, with a minimum difference of 2.382 US dollars per tonne (Fig. 3h); the regret has 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles of 30.48%, 40.89% and 54.25%. Descriptor uncertainty matters more: across 1,000 descriptor samples Fe ranks first economically in 68.1% and Ru in 31.9%, in most of the latter because the Fe bed exceeds the 90 m³ limit (Fig. 3f,g; Supplementary Note 2).

The benchmark formulation charges Ru as the bed of the industrial fused-iron catalyst. A promoted Ru/C ammonia catalyst exposes 11% of its Ru atoms,[45] against fewer than 1% of the Fe atoms in reduced fused iron;[46] the commercial catalyst carries about 8 wt% Ru,[71] and 89–98% of the Ru in spent carbon-supported Ru ammonia catalyst is recovered.[47] Read at the effective Ru price p(1 − r)/u, 147–490 US dollars per kilogram for 90–97% recovery, Ru costs 15.24–15.67 US dollars per tonne of NH₃, from 0.05 below to 0.38 above Fe (Fig. 3d). With the supported catalyst's own bed (8 wt% Ru at 430–550 kg m⁻³), it costs 15.63–16.01, 0.33–0.72 above Fe; in a KAAP-type loop at about 9 MPa with a −20 °C refrigerated condenser,[70] it costs 18.49–19.08 against 19.07 for fused iron, below Fe at 97% recovery in every bed and, in the denser bed, already at 90%. On its own bed, supported and recovered Ru thus reaches the Fe cost in the low-pressure loop for which it was developed and stays above it in the high-pressure Fe loop. Sampling dispersion, recovery and bed density turns the decision into a probability: Fe is cheaper in 69.0% of draws for a commercial Ru/C bed (5–10 wt% Ru, 430–550 kg m⁻³), and in 94.7% with the activities and Ru contents of the 54 measured Ru catalysts (Extended Data Fig. 7).

### Backward design separates the required catalyst-property region from physical reachability

Multiplying Ru intrinsic activity by a factor α and reoptimizing the process at each value, Ru reaches the Fe cost only after a 201.22-fold increase; the 5th–95th percentile requirement under economic uncertainty is 70.78–462-fold (Fig. 4a). The scaling relation allows much less: at most 1.090-fold at 673 K and 2.525-fold across all process conditions, and the lowest strict-scaling Ru cost, 21.398 US dollars per tonne at *E*_N = −1.215 eV, remains above the Fe cost (Fig. 4b). Improving Ru activity along the present scaling relation cannot make Ru competitive.

Measured catalysts show which modifications supply gains of that size (Fig. 4a, bottom). Ba promotion of Ru/C raises the turnover frequency per surface Ru 75-fold and Cs promotion 65-fold,[50] Ba promotion of Ru/BN more than 100-fold at 5 MPa[51] and Cs promotion of Ru/MgO more than 134-fold;[52] the Ba and MgO-supported Cs gains lie within the parity band and the Cs–Ru/C gain just below it, whereas electride and oxyhydride supports give about tenfold[53,54] and confinement inside carbon nanotubes 0.5-fold.[56] Electronic promotion, which changes the energetics beyond a single scaling relation, is the measured route that reaches the parity band.

Lifecycle variables move the target before reachability is tested. Allowing Ru recovery and catalyst lifetime to improve together lowers the activity requirement: at 99% recovery, a 20-year lifetime needs at most a 1.401-fold gain (Fig. 4c). On the strict *E*_N scaling manifold across all 14,136 process states, parity is controlled by the catalyst-loss factor \(q=(1-r)/L\), with \(q^*=4.4068\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{y}^{-1}\). At a 20-year lifetime and 99% recovery the best strict-scaling state costs 15.362 US dollars per tonne, 0.071 US dollars per tonne above Fe, and parity requires 99.119% recovery at 20 years or a 22.69-year lifetime at 99% recovery (Fig. 4d). The backward-design result is thus a narrow property region near the edge of the present scaling-constrained design space rather than a single activity threshold (Supplementary Note 3).

### Selectivity, recycle and purge reshape the methanol ranking

The mechanism behind the methanol field result is visible in four Re/TiO₂ catalyst–temperature states (Fig. 5a,b).[13] By space-time yield per gram of Re the order is 1 wt% Re at 250 °C, 1 wt% Re at 200 °C, 5 wt% Re at 200 °C and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C. After the recycle and separation model at 2% purge, the economic order becomes 5 wt% Re at 200 °C, 1 wt% Re at 250 °C, 1 wt% Re at 200 °C and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C, with net production costs of 943, 962, 967 and 1,258 euros per tonne (Fig. 5c). Selecting the space-time-yield winner costs 1.93% more, and two of six pairwise orderings invert. Methane suppression has a local economic leverage of 0.37579, against 0.05883 for single-pass conversion and 0.00289 for space-time yield (Fig. 5a,g), because methane consumes reactants and accumulates in the loop, raising purge, recycle and compression.[14,15] No purge level between 0.5% and 40% makes the space-time-yield winner the least-cost state (Fig. 5f).

Counterfactual and backward calculations locate the cause. Moving every state's methane selectivity into methanol reverses the correlation to rho = -0.60, whereas equalizing conversion raises it to +1.00. For the space-time-yield winner, no increase in space-time yield reaches parity: unlimited productivity still costs 954.64 euros per tonne against 943.30. Raising its single-pass conversion from 0.23 to 0.279, inside the range measured on the same catalyst family, or reaching complete methanol selectivity does. When each state's own measurement uncertainty is propagated through the plant model, the economic winner keeps first place in 91.2% of 5,000 draws (Fig. 5d; Supplementary Note 4).

### A monotonic catalyst-to-process mapping preserves the ranking

Process evaluation does not change every ranking. In Au/TiO₂ CO oxidation, with the rate anchor of Janssens et al.[18] and the particle-size dependence of Overbury et al.,[19] candidates differ only in particle size from 2 to 6 nm under one operating condition and process model. Intrinsic activity and required catalyst mass preserve the order 2 > 3 > 4 > 5 > 6 nm in all 10,000 parameter samples drawn from the literature ranges (Fig. 6b–d), and in 92.16% when candidate-specific kinetic and operating variations are introduced over 273.15–293.15 K (Fig. 6e). Rankings change when catalyst properties alter downstream operating requirements strongly enough to change relative costs: through catalyst inventory and operating regime in ammonia, and through reactant loss, purge and recycle in methanol (Fig. 6a,f; Supplementary Note 5).

## Discussion

Rank agreement across a screen does not guarantee the same industrial choice. In ammonia the full 15-metal ranking remains strongly correlated while the leading order Ru > Os > Fe becomes Fe > Ru > Os. The reversal occurs where catalyst demand is monetized through metal price and replacement; process reoptimization narrows the Ru penalty by moving Ru to high pressure and a small bed, and shifts the remaining gap into compression and refrigeration. Selecting on the upstream proxy costs 44.1% in the ammonia objective, 1.93% in the methanol case and nothing in the Au/TiO₂ control. A first-order expansion makes the condition explicit. For a higher-is-better upstream metric s and a lower-is-better downstream objective J, Δln J ≈ -ε_s Δln s + Σ_k ε_k Δln z_k, where the z_k are catalyst-dependent process or economic variables; the upstream order inverts when the aggregate downstream penalty exceeds the direct benefit. The transfer index χ_AB = ln(J_A/J_B) / ln(s_A/s_B) is a model-agnostic sign diagnostic of this outcome: +0.085 for Ru versus Fe, +0.015 for the methanol space-time-yield winner versus the cost winner and approximately -1.00 for 2 nm versus 6 nm Au/TiO₂.

The field measurement shows that these reversals are not hand-picked exceptions. Within the comparisons that published papers make themselves, the laboratory leader is not the plant-cost leader in 40% of methanol and 35% of ammonia comparisons. The causes are specific to each process and identifiable. In methanol, methane selectivity and conversion, which set purge and recycle, outweigh productivity, and ranking by selectivity alone, which favours low-conversion operating points, misleads in 76%. In ammonia, iron catalysts tested alongside more active ruthenium catalysts are cheaper to operate. Screening should therefore target the catalyst property that has sufficient downstream leverage to alter the industrial decision, and a laboratory comparison can report the process-level consequence of its leader alongside its rate or yield.

Backward design converts the ammonia mechanism into materials targets. Ru would need a 201-fold activity gain, far beyond the 2.5-fold reachable along its scaling relation, yet measured electronic promotion supplies gains inside the parity band, and dispersion with metal recovery brings supported Ru to the Fe cost only in a low-pressure loop at high recovery. Independent process analyses describe the same trade-off between lower-pressure operation, ammonia-separation refrigeration and the price, loading, lifetime and recycling of Ru.[57–59]

ACSA adds no new scientific model; its contribution is scale. Extraction reaches 97% curated-entry recall, with numerical accuracy depending on the field and source format; the agent reproduces the hand-built cases before it scores new candidates, and cost bounds spare most of the full optimizations without losing a leader. The framework is modular: formulation-specific catalyst physics and higher-fidelity kinetic, process and economic models can replace the present ones without changing the decision logic, so every comparison a catalysis paper reports can be taken to its plant-level consequence.

## Methods

### Multiscale decision analysis

Each catalytic system is evaluated with a process-level objective matched to the catalyst-to-process pathway under study. For ammonia, the economic objective contains the terms that respond directly to catalyst identity and catalytic performance: catalyst inventory and replacement, reactor requirements, compression, refrigeration and pressure-dependent equipment. Common upstream H₂/N₂ supply contributions are held fixed so that they do not dilute the catalyst-dependent decision signal. The methanol model evaluates net production cost through its recycle and separation process, while the Au/TiO₂ control maps particle-size-dependent activity to required catalyst mass under a common process model. Cross-system comparison is made at the level of ranking propagation and coupling topology rather than by equating the absolute economic objectives of different reactions.

Within each system, rankings before and after process evaluation are compared using Spearman and Kendall correlations, pairwise inversions, top-rank identity and normalized decision regret. For a lower-is-better downstream objective J, regret is defined as R = (J_upstream-winner - J_downstream-winner) / J_downstream-winner. Analyses of the leading candidates are reported separately from full-set statistics so that changes at the top of the ranking are not obscured by agreement among weaker candidates. Backward design varies a selected catalyst property, reoptimizes the process and identifies the value required for economic parity. This value is then compared with the range accessible under the corresponding catalyst-property relation or scaling relation. Perturbation tests change one predefined input while preserving the other model assumptions and repeating the same downstream optimization.

### Ammonia synthesis

The ammonia calculation includes the 15-metal activity screen, scaling and kinetic relations, catalyst productivity and inventory, candidate-specific process optimization, pressure-dependent equipment capital and seven catalyst-dependent cost components. Temperature, pressure and separator temperature are reoptimized for each candidate over the full 14,136-state process grid. Descriptor uncertainty is propagated through 1,000 samples; Fe feasibility is evaluated against the 90 m³ bed-volume limit, and economic winner, preservation of the atomistic winner and top-three membership are recorded separately. The S1 source contains Fe terrace energetics but no Fe step-site row, so the Fe step descriptor is obtained from the 14-metal terrace-to-step scaling relation. Leave-one-out validation and prediction-interval propagation are reported in Supplementary Table 4; the regression residual standard error is 0.2271 eV, which is the Fe descriptor uncertainty used in the descriptor Monte Carlo analysis. The atomistic reference conditions (673 K, 24.5 bar N₂, 74.25 bar H₂ and 1 bar NH₃) and step-site descriptor framework follow the Wang–Abild-Pedersen Dataset S1 source.[39] Under those conditions, the source study places Fe and Ru close to the activity plateau and undercoordinated Os close to the volcano maximum.[39] The descriptor-level reconstruction recovers the same high-activity metal set, with Ru, Os and Fe as its leading three candidates, providing the ranking fidelity required for the downstream decision analysis. The Fe process optimum (425 °C, 180 bar) lies within the conventional industrial Fe Haber–Bosch operating envelope discussed in the ammonia-catalysis and process literature.[40,41] The resulting catalyst-specific optima quantify how intrinsic energetics propagate into operating severity and economic consequence within a common, controlled process model. Published Fe/Ru loop studies provide independent process-level context for these trends despite differences in catalyst formulation, plant scale and flowsheet design.[42] Pressure-CAPEX and equipment-cost diagnostics perturb the compressor, vessel, cost-index and capital-recovery assumptions by approximately 10–25%; the Fe > Ru > Os economic order and the activity-only reachability verdict are retained, demonstrating that the central decision is robust to these model-form perturbations.

For the Ru-price test, only the Ru metal purchase price is changed. It is first set equal to the Fe value of 8 US dollars per kilogram, after which Ru is reoptimized over the full process grid. The Ru-price sweep repeats this optimization at 241 logarithmically spaced prices between 1 and 3 × 10^5 US dollars per kilogram together with the two reference prices. Fe-Ru parity is located by bisection in log price. The baseline Fe-Ru cost difference is decomposed into the same seven cost components.

The preregistered economic Monte Carlo analysis uses 5,000 samples with seed 20260920. Fe and Ru metal-price multipliers are sampled independently from a log-uniform distribution over 0.5–2.0. A common CAPEX multiplier is sampled uniformly over 0.8–1.2, electricity price uniformly over 20–100 US dollars per megawatt-hour, and catalyst lifetime uniformly over 5–20 years. Fe and Ru are reoptimized for every sample. The Ru activity multiplier required for parity, α*, is recalculated against the sample-specific reoptimized Fe cost. The complete draw sequence has been reconstructed from the frozen seed and priors and reproduces the previously reported Fe/Ru cost and cost-gap quantiles exactly; normalized regret is then computed draw-by-draw as (C_Ru − C_Fe)/C_Fe. The supporting descriptor-correlation sensitivity does not modify this preregistered Monte Carlo. Instead, it preserves the frozen Fe normal marginal and the ±0.15 eV Ru/Os uniform marginals using a Gaussian copula with latent pairwise correlations from 0 to 0.9, and evaluates atomistic ordering on the frozen 673 K one-descriptor volcano. A separate common-bias sweep applies the same additive shift to Ru, Os and Fe descriptors. Neither sensitivity is used to report a new downstream economic probability. The actual-catalyst Monte Carlo adds inputs drawn from a second generator (seed 20261006), so that every preregistered draw keeps its values: the Ru dispersion ratio u = D_Ru/f_Fe, log-uniform over 11–50, with D_Ru from 11% for a Ba–Cs–K-promoted Ru/C catalyst[45] to about 50% for 2 nm Ru particles in Ba-promoted Ru/C[68] and f_Fe = 1% for the exposed Fe atoms of reduced fused iron;[46] Ru recovery uniform over 90–97%;[47] Ru content of the supported bed uniform over 5–10 wt%,[71] from a third generator (seed 20261007); and bed density uniform over 430–550 kg m⁻³, the range derived from a promoted Ru/C laboratory bed[72]. In the benchmark-bed variant the Ru price is lowered to p(1 − r)/u, as in Fig. 3d; in the supported-bed variant the Ru activity per kilogram of Ru is multiplied by u, the Ru charge is replaced at (1 − r), and the bed volume follows from the Ru content and bed density. The measured-activity variant replaces u by the effective descriptor and residual multiplier of a randomly drawn measured Ru catalyst together with its Ru content.

For backward design, Ru intrinsic activity is varied directly and the full process is reoptimized at each activity multiplier. Reachability is evaluated separately along the descriptor path defined by the ammonia scaling relation and over the full process grid. For the joint activity–lifecycle target analysis, catalyst lifetime and Ru recovery are represented through the effective replacement-metal price, P_eff = P_Ru(1 − r)(10 y/L). A conservative target region is constructed from the 53 distinct process states already visited by the independently reoptimized Ru price sweep: for each state, catalyst demand is rescaled by a direct activity multiplier and the volume- and pressure-dependent cost terms are recalculated. Restricting the optimization to this state subset can only overestimate the activity improvement required for parity, so the reported multipliers are upper bounds on the backward target.

Physical reachability of that target is then evaluated on the strict *E*_N scaling manifold using all 14,136 process states. At fixed process state, *E*_N affects cost through TOF and therefore the required active-metal inventory and bed volume; the metal-replacement, reactor-volume and pressure-vessel terms are monotone in that requirement, whereas state-specific compression and refrigeration costs are independent of *E*_N. The cost-minimizing descriptor for each state is therefore its TOF-maximizing value on the canonical 0.005-eV scaling grid. Taking the lower envelope across all states gives the lifecycle parity boundary as a function of \(q=(1-r)/L\). The canonical strict-scaling minimum is reproduced before the lifecycle calculation is accepted. The kinetic and scaling relations follow the established transition-metal ammonia-synthesis literature.[5,11,12,39]

The actual-catalyst analysis reads the fully reoptimized Ru price sweep at the effective price p(1 − r)/u, where u is the ratio of exposed metal fractions of the supported catalyst and fused iron (u = 0.11/0.01 = 11, a lower bound) and r the recovered fraction (90–97%); the activity multiple for parity is recomputed with full reoptimization at every price. The KAAP comparison restricts both catalysts to the 90 bar, −20 °C separator states. A supported bed holding 8 wt% Ru (5–10 wt%) instead of the common formulation changes only the reactor term; recomputing it for bed densities of 430–550 kg m⁻³ adds 0.10–0.13 US dollars per tonne without recovery and 0.25–0.49 with 90–97% recovery (0.12–0.47 in the KAAP loop) at 8 wt%. Literature activity gains are taken only from comparisons with a reference Ru catalyst in the same study and are placed on the same multiplier axis without entering the cost model.

### Bimetallic surface extension

N adsorption energies (0.5 N₂(g) + * → N*) for the 1,796 surfaces of Mamun et al.[60] were retrieved from Catalysis-Hub;[61] two independent retrievals give identical files of 5,696 adsorption sites. The most stable site of each surface defines its terrace descriptor. A linear fit over the 14 pure metals present in both this database and the source of the model's descriptors (slope 0.845, R² = 0.973, RMS 0.22 eV) maps it onto that source's terrace scale, and the 14-metal terrace-to-step relation already used for Fe (step E_N = 1.1547 × terrace E_N − 0.1701 eV) gives the step-site descriptor. The element-anchored bridge adds to this value each element's offset between its frozen step descriptor and its own bridged value, weighted by atomic fraction. Per-state activity is read from the cached response surface of the frozen 14,136-state library; the cost uses the alloy's mole-weighted molar mass and mass-weighted metal price and is minimized over all states within the 90 m³ bed limit. The 15 model metals keep their frozen prices; 21 other elements take USGS annual-average prices per kilogram of contained metal (2025; Hg 2024),[63] with oxide, ore and ferroalloy quotations divided by the metal mass fraction. Tc has no market price and its 101 surfaces are not costed. Surfaces are labelled transition-metal, group 3–5 (Sc, Y, La, Ti, Zr, Hf, V, Nb, Ta) or sp-metal (Al, Zn, Cd, Hg, Ga, In, Tl, Sn, Pb, Bi) and scored per layer. The descriptor-only lower bound for each candidate combines its best-state activity, the smallest reactor term and the cheapest process state. For the surfaces below Fe and the highest-activity surface of each bridge, the counterfactual sets the metal price to the Fe price, and backward design solves for the activity multiplier and the metal price at which the reoptimized cost equals that of Fe; reachability is the lowest cost at any descriptor on the strict scaling range with the surface's molar mass and price.

### Measured ammonia catalysts

Tables 1–6 of the review[48] were extracted page by page (text and page image, two independent passes); disagreements and values absent from the page text were resolved on the page images. Rates are taken per gram of catalyst as printed, converted from mL h⁻¹ g⁻¹, or derived from the printed outlet NH₃ fraction and space velocity; rows printed twice are counted once. Rows were checked against the primary papers, and ten transcription errors in nine rows were replaced by the papers' values. Catalysts with one model metal and a stated metal content enter the chain: 83 of 161 rows. The model's rate per gram of metal is F_CAL·TOF(E_N)/M, with F_CAL the fused-iron plant calibration; each catalyst's measured rate per gram of metal fixes the effective descriptor E_eff at which the model reproduces it at the laboratory condition (H₂/N₂ = 3), on the side of the volcano where its metal lies, with a residual multiplier where the rate exceeds the volcano top. Because the model's NH₃ formation free energy lies 0.064–0.065 eV per NH₃ above experiment at 573–773 K, the laboratory NH₃ fraction (half the outlet value) is entered at the same approach to equilibrium as in the experiment, using the experimental equilibrium constant.[66] The catalyst is then optimized over the 14,136 process states with descriptor E_eff, the molar mass and price of its metal, its own metal content and a bed density of 1,000 kg m⁻³ (fused iron: 71.51 wt% and 2,500 kg m⁻³), without metal recovery unless stated. The primary set contains catalysts measured at 300–500 °C under steady thermal operation with an outlet below 90% of equilibrium; chemical-looping, field- or microwave-assisted rows are reported separately. Bed densities of 500 and 2,500 kg m⁻³, catalysts measured at 5 MPa or above, and a constant multiplier on the metal's own TOF in place of E_eff were tested. The primary-paper set contains 30 papers on thermal ammonia synthesis that compare at least two catalysts or conditions with a stated metal content, 28 of them with Supporting Information; extraction uses the methanol passes with an ammonia schema, and mapping and process optimization follow the review chain. Comparison groups contain the entries of one paper at the same temperature, pressure, H₂/N₂ ratio and space velocity; entries with the same metal, metal content and rate (three significant figures) are counted once, and a catalyst is the fused-iron reference only when it is a commercial fused or wüstite iron catalyst without a stated support or added component. The paper leaderboard is the rate per gram of catalyst in all 124 groups, and the confidence interval is a paper-cluster bootstrap with 10,000 resamples.

### CO₂-to-methanol

The methanol analysis uses a recycle and separation model, calibrated on a published CO₂-to-methanol loop,[69] applied to four Re/TiO₂ states derived from the 1 and 5 wt% catalysts reported by Gothe et al.[13] The reference comparison uses 2% purge. Upstream rankings are reconstructed from space-time yield per gram of Re, single-pass methanol yield and space-time yield per gram of catalyst; the downstream ranking uses net production cost. Re purchase price and catalyst replacement are not included in the reference objective. Purge is varied from 0.5% to 40% over 396 levels, with an additional optimization in which purge is selected separately for each catalyst state. Local economic leverage for space-time yield, conversion and methane suppression is evaluated at the 5 wt% Re, 250 °C state. The process interpretation follows established CO₂-to-methanol assessments in which recycle, compression and separation materially affect plant performance.[14,15] The plant model was checked against published process assessments and pilot-plant data (Supplementary Table 5; Extended Data Fig. 4): at 22–33% per-pass conversion its hydrogen and CO₂ consumption (0.193–0.208 and 1.41–1.47 tonnes per tonne of methanol), carbon efficiency (0.93–0.98) and recycle ratio (2.6–5.4) lie within the reported ranges, and at the operating point and price assumptions of ref. 14 it reproduces its break-even methanol price, production cost plus capital recovery, to within 2.4%. Setting the loop pressure drop, recycle flow and prices to reference values leaves the space-time-yield disagreement at 32–34 of 83 comparisons.

The measurement Monte Carlo samples the CO₂ conversion and selectivities of each catalyst state from that state's own uncertainty and re-solves the full methanol plant model in each of 5,000 draws (seed 20261005), with every cost parameter held at its reference value. The source reports single integer values, so the uncertainty is built from the source data: the reporting resolution (±0.5 percentage points, with '<1%' sampled over 0–1%), a 5.2% relative uncertainty in conversion derived from the consistency between space-time yield and conversion across the 21 reported runs, and a 0.85-percentage-point exchange between methanol and methane selectivity derived from the 11 runs of a single catalyst. Space-time yield follows conversion and methanol selectivity. Halving or doubling every width retains the economic winner in 99.3% or 78.4% of draws. The 1 wt% Re, 250 °C state uses the Table 3 selectivities of 97% methanol, 1% CO and 1% CH₄.

Counterfactuals remove one candidate difference at a time (methane selectivity moved to methanol; conversion set to the four-state mean) and re-solve the plant at the reference purge. Backward design changes one property of the space-time-yield winner until its cost equals the economic optimum.

### Published methanol comparisons

Every extracted record with CO₂ conversion, methanol selectivity, temperature, pressure and H₂/CO₂ ratio is a candidate operating point. Selectivities are closed as in the hand-built case: methanol and methane as reported, entries below the reporting limit set to zero, and the CO-like residual equal to one minus the methanol and methane selectivities; when methane is not reported, it is one minus the methanol and CO selectivities if CO is reported and zero otherwise. Comparison groups contain the entries of one paper at the same pressure, H₂/CO₂ ratio and space velocity. Entries whose feed adds water or CO to CO₂ and H₂ are excluded, because the plant model takes a dry CO₂/H₂ make-up. Space-time yield per gram of catalyst defines the paper leaderboard: the printed value when every entry of a group prints it and the ratio of printed to conversion-derived productivity varies by less than a factor of 3 within the group, otherwise the value derived from the mass space velocity, otherwise from the volumetric space velocity with a bulk density of 1.0 g mL⁻¹ (0.5 and 2.0 g mL⁻¹ tested). The plant leaderboard is the net production cost at each entry's own temperature, pressure and H₂/CO₂ ratio, with catalyst mass from the group's productivity basis, recycled CO converted per pass to the smallest extent that keeps the reactor outlet at or below reverse-water-gas-shift equilibrium, and purge optimized over 0.5–40%. Variants use inert CO, 2% purge, single-pass yield, conversion or selectivity as the paper leaderboard, printed values only, and entries whose reported product selectivities sum to at least 95%. Pressure-dependent condensation enters both leaderboards of a group equally because a group shares one pressure. The lower bound used to allocate the full plant optimization evaluates the same economics at each purge level on lower-bound loop flows that hold for any per-pass conversion of recycled CO between 0 and 1: reactor-inlet CO₂ of at least 1/[X(S_MeOH + (1 − p)S_CO)] per mole of methanol, three moles of H₂ per mole of methanol and four per mole of methane, the loop methane and nitrogen that the purge fraction p sets, and at least two moles of crude liquid; every equipment term rises with its flow, so the minimum over purge levels bounds the plant cost from below. Within each group the paper's leader is evaluated in full, then the remaining candidates in increasing order of the bound until the next bound exceeds the best cost found. Sampling uncertainty is a paper-cluster bootstrap with 10,000 resamples. Measurement uncertainty re-draws every entry 2,000 times with the Re/TiO₂ basis above (5.2% relative conversion error, a 0.85-percentage-point methanol–methane selectivity exchange and, for printed space-time yields, an independent 5.2% relative error), at one, two and four times these widths, and rebuilds both leaderboards from the same draw; plot-reading errors are resampled from the agent's adjudicated plot readings. Plant costs of perturbed entries come from an additive per-coordinate response surface built from exact model runs, with draws outside its grid run exactly; 6 of 1,660 group verdicts differ from exact runs of complete replicates. The catalyst-price variant prices each catalyst from its extracted composition, with metal contents at the model and 2025 commodity prices[63] plus a base cost that gives commercial Cu/ZnO/Al₂O₃ 18.1 euros per kilogram,[69] and charges replacement per tonne of inventory.

### Au/TiO₂ rank-preservation control

The Au/TiO₂ control compares 2–6 nm particles under a common operating condition and process mapping. The reference rate, Au loading and dispersion are anchored to Janssens et al.,[18] and the particle-size dependence is taken from the 4.5 wt% Au/TiO₂ series reported by Overbury et al.[19] The broader physical basis for low-temperature Au/TiO₂ activity and particle-size effects follows the established literature.[16,17] Rank preservation is evaluated in 10,000 parameter samples drawn from ranges reported in these studies. An additional analysis introduces candidate-specific kinetic variation and independently optimizes temperature and O₂/CO ratio within the specified ranges derived from the literature.

### Adaptive Catalyst Screening Agent

ACSA fetches each paper through the institutional library service, converts it to page-marked text and page images, and extracts every catalyst entry into a fixed schema in which each number carries its value, unit, qualifier, location and page. Extraction uses one strict-JSON language-model call per paper and pass (gpt-5.5, medium reasoning effort): a main pass over text and page images, a figure pass that digitizes every catalyst-performance point in the main-text figures, and passes over the text, tables and figures of the Supporting Information. A printed main-text value outranks a printed Supporting-Information value, which outranks a plot reading. Units are normalized to the model basis. Prompts contain only the paper itself.

Accuracy is scored against two curated methanol datasets[62,67] and the 21 Re/TiO₂ entries of the hand-built case. Entries are matched by paper and catalyst name, or, for the dataset that lists compositions instead of names,[67] by paper, operating conditions and elemental composition, never by the values being scored; a pair with an equally admissible alternative partner counts for recall but not for value accuracy. Strict tolerances are ±1 K for temperature, ±1% for pressure and space-time yield, ±0.05 for the H₂/CO₂ ratio and ±0.5 percentage points for conversion and selectivity. Disagreements between extracted and curated values were checked against the paper in 12 papers; curated values contradicted by the printed value (331 cells and 8 rows) are corrected before scoring, and both raw and corrected scores are reported. Among entries without a curated partner, 150 of 179 from the first 18 papers were manually checked against their sources; a further random sample of 87 entries from the 31 additional papers was checked.

Before new candidates are scored, ACSA runs the hand-built inputs of all three systems, and the extracted Re/TiO₂ and Au/TiO₂ inputs, through the same code path and requires the frozen results to be reproduced; the batch runs do not start otherwise. Every extracted Au/TiO₂ input must also equal the hand-built value, because the dispersion enters the chain only as a ratio and cannot be checked through the outputs. A value that the source prints twice with different numbers is accepted in either form: the Au loading appears as 4.41 wt% in the text and 4.40 wt% in Table 1 of the source paper,[18] which changes the required Au mass by 0.2% and no ranking quantity. Candidates are then taken through the forward chain; the descriptor-only lower bound decides which ammonia candidates need the full process optimization, the purge-wise plant bound decides which methanol candidates need the equilibrium-constrained purge optimization, and all candidates were additionally evaluated in full to verify both bounds. The counterfactual and backward calculations are run on the leading candidates only.
