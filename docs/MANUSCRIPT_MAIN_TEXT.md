# Laboratory catalyst leaders are often not the plant-level optimum

## Abstract

Catalysts are ranked by descriptors or laboratory performance, yet a plant selects the catalyst with the lowest production cost at optimized operation. We couple catalyst kinetics to process optimization and costing responsive to the catalyst, and invert the chain for the properties that cost parity requires. In ammonia synthesis, descriptor and measured kinetics agree that Ru, the most active metal, must cost over 90% less to match iron, or become 16–115 times more active, far beyond what its scaling relation allows. With measured rate laws, supported Ru/C with metal recovery undercuts fused iron whenever its Ru is financed below 4.6–6.5% per year, and the least-cost converter places fused iron upstream of Ru/C, as industrial Ru converters do. An extraction agent takes reported catalysts through the same chain: each at its best measured point and with the catalyst inventory a plant would choose, the catalyst with the highest space-time yield is not the plant-level optimum in 21 of 63 methanol comparisons (33%), nor is the fastest catalyst in 12 of 44 ammonia comparisons (27%).

## Introduction

Every catalyst is chosen twice. It is chosen first in a laboratory or a computation, where it is ranked by an adsorption energy, a turnover frequency (TOF) or a space-time yield (STY), and again in a plant, where it is chosen by what it costs to make a tonne of product. Computational screens rank catalysts by descriptors, scaling relations, volcano plots and surrogate models,[1–6] microkinetic and multiscale models link these quantities to rates and operating conditions,[6–8] and experimental papers rank their catalysts by activity, selectivity and STY. All of these rankings stand in for the second choice, in which a catalyst acts through reactor operation, separation, recycle and cost. Descriptors have been linked to reactor design for ammonia synthesis,[9] catalyst and process design have been posed as coupled problems,[10,11] and laboratory rankings are known to depend on the test environment.[12] What a plant acts on, however, is the identity of the leader. A screen can stay correlated while the catalyst a plant would build changes, and the price of a catalyst per kilogram says little about its cost per unit product once stability and scale enter.[13] Process studies of Fe and Ru ammonia catalysts find that the preferred catalyst depends on plant scale and loop configuration.[14,15] How often the two choices disagree, across the comparisons that catalysis papers make, has not been measured.

We follow catalyst properties forward through kinetics, process optimization for each candidate and a cost model responsive to the catalyst, and backward from an economic target to the catalyst property it requires, asking whether that property lies within reach of the underlying scaling relations.[10,11,16] Three optima mark the way. The atomic optimum is set by a descriptor and the intrinsic TOF, the laboratory optimum is the leader of the comparison a paper makes, and the plant-level optimum is the catalyst with the lowest cost once its operation has been optimized under stated process and economic conditions (Methods). Ammonia synthesis, in which intrinsic activity and metal price set the catalyst inventory and operating conditions,[5,17,18] runs from the atomic to the plant-level optimum. CO₂ hydrogenation to methanol, in which selectivity sets reactant loss, purge, recycle and compression,[19–21] runs from the laboratory to the plant-level optimum, and Au/TiO₂ CO oxidation, in which particle size maps monotonically onto catalyst requirement,[22–25] is the control (Fig. 1a). An independent reactor study of CO₂ methanation[26] tests the same reasoning outside our models. Language model agents can now read the chemical literature and operate computational tools.[27–31] We use one, the Adaptive Catalyst Screening Agent (ACSA), to take every catalyst reported in the literature through the same chain and so count how often the laboratory leader is not the plant-level optimum.

## Results

### Ru must cost over 90% less to match iron

Ammonia synthesis offers the cleanest case. The 15-metal screen ranks Ru, Os and Fe first, second and third in intrinsic activity (Fig. 1c), as established for transition-metal ammonia synthesis.[17,18] Ru turns over 607 times faster than Fe but costs 6,732 times more per kilogram (Fig. 1e). That a precious metal loses on cost surprises no one. What a plant needs to know is how large the gap is and what would close it. Once the operating conditions are optimized separately for each catalyst over 14,510 process states and the catalyst-dependent costs are counted (Fig. 1a,d), the order becomes Fe, Ru and Os, at 11.52, 16.63 and 19.75 US dollars per tonne of NH₃ (Fig. 1b,h). The screen as a whole survives, with a Spearman correlation of 0.911 across all 15 metals, while its top reverses, with ρ = −0.50 among the three leaders (Fig. 1f,g). Choosing Ru would raise the catalyst-dependent cost by 44.4%. The 5.12 US dollars per tonne at stake is a small part of the roughly 160 US dollars per tonne of ammonia from state-of-the-art gas-based plants,[32] but it is the part that catalyst selection controls, 1.77 million US dollars a year for a 1,000 t/d plant (Supplementary Note 1). Closing it would take a 99% lower Ru price or a 115-fold activity gain, and measured rate laws for the industrial pair give 94% or 16-fold, again far beyond what the scaling relation allows. A supported Ru/C catalyst with metal recovery, by contrast, closes the gap once its metal is financed below 4.6–6.5% per year. The sections below derive these boundaries and show that the cheapest converter then places Ru/C behind fused iron, as industry does.

### Laboratory leaders are often not the plant-cost leaders

Is a laboratory leader that loses in the plant a curiosity of one screen, or the rule for the comparisons that catalysis papers make every day? To find out, ACSA applies the same kinetic, process and economic models to every candidate that papers and databases provide (Fig. 2a). It records each catalyst entry of a paper and its Supporting Information with the location of each value, reproduces the results of all three hand-built cases before it scores a new candidate, and uses a lower bound on cost to decide which candidates need the full process optimization. Against two curated methanol datasets[33,34] the agent recovers 719 of 741 entries (97%) and transcribes 92–100% of printed values correctly (Extended Data Fig. 1; Supplementary Note 2). In the methanol loop without an inlet limit, the cost bound spares 62.3% of the full plant optimizations without missing a single plant-cost leader (Extended Data Fig. 2).

Methanol papers compare their catalysts by STY, the productivity metric that data-driven models of the field also predict.[34] Two extractions supply 81 comparisons within papers, 632 entries tested at a common pressure, H₂/CO₂ ratio and space velocity in 50 papers (Supplementary Tables 1 and 1b). Each entry enters a methanol recycle–economics model at its own conditions. As in a working loop, the reactor inlet is held to at most 6.86% CO, methane and N₂, the limit of the loop that anchors the cost model,[35] and the purge is optimized within it. Industrial loops purge to keep inert gases from accumulating, because they dilute the reactants, slow the synthesis and lower the productivity of the catalyst,[36,37] and loop designs carry 4.4–9.8% CO, methane and N₂ at the reactor inlet. A plant, like a chemist, chooses the best operating point of each catalyst and how much of it to load, so each catalyst enters at its best measured point and may load more catalyst to raise its conversion towards equilibrium (Methods). What counts as a different catalyst was fixed before anything was counted.

In 21 of the 63 comparisons that contain at least two catalysts (33%; 95% confidence interval 22–46%), from 19 papers, the catalyst with the highest STY is not the one a plant would build, and in 10 of them building it would cost more than 5% extra (Fig. 2b). Held at laboratory conversion, the count rises to 26 of 63 (41%). Between a third and two-fifths of the catalysts that a paper's own STY data rank first are therefore not the ones a plant would build. Across inlet limits of 5–10%, the range of industrial loop designs, the leaders differ in 15–25 of 62–63 comparisons with more catalyst loaded and in 23–28 at laboratory conversion (Supplementary Note 3). Counting every operating point at laboratory conversion, the STY leader and the plant-cost leader differ in 50 of 81 comparisons (Supplementary Note 3). The additional cases are changes of operating point, and temperature is the clearest of them: of 112 catalysts measured at several temperatures, 82 are cheapest to run at another temperature than the one that gives their highest STY, 75 of them at a lower one, by a median of 50 °C. The disagreements survive measurement noise, with 42 of the 50 persisting in at least 90% of 2,000 re-measurements (Extended Data Fig. 3). They also survive every product closure, catalyst price and variant of the plant terms we tested (Supplementary Note 3). The plant model itself reproduces the hydrogen and CO₂ consumption, carbon efficiency and recycle ratio of earlier plant assessments and the break-even methanol price of one of them to within 2.4% (Extended Data Fig. 4).

The by-products decide (Fig. 2d,e). At the cost-optimal purge, recycled CO is converted little beyond what the shift and CO-hydrogenation equilibria require, so it accumulates: without an inlet limit the reactor feed would carry a median 8.8% CO, against 1.3–4.7% in industrial loop designs. Holding the feed to a working composition costs purge, and the purge a catalyst needs grows with the CO and methane it makes. The plant-cost leader is the more selective catalyst in 40 of the 50 disagreements. Without the limit the leaders differ in only 25 of 81 comparisons, and between catalysts in 16 of 63 at laboratory conversion and 8 with more catalyst loaded, while limiting methane and N₂ alone gives 28. It is CO build-up, the accumulation that plants purge to avoid, that turns a laboratory leader into a costly choice. InNi₃C₀.₅ shows the trade on four supports. On m-ZrO₂ it gives the highest STY (8.5% conversion, 88% methanol selectivity, 12% CO) but needs a 4.0% purge and costs 1,289 euros per tonne, whereas on SiO₂ (2.2% conversion, 95% selectivity, 5% CO) it runs at 0.6% purge for 1,138. No single laboratory metric replaces the plant model. Ranking by conversion disagrees in 54 of 81 comparisons and ranking by methanol selectivity in 30, and a high-pressure Cu/Al₂O₃ study that supplies 8 comparisons[38] gives the largest regrets without the limit.

Ammonia papers crown the catalyst with the highest rate. ACSA extracted 52 primary ammonia synthesis papers (Supplementary Tables 2 and 2b), inverted each catalyst's measured rate into an effective descriptor on its metal's side of the volcano and optimized the process for it with its own metal content (Methods). In 12 of 44 comparisons (27%; 95% confidence interval 14–42%) the fastest catalyst is not the cheapest to run, 8 of them by more than 5% (Fig. 2c). Three times the winner is an Fe catalyst on the same support as the faster Ru (Fig. 2f). Five times it is the commercial fused-iron catalyst that the authors included as a reference and that their new catalyst outperformed in the laboratory, and the plant would keep the benchmark. Across the 73 catalysts of a review of ammonia synthesis catalysts,[39] laboratory rate and plant cost are uncorrelated, with a Spearman ρ of 0.07 (Extended Data Fig. 5; Supplementary Note 4).

### Selectivity, recycle and purge reshape the methanol ranking

Four Re/TiO₂ catalyst–temperature states show the mechanism in full (Fig. 3a,b).[19] By STY per gram of Re, 1 wt% Re at 250 °C leads. Through the recycle and separation loop at 2% purge, 5 wt% Re at 200 °C, third by STY, is cheapest, at 943 euros per tonne against 962 (Fig. 3c,e), and no purge between 0.5% and 40% makes the STY winner the least-cost state (Fig. 3f). In the inlet-limited loop of Fig. 2d another state is cheapest, and the STY leader loses there too. Which state wins depends on how the loop is run, but the STY winner loses either way. The reason is leverage. Methane suppression has a local economic leverage of 0.376, against 0.0588 for single-pass conversion and 0.00289 for STY (Fig. 3a,g), because methane consumes reactants and accumulates in the loop, raising purge, recycle and compression.[20,21] Moving every state's methane selectivity into methanol reverses the rank correlation to ρ = −0.60, whereas equalizing conversion raises it to +1.00. For the STY winner, even unlimited productivity costs 955 euros per tonne, still above 943, whereas a single-pass conversion of 0.279 instead of 0.23, inside the range measured on the same catalyst family, or complete methanol selectivity closes the gap. The economic winner keeps first place in 91.2% of 5,000 draws of each state's own measurement uncertainty (Fig. 3d; Supplementary Note 5).

### Alloy surfaces that undercut Fe pair a cheap 3d metal with a group-6 metal

The same chain can search where no paper has looked. ACSA took the 1,796 bimetallic and pure-metal surfaces of a high-throughput adsorption database[40,41] through the ammonia model (Methods). Of the 372 surfaces made only of transition metals outside groups 3–5, 137 fit the 90 m³ limit on bed volume, and eight undercut Fe when the database is bridged linearly onto the model's descriptor, nine under a bridge anchored to each element. Every one pairs Fe, Co, Ni or Cu with Cr, Mo or W (Extended Data Fig. 6), the combination that descriptor interpolation had identified for ammonia synthesis.[42] Surfaces with group 3–5 elements that form nitrides, or sp metals outside the scaling relations of the activity model, are scored as separate layers (Supplementary Table 3). The most active surface, Ni₃Re, ranks only 12th on cost (28.0% regret), and the lower bound from the descriptor alone excludes 82.5% of the 1,695 priced surfaces from the full optimization without losing any that undercut Fe (Supplementary Note 6). The screen tests the iron benchmark against a candidate space more than a hundred times larger than the 15 metals, and the few surfaces that beat iron on cost form one chemical family, so the economic filter returns a design rule rather than a list.

### What Ru must cost to match fused iron

Why does the most active metal lose? Converting TOF into the metal a plant must hold keeps the intrinsic order, but charging that metal at its price over a 10-year life already gives Fe < Ru < Os, at 0.204 US dollars per tonne of annualized metal replacement for Fe against 4.09 for Ru, before a reactor, compressor or refrigerator is costed. Priced as iron, at 8 US dollars per kilogram, Ru wins (10.24 against 11.52 US dollars per tonne), and a price sweep with full reoptimization places parity at 404 US dollars per kilogram, 99% below the Ru price of the model (Fig. 4d). The reversal therefore has one cause, the price of Ru. Process optimization cannot remove it, only move it. Ru retreats to about 450 °C and 220 bar, where a small bed holds little metal, while Fe runs near 425 °C and 105 bar (Fig. 4b,c), and the 5.12 US dollars per tonne between them reappears mainly as compression of fresh feed (+3.988), metal inventory (+1.694) and compressor capital (+1.024) (Fig. 4a,e).

The order is robust. Fe is cheaper in every one of 5,000 draws of metal prices, capital cost, electricity price and catalyst lifetime (Fig. 4h), and first in 65.3% of 1,000 descriptor samples (Fig. 4f,g). It is also robust to the model's own errors. Against 64 values from industrial ammonia loops and converters, the model is within tolerance for every flow and bed volume at plant conditions, and low in NH₃ at the converter inlet (typically by 63%), compressor power (42%) and the bed volume of cost-optimal converter designs (73%) (Supplementary Table 4). A catalyst decision rests on the difference between two catalysts in the same plant, not on the absolute cost of either. We therefore traced each deviation to the model component that produces it and imposed it, in either direction and at its typical or largest size, before reoptimizing both catalysts. No single deviation puts Ru ahead of Fe, with a smallest margin of 1.34 US dollars per tonne, and neither does any combination of directions at the typical deviations (smallest margin 2.39; Supplementary Note 7). The ranking does not hinge on how closely the model reproduces any one plant.

A real Ru catalyst, however, holds far less metal than the benchmark bed. A promoted Ru/C catalyst exposes 11% of its Ru atoms,[43] against fewer than 1% of the Fe atoms in reduced fused iron.[44] The commercial catalyst carries about 8 wt% Ru,[45] and 90–95.55% of the Ru in spent promoted Ru/C catalysts is recovered.[46,47] To compare this catalyst with fused iron, descriptor kinetics are not enough. At 90 bar, 400 °C and 2% NH₃ they give Ru apparent orders of +2.4 in H₂ and −1.9 in NH₃, against +0.3 and −0.4 measured on promoted Ru/C, and fused iron −0.1 in NH₃ against −0.9 measured. We therefore compare the pair on measured rate laws, one fitted to commercial fused-iron data from 1 to 324 bar with the activity of the industrial magnetite catalyst whose data Dyson and Simon fitted,[48–50] the other fitted to promoted Ru/C at 50–100 bar,[43] each integrated along the bed and each catalyst optimized only at the pressures its law was fitted on (Methods). On this footing bulk Ru still loses. It would need a 16-fold activity gain or a 94% lower price, 3,371 US dollars per kilogram, to match fused iron at 15.31 US dollars per tonne.

Supported Ru/C (8 wt% Ru, 95.55% recovery) changes the question. With its Ru owned and carrying no charge, Ru/C costs 3.83 US dollars per tonne less than fused iron. Financed over the 25-year plant life at the 12% per year applied to equipment, it costs 2.13 more (Fig. 5a). The decision has moved from activity to the annual cost of holding Ru, and it flips at 6.49% per year. In a loop of the type used by the Kellogg Advanced Ammonia Process (KAAP), at 9 MPa with a −20 °C refrigerated condenser,[51] where both rate laws hold at the same operating point, it flips at 4.57%. What do plants pay to hold catalyst metal? No Ru lease rate is public, but one refiner disclosed 32 fixed-rate leases of platinum and palladium catalyst metal between 2013 and 2022 at 1.35–5.10% per year, median 2.10%.[52] The annual fee on the metal value is the quantity our financing rate represents, so we take 2.10% per year as the reference rate, with 0% and 12% as bounds. At that rate Ru/C is the cheaper catalyst, by 1.99 US dollars per tonne at the two catalysts' own optima and 0.65 in the KAAP-type loop. Priced as a supplier lease, the break-even rates are 6.81 and 5.00% per year. The break-even spans 1.23–15.65% per year across 16 variants of the price, catalyst and sizing inputs and 0.71–8.43% in the KAAP-type loop (Extended Data Fig. 7). Benchmark deviations of typical size, applied one at a time, move the difference by at most 2.3 US dollars per tonne without financing and 1.2 at 12% per year without reversing it (Supplementary Table 4).

### The optimizer recovers the industrial bed order

Industry has already answered this question. In the KAAP converter, a magnetite bed is followed by Ru/C beds.[51] We gave the optimizer a three-bed adiabatic converter at 9 MPa with interbed cooling and let it choose the catalyst of each bed. Fused iron in the first, hottest bed with Ru/C downstream is the least-cost design for every Ru financing rate from 0 to 5.46% per year, an all-iron converter wins above that rate, and no design with Ru/C upstream of fused iron is optimal at any rate (Fig. 5b). The model was not told the industrial arrangement. It reaches the bed order of the KAAP converter and of earlier Fe + Ru multibed designs[53] on its own. At the reference rate the converter is fused iron followed by two Ru/C beds, with fused iron 65% of the catalyst volume, against about half in the KAAP converter,[54] and it costs 0.37 US dollars per tonne less than the best all-iron converter (Fig. 5c). The order has a kinetic reason. Ammonia inhibits fused iron more strongly than Ru/C (apparent NH₃ orders −0.89 and −0.39), so the advantage of Ru/C per kilogram grows from 9-fold at 2% NH₃ to 62-fold at 14% (Fig. 5d), while the first bed runs hotter than the 460 °C up to which the Ru/C rate law was measured. Recovery and Ru price move the window (0–4.92% per year at 90% recovery, 0.12–8.21% and 0–4.03% at a 30% lower and higher price), but Ru/C never moves upstream of fused iron.

### Lifecycle engineering, not activity, brings Ru to parity

The backward calculation makes the same point from the other side. Multiplying Ru intrinsic activity by a factor α and reoptimizing at each value, Ru matches Fe only after a 115-fold increase (44–262-fold, 5th–95th percentile under economic uncertainty; Fig. 6a). Its scaling relation allows at most 1.02-fold at 673 K and 1.58-fold across all process conditions, and the cheapest Ru on the strict scaling relation, at \(E_{\mathrm{N}} = -1.180\) eV, still costs 16.45 US dollars per tonne (Fig. 6b). Gains of the required size exist only off the scaling relation (Fig. 6a, bottom). Ba and Cs promotion of Ru/C raise the TOF per surface Ru 75- and 65-fold over unpromoted Ru,[55] Ba promotion of Ru/BN more than 100-fold[56] and Cs promotion of Ru/MgO more than 134-fold,[57] whereas electride and oxyhydride supports give about tenfold[58,59] and confinement in carbon nanotubes 0.5-fold.[60] The Ru/C catalyst of the measured kinetics already carries Ba, Cs and K and still needs a 16-fold gain in the benchmark bed, so what closes the remaining gap is the economy of the metal (Table 1). At 99% recovery, a 10-year catalyst life needs at most a 1.29-fold activity gain and a 20-year life none (Fig. 6c). On the strict \(E_{\mathrm{N}}\) scaling manifold across all 14,510 process states, parity is set by a single catalyst loss factor, \(q=(1-r)/L\), with \(q^*=8.25\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{y}^{-1}\). At a 20-year life parity requires 98.35% recovery, and at 99% recovery the best state on the strict scaling relation costs 11.20 US dollars per tonne, 0.32 below Fe (Fig. 6d; Supplementary Note 8). Recovery and lifetime reach parity inside the design space constrained by the scaling relation, where activity alone cannot.

### Without a price or selectivity channel the ranking holds, and catalyst life sets its limit

When does the laboratory ranking survive? In the Au/TiO₂ CO-oxidation control, with the rate anchor of Janssens et al.[24] and the dependence on particle size of Overbury et al.,[25] 2–6 nm particles share one metal, loading, operating condition and process model. Neither a price nor a selectivity channel exists, and the required catalyst mass simply follows the mass activity: 6 nm particles need 8.1 times the catalyst of 2 nm particles. With an independent activity scatter for every particle size (\(\sigma = 0.25\) in natural log units), the 2 nm catalyst stays the least-cost choice in 99.4% of 10,000 draws and the full order 2 > 3 > 4 > 5 > 6 nm holds in 77.7%. With kinetic and operating variation for each candidate over 273–293 K it holds in 92.2% (Extended Data Fig. 8). One channel can still reverse it, catalyst life. If life grows with particle size as \(d^{\lambda}\), catalyst cost scales as \(d^{\,n+m-\lambda}\), where \(n + m = 1.9\) (1.5–3.2) is the size exponent of the catalyst requirement, and the order inverts above \(\lambda = 1.9\). The exponent is a model parameter whose value depends on how the catalyst dies. Powder Au/TiO₂ at 273–313 K loses activity through carbonate accumulation at the particle perimeter without particle growth,[61,62] which corresponds to \(\lambda \approx 0\) and keeps the order. Where Ostwald ripening controls life, its kinetics and the size-dependent stability of sub-3 nm Au on TiO₂[63] place λ at 3–4, and the larger particles win.

The three systems span both outcomes, and one number places them on a common scale. The realized pairwise transfer index

$$\chi_{AB}=\frac{\ln\left(J_A/J_B\right)}{\ln\left(s_A/s_B\right)},$$

with \(s_A > s_B\) the higher-is-better upstream property and \(J\) the lower-is-better plant objective, is ≈ −1.00 for Au, whose 2 nm particles have 8.1 times the mass activity of 6 nm particles and need 8.1 times less catalyst. It is +0.015 for methanol, where a 3.6-fold STY advantage comes with a 1.9% higher production cost, and +0.057 for ammonia, where 607 times the intrinsic TOF comes with a 44% higher catalyst-dependent cost (Supplementary Note 9). Once the plant objective of two catalysts is known, χ reduces their comparison to one number that can be set against any other system, and its sign tells at once whether the laboratory lead survives.

### An external case: Ru against Ni in CO₂ methanation

Does the reasoning hold in a system we did not model ourselves? For CO₂ methanation, Moioli and Züttel[26] found with their own reactor model that 0.5 wt% Ru/Al₂O₃ reaches 99.5% conversion in one stage, whereas Ni/Al₂O₃ needs 1.89 times the catalyst volume and a second stage after intermediate water condensation, and they recommended Ru for small plants and Ni for large ones. Before taking their inputs through our inventory, reactor and replacement chain with the metal prices of the ammonia case, we fixed the decision criterion (Methods). Ru is cheaper when

$$r_V\left(1+\varphi_{\mathrm{proc}}\right)>\Pi,$$

with \(r_V\) the Ni-to-Ru catalyst volume ratio, \(\Pi\) the Ru-to-Ni price ratio per kilogram of catalyst and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{proc}}\) the cost of the second Ni stage per unit of Ni catalyst cost. Because \(\Pi = 5.18\) exceeds \(r_V = 1.89\), catalyst inventory alone favours Ni at every scale, exactly as it favours Fe in ammonia. Ru wins only while the fixed part of the second Ni stage outweighs its price, below 0.60 MW of synthetic natural gas (SNG) at the central parameters (Monte Carlo median 0.86 MW, 5–95% range 0.18–7.0 MW). Ru is cheaper in 98.3% of draws at 0.1 MW and in 0.05% at 100 MW, and in every deterministic variant at the small end and in none at the large end (Extended Data Fig. 9; Supplementary Note 10). The laboratory ranking survives where the cheap metal needs the more complex process and reverses where it does not.

## Discussion

A catalyst ranking carries from one optimum to the next only as far as the upstream advantage reaches the plant objective. In ammonia, the price of the metal held in the bed stands in the way, and process optimization converts it into compression as Ru retreats to high pressure and a small bed. In methanol, it is the reactant lost to purge. In Au/TiO₂ nothing stands in the way, and the ranking holds for as long as catalyst life does not grow with particle size. The transfer index χ turns this reach into one number, negative where the advantage carries and positive where a channel blocks it. The independent methanation study follows the same inequality, with plant scale deciding which side of it applies.

The reversal is not an artefact of hand-picked cases. It is common in the literature itself. In a third of the methanol comparisons between catalysts and in more than a quarter of the ammonia comparisons, the catalyst that a paper's own data rank first is not the one a plant would build, and the reasons are those of the hand-built cases: CO and methane that accumulate in a recycle loop, and metal that a plant must buy. Even the operating point carries a lesson. The temperature at which a catalyst performs best in the laboratory is usually not where a plant would run it, and in most cases the plant would run it cooler. A laboratory comparison could report the plant-level consequence of its leader alongside its rate or yield, and a screen could target the catalyst property with enough downstream leverage to change the decision, such as methane selectivity rather than STY.

For Ru, the lesson is that metal economy does what activity cannot. Bulk Ru would need a 94% lower price, or an activity gain its scaling relation does not allow. Supported Ru/C with metal recovery already undercuts fused iron whenever its Ru is financed below 6.5% per year at the two catalysts' own optima and 4.6% in a KAAP-type loop. At the 2.1% per year that leases of catalyst metal carry, the least-cost converter puts fused iron in the first bed and Ru/C behind it, the arrangement that the KAAP adopted. Independent process analyses describe the same balance between lower-pressure operation, refrigeration and the price, loading, lifetime and recycling of Ru.[15,64,65]

ACSA is what turns the question into a measurement. With 97% recall and 92–100% of printed values transcribed correctly, it takes every comparison in a body of literature through the plant model, and its cost bounds spare most full optimizations without losing a leader. Models of higher fidelity and catalyst physics specific to a formulation slot into the same chain without changing its decision logic, so that every comparison a catalysis paper reports can be taken to its plant-level consequence.

## Methods

### Multiscale decision analysis

Each catalytic system is evaluated with a process-level objective matched to its catalyst-to-process pathway. For ammonia, the objective contains the terms that respond to catalyst identity and performance, namely catalyst inventory and replacement, reactor requirements, compression, refrigeration and pressure-dependent equipment. Common upstream H₂/N₂ supply contributions are held fixed so that they do not dilute the catalyst-dependent decision signal. The methanol model evaluates net production cost through its recycle and separation process, and the Au/TiO₂ control maps the activity, which depends on particle size, to the required catalyst mass under a common process model. The plant-level optimum of a set of catalysts is the catalyst with the lowest value of its system's objective under the stated process and economic conditions, each catalyst at the operating point that minimizes the objective, the basis on which techno-economic assessments compare options.[66] For descriptor-based and measured ammonia catalysts this point is sought over the full process library, for a measured rate law within its fitted pressure range and for a methanol catalyst from the literature over its measured operating points and the catalyst inventory a plant would choose. The plant-cost leader of a comparison is its plant-level optimum. Rankings before and after process evaluation are compared by Spearman and Kendall correlations, pairwise inversions, top-rank identity and the normalized decision regret

$$R=\frac{J_{\mathrm{up}}-J_{\mathrm{opt}}}{J_{\mathrm{opt}}},$$

where \(J\) is the lower-is-better objective, \(J_{\mathrm{up}}\) its value for the upstream leader and \(J_{\mathrm{opt}}\) its value for the plant-level optimum. Backward design varies one catalyst property, reoptimizes the process and finds the value required for parity, which is then compared with the range accessible under the corresponding scaling relation.

### Ammonia synthesis

The ammonia calculation combines the 15-metal activity screen, scaling and kinetic relations, catalyst productivity and inventory, process optimization for each candidate, pressure-dependent equipment capital and seven catalyst-dependent cost components. The NH₃ formation free energy is taken at each temperature from the Gillespie–Beattie equilibrium constant,[67] and N₂, H₂ and NH₃ enter the loop equilibrium and the microkinetic gas activities as fugacities with Newton–Cooper coefficients,[48] so that the reactor and the loop share one equilibrium. The atomistic reference conditions (673 K, 24.5 bar N₂, 74.25 bar H₂, 1 bar NH₃) and step-site descriptors follow the Wang–Abild-Pedersen Dataset S1,[68] whose most active metals the reconstruction recovers. The Fe step descriptor comes from the 14-metal terrace-to-step relation, whose residual standard error of 0.2271 eV is the Fe descriptor uncertainty (Supplementary Table 5). Catalyst productivity is calibrated on the fused-iron bed at 400 °C, 80 bar and a −35 °C separator, and temperature, pressure and separator temperature are reoptimized for each candidate over 14,510 process states within a 90 m³ limit on bed volume. The Fe optimum (425 °C, 105 bar) lies within the pressure range of industrial Fe loops,[51] milder conditions being the stated direction of Haber–Bosch development,[69,70] and Fe/Ru loop studies give independent context for the optima of each catalyst.[14]

The Ru price test sets only the Ru metal price to the Fe value of 8 US dollars per kilogram and reoptimizes. The price sweep repeats this at 241 logarithmically spaced prices from 1 to 3 × 10⁵ US dollars per kilogram and locates parity by bisection. The economic Monte Carlo (5,000 draws) samples Fe and Ru price multipliers (log-uniform, 0.5–2.0), a common capital multiplier (0.8–1.2), electricity price (20–100 US dollars per megawatt-hour) and catalyst lifetime (5–20 years) and reoptimizes both catalysts in every draw, and descriptor uncertainty is propagated through 1,000 samples. Backward design multiplies Ru intrinsic activity and reoptimizes at each value. Reachability is evaluated on the strict \(E_{\mathrm{N}}\) scaling manifold over all 14,510 states. At a fixed state the metal, reactor and pressure-vessel terms are monotone in the required metal inventory, and the compression and refrigeration terms do not depend on \(E_{\mathrm{N}}\). The cost-minimizing descriptor of each state is therefore its TOF-maximizing value, and the lower envelope across states gives the lifecycle parity boundary in \(q=(1-r)/L\). The joint activity–lifecycle target uses the 42 states visited by the reoptimized price sweep, which can only overestimate the required activity gain.

The plant benchmark compares the model with 64 values from industrial ammonia synthesis: loop states of 13 industrial loop records, forward runs and cost-optimal designs of the converter, compressor power, separator vapour and the Fe optimum (Supplementary Table 4). Deviations are (model − industrial)/industrial for point values, differences in °C for temperatures and the distance to the nearer edge for ranges, and 33 of the 64 items lie within their tolerance. The failing items trace to five model components (separator vapour, approach to equilibrium, compression power, catalyst amount and bed temperature). Each was perturbed by the typical and by the largest deviation of its items, in both directions, alone and in all 32 sign combinations, and Fe, Ru and Os were reoptimized in every case.

### Fe and Ru/C on measured rate laws

Supported Ru/C is compared with fused iron on the Model 3 rate law of Keestra et al. for commercial fused-iron catalysts, fitted to data from 1 to 324 bar, with the activity factor (0.350) of the Topsoe KM1R catalyst whose data Dyson and Simon fitted,[48–50] and on the rate equation of a Ba–Cs–K-promoted Ru/C catalyst fitted at 50–100 bar.[43] Catalyst requirement is integrated along the bed in plug flow at every process state, and each law is calibrated on its own measured rate. The Ru/C law reproduces the 125 measured points of its source with a mean error of 10%, and the fused-iron law the outlet of a commercial magnetite catalyst at 100 bar within 16%. Each catalyst is optimized only over the states inside its fitted pressure range, so that every comparison lies inside both fits. The supported bed holds 8 wt% Ru[45] at 490 kg m⁻³, the centre of the range derived from a promoted Ru/C laboratory bed,[71] with 95.55% Ru recovery. The Ru dispersion, 11% for the promoted catalyst[43] and up to about 50% for 2 nm Ru particles,[72] enters through the measured rate per kilogram of catalyst. The KAAP-type loop restricts both catalysts to the 90 bar, −20 °C separator states.

Metal cash flows price every metal purchase (at 0, 10 and 20 years) and credit for recovered metal (at replacement and closure) at its date over the 25-year plant life, discounted at a metal financing rate of 0–12% per year with Fe on the same convention. The reference rate is the median of the 32 fixed annual rates that one refiner disclosed for its leases of platinum and palladium catalyst metal (1.35–5.10% per year, terms of 8 months to 3 years).[52] Under these contracts the refiner sold the catalyst metal to banks, leased it back for an annual fee on its value and repurchased it at the end of the term, so the fee is the annual cost of holding catalyst metal. It is applied to Ru by analogy. The supplier lease variant leaves title with the supplier, charges the fee on the Ru value, settles the shortfall of fine metal at each return and discounts the operator's cash flows at 12% per year.

The staged converter has three adiabatic beds with interbed cooling at 9 MPa, a −20 °C separator and 8% inerts, each bed filled with fused iron (up to 530 °C) or Ru/C (up to 460 °C, the top of its measured range). Bed temperatures and catalyst amounts are optimized for every arrangement, including those with Ru/C upstream of fused iron, and the least-cost arrangement is read at each financing rate. The 16 robustness cases vary the Fe catalyst price, Ru/C bed density, Ru recovery, Ru price, catalyst life, the effectiveness factor of either catalyst, the converter pressure premium and the bed sizing. The check of the recycle boundary raises the recycle pressure rise from 3 bar to the 9.2 bar of the 1,500 t/d Krupp–Uhde flowsheet[73] and reoptimizes all 15 metals, and an independent five-component Peng–Robinson loop with the NH₃ interaction parameters of Mace and Vrabec[74] checks the cost model in the KAAP-type loop (Supplementary Note 7).

### Bimetallic surfaces and measured ammonia catalysts

N adsorption energies for the 1,796 surfaces of Mamun et al.[40] were retrieved from Catalysis-Hub.[41] The most stable site of each surface defines its terrace descriptor, a linear fit over the 14 shared pure metals (slope 0.845, R² = 0.973) maps it onto the model's terrace scale, and the terrace-to-step relation gives the step descriptor. The second bridge, anchored to the elements, adds each element's offset between its reference and bridged descriptors, weighted by atomic fraction. Costs use the alloy's mole-weighted molar mass and mass-weighted metal price, with the 15 model metals at their model prices and 21 other elements at USGS 2025 annual average prices,[75] and are minimized over all states within the bed limit. The lower bound from the descriptor alone combines a candidate's activity at the best state, smallest reactor term and cheapest process state.

For measured catalysts, each catalyst's measured rate per gram of metal fixes the effective descriptor at which the model reproduces it at the laboratory condition, on the side of the volcano where its metal lies. The catalyst is then optimized over the 14,510 states with that descriptor, the molar mass and price of its metal and its own metal content. The review set is Tables 1–6 of ref. 39, extracted in two independent passes and checked against the primary papers. Primary papers were extracted with their Supporting Information. Comparison groups contain the entries of one paper at the same temperature, pressure, H₂/N₂ ratio and space velocity, only entries whose rate, temperature and outlet NH₃ fraction are printed enter the primary comparison, and a catalyst is the fused-iron reference only when it is a commercial fused or wüstite iron catalyst. The paper leaderboard is the rate per gram of catalyst, and confidence intervals are paper-cluster bootstraps with 10,000 resamples.

### CO₂-to-methanol

The methanol analysis uses a recycle and separation model calibrated on a CO₂-to-methanol loop from the literature,[35] applied to four Re/TiO₂ states of the 1 and 5 wt% catalysts of Gothe et al.[19] at 2% reference purge, with purge also varied from 0.5% to 40% and optimized for each state. Local economic leverages for STY, conversion and methane suppression are evaluated at the 5 wt% Re, 250 °C state, counterfactuals remove one candidate difference at a time, and the measurement Monte Carlo (5,000 draws) samples each state's conversion and selectivities from uncertainties built from the source data. The plant model was checked against process assessments and pilot plant data (Supplementary Table 6). At 22–33% per-pass conversion its hydrogen and CO₂ consumption, carbon efficiency and recycle ratio lie within the reported ranges, and at the operating point and prices of ref. 20 it reproduces the break-even methanol price to within 2.4%.

For the literature comparisons, every extracted record with CO₂ conversion, methanol selectivity, temperature, pressure and H₂/CO₂ ratio is a candidate operating point, and the primary analysis uses entries whose conversion and selectivities are printed (81 groups; Supplementary Table 7). Comparison groups contain the entries of one paper at the same pressure, H₂/CO₂ ratio and space velocity. STY per gram of catalyst defines the paper leaderboard, and net production cost at each entry's own temperature, pressure and H₂/CO₂ ratio the plant leaderboard. Recycled CO is converted per pass to the smallest extent that keeps the reactor outlet at or below the reverse-water-gas-shift and CO-hydrogenation equilibria, and single-pass CO₂ conversion is capped at the loop's CO₂-hydrogenation equilibrium. The purge is optimized over 0.5–40% among the levels at which CO, methane and N₂ make up at most 6.86% of the reactor inlet, the limit of the loop that anchors the cost model (6.46% CO and N₂ in its stream table[35]). The loop without the limit and limits of 4–30% are sensitivities (Extended Data Fig. 10). The lower bound that allocates the full plant optimization evaluates the same economics on loop flows at the lower bound, valid for any per-pass conversion of recycled CO. In the treatment of catalyst inventory each entry may load more catalyst, raising its conversion along an approach-to-equilibrium curve calibrated on its measured point to at most 95% of equilibrium at constant selectivity. Sampling uncertainty is a paper-cluster bootstrap with 10,000 resamples, and measurement uncertainty re-draws every entry 2,000 times.

What counts as a different catalyst was fixed before any comparison was counted. Two entries of a group are different catalysts when the authors report them as different samples that differ in composition (promoters and dopants included), loading of the active component, support or synthesis. Entries of one sample that differ only in reaction temperature, time on stream, activation or reactor operating mode are one catalyst at different operating points. Each catalyst enters the laboratory ranking at its highest STY and the plant ranking at its lowest cost, and a comparison changes when its two leaders are different catalysts. In the primary count every entry may load more catalyst as above, so that each candidate is costed at the best condition its own measurement permits, and counts at laboratory conversion are reported alongside. A stricter identity merges samples that differ only in synthesis. The second extraction took papers published from 5 November 2025 to 13 October 2026 in the relevance order of one literature search and screened them against the inclusion rules of the first set until 40 were included (Supplementary Table 1b). They were extracted, grouped and costed with the same prompts, model and code.

### Au/TiO₂ control and CO₂ methanation

The Au/TiO₂ control anchors the reference rate, Au loading and dispersion to Janssens et al.[24] and the dependence on particle size to Overbury et al.[25] on the physical basis of the established literature.[22,23] Mass activity scales as \(d^{-(n+m)}\), and the required catalyst mass is its inverse. A Monte Carlo for each candidate (10,000 draws) takes shared exponents \(n\sim U(0.7,\,2.0)\) and \(m\sim U(0.8,\,1.2)\) and gives each particle size an independent log-normal activity factor (\(\sigma = 0.25\), from the exponent uncertainty of the Overbury series). The analysis of operating freedom optimizes temperature and O₂/CO ratio for each candidate. Catalyst life enters as \(\tau\propto d^{\lambda}\), with annual catalyst cost \(m_i/\tau_i\).

For CO₂ methanation, the inputs of Moioli and Züttel[26] (catalyst loadings, the Ni-to-Ru volume ratio of 1.89 at 99.5% conversion, the single Ru stage and the two Ni stages with intermediate condensation, and their bases for catalyst cost) were recorded with their locations, and the decision criterion and the swept ranges were fixed before the first calculation. The catalyst-dependent cost per MWh of SNG comprises catalyst inventory at the design space velocity, replacement at the metal prices of the ammonia model, capital of the reactor stage with the vessel correlation and capital recovery of the ammonia model and, for Ni, a second vessel with a condensation set. Scale is swept from 0.1 to 100 MW, and a 10,000-draw Monte Carlo samples the bracketed inputs.

### Adaptive Catalyst Screening Agent

ACSA collects each paper and its Supporting Information, converts every page to text and an image, and extracts every catalyst entry into a fixed schema in which each number carries its value, unit, qualifier, location and page. Each pass reads the paper in one call to a language model that returns strict JSON: a main pass, a figure pass that digitizes every point of catalyst performance in the figures of the main text, and passes over the Supporting Information. A printed value from the main text outranks a printed value from the Supporting Information, which outranks a plot reading, and prompts contain only the paper itself. Accuracy is scored against two curated methanol datasets[33,34] and the Re/TiO₂ entries of the hand-built case, with entries matched by paper and catalyst name or composition and never by the values being scored. Every disagreement between extracted and curated values was checked against the paper, curated values that the paper contradicts are corrected to the printed value before scoring, and both raw and corrected scores are reported (Supplementary Table 8). A random sample of 100 extracted values from 10 papers was checked by the authors against the papers and Supporting Information. Of these, 98 are correct. The two errors, a conversion printed for a stability test and attached to a table entry of the same catalyst and a methanol formation rate recorded as STY without the paper's name for it, change no comparison at the catalyst level (Supplementary Table 9). Before new candidates are scored, ACSA runs the hand-built inputs of all three systems through the same code path and requires their results to be reproduced, and every candidate was additionally evaluated in full to verify both cost bounds.
