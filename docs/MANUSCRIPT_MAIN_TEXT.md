# Process optimization and economics can reverse catalyst rankings

## Abstract

Atomistic catalyst screening ranks materials by descriptors or intrinsic rates, whereas industrial selection depends on how catalyst properties propagate through reactor operation and economics. We couple catalyst-specific kinetics to process optimization and a catalyst-responsive economic objective, then use backward design to ask whether the properties required for economic parity are physically accessible. In ammonia synthesis, the intrinsic order Ru > Os > Fe becomes Fe > Ru > Os after process and economic evaluation. A layer-wise ablation shows that the ordering is preserved through catalyst demand and first reverses when that demand is monetized through metal price and replacement economics; catalyst-specific process reoptimization subsequently amplifies the gap. Selecting Ru from intrinsic activity instead of Fe incurs a 44.1% normalized regret in the modeled objective. Activity improvement alone would require a 201.22-fold increase and remains outside the strict scaling-constrained space. Joint improvements in activity, catalyst lifetime and Ru recovery move the target close to parity, but the prespecified 20-year, 99%-recovery property box still misses Fe by 0.46%. CO₂ hydrogenation to methanol shows a smaller reversal driven by selectivity, purge and recycle, whereas an Au/TiO₂ control preserves the upstream ranking. Finally, an Adaptive Catalyst Screening Agent provides a reusable execution layer for the deterministic analysis: under constrained budgets it can recover the ammonia selection and reachability conclusions below the fixed-sequence completion threshold, while non-binding runs expose a distinct stopping-efficiency limitation. Together, these results show that catalyst screening should be evaluated by downstream decision consequence and physical reachability, not activity rank alone.

## Introduction

Computational catalyst discovery commonly uses adsorption energies, reactivity descriptors, scaling relations, volcano plots and surrogate models to rank candidate materials.[1–6] Descriptor-based microkinetic models have made these relations useful across broad catalyst families, while first-principles multiscale models have connected elementary-step energetics to rates and operating conditions.[6,7] Previous work has also linked ammonia descriptors to reactor design and catalyst selection,[32] and formulated catalyst design and process optimization as coupled problems.[33,36] Laboratory reactor studies further show that catalyst rankings can depend on the test environment.[35] What remains less clear is whether the ordering of discrete catalyst candidates is preserved when atomistic predictions are propagated through candidate-specific process optimization and economic evaluation.

This question matters most for the leading candidates. A screening model can remain well correlated across a large candidate set while changing the identity of the material that would actually be selected. Global rank correlation therefore does not by itself establish that the top of the ranking is reliable. Early-stage catalyst-economic analysis has likewise shown that catalyst cost per kilogram can be a poor proxy for cost per unit product once catalyst stability and production scale are included.[44] In ammonia synthesis, process-level Fe/Ru studies further show that the preferred catalyst and pressure depend on plant scale and loop configuration.[41,42] These studies motivate a decision-level comparison, but they do not answer whether a broad atomistic candidate ranking survives candidate-specific process optimization. The same principle applies to uncertainty: adaptive-sampling and Bayesian approaches show that uncertainty is most useful when interpreted in relation to the quantity or decision of interest.[8–10]

We examine the problem in both directions. In the forward direction, catalyst properties are propagated through kinetics and process optimization to an economic objective. In the reverse direction, we ask what change in a catalyst property would be required to reach a specified economic target and whether that change lies within the range allowed by the underlying scaling relations. Integrated material-and-process design has previously inverted process objectives into molecular targets,[34] and catalyst-design frameworks have optimized descriptors within kinetic models.[33,36] Here the emphasis is on candidate ranking, economic parity and physical reachability.

We apply this analysis to two reaction systems and one control case. Ammonia synthesis provides a 15-metal screen in which intrinsic activity and metal price affect catalyst inventory and the preferred operating conditions.[5,11,12] CO₂ hydrogenation to methanol provides a different process structure in which selectivity affects reactant loss, purge, recycle and compression.[13–15] Au/TiO₂ CO oxidation is used as a control in which particle size is mapped monotonically to catalyst requirement under a common process model.[16–19]

Together, these systems span ranking reversal, partial reordering and rank preservation. They allow us to relate changes in ranking to the process variables controlled by each catalyst property and to test whether an economically relevant catalyst target remains within the material space allowed by the underlying scaling relations. As a methodological extension, we expose the deterministic calculations to an Adaptive Catalyst Screening Agent (ACSA), which selects the calculations needed for each decision instance under a finite computational budget. The scientific models remain unchanged; the Agent provides the execution layer that allows the same ranking, parity and reachability analysis to be repeated across candidate sets.

## Results

### Process and economic evaluation reverse the leading ammonia ranking

The 15-metal ammonia screen ranks Ru, Os and Fe first, second and third in intrinsic activity (Fig. 1c), consistent with the established activity landscape for transition-metal ammonia synthesis.[11,12] After optimizing the operating conditions separately for each catalyst and evaluating catalyst-dependent costs (Fig. 1a,d,e), the order becomes Fe, Ru and Os, with costs of 15.292, 22.031 and 25.832 US dollars per tonne of NH₃, respectively (Fig. 1b,h).

The change is concentrated among the leading candidates. For Ru, Os and Fe, Spearman rho is -0.50 and Kendall tau is -0.33, while the Spearman correlation across all 15 metals remains 0.929 (Fig. 1f,g). The overall screen can therefore remain strongly correlated even when the preferred catalyst changes at the top of the ranking. Agreement among the leading candidates is thus more relevant to catalyst selection than full-set correlation alone. Agreement can also be expressed in decision terms. If the intrinsic-activity winner Ru were selected instead of the economic winner Fe, the catalyst-dependent cost objective would increase from 15.292 to 22.031 US dollars per tonne of NH₃, corresponding to a normalized decision regret of **44.1%**.

### Metal price and process optimization jointly determine the Fe-Ru ranking

The leading metals favor different operating conditions. Fe has an interior optimum near 425 °C and 180 bar with a 30 °C separator, whereas Ru favors approximately 450 °C and 425 bar with a 25 °C separator; Os has a broad, shallow minimum at high pressure (Fig. 2b,c). The economic comparison therefore reflects both catalyst properties and the operating conditions favored by each catalyst.

To isolate the effect of metal price, we repeated the Ru optimization after setting its metal price equal to the Fe value of 8 US dollars per kilogram. All other model inputs, constraints and costing assumptions were unchanged. The Ru optimum shifts from 450 °C and 425 bar to 425 °C and 170 bar, and its optimized cost falls to 14.712 US dollars per tonne of NH₃, 0.580 US dollars per tonne below Fe (Fig. 2b–e). Thus removing the Ru-Fe price difference reverses the economic order after process reoptimization.

A full Ru-price sweep, with process reoptimization at every price, places Fe-Ru parity at 163.76 US dollars per kilogram. At this price, Ru operates at 425 °C and 185 bar with a 6.24 m³ catalyst bed (Fig. 2d). Across the sweep, the optimum moves from 170 bar and a 9.4 m³ bed at 1 US dollar per kilogram to 420–430 bar, a 0 °C separator and a 0.014 m³ bed at 3 × 10^5 US dollars per kilogram. Metal price therefore affects the final ranking directly through catalyst cost and indirectly by changing the preferred process conditions.

The baseline Ru-Fe cost difference of 6.739 US dollars per tonne is distributed across several cost components. Fresh-feed compression contributes +4.632 US dollars per tonne, metal inventory +1.763, compressor capital +1.181 and refrigeration +0.629, while vessel pressure, recycle compression and reactor-base terms partly offset these contributions (Fig. 2a,e). This decomposition identifies the main contributions to the baseline difference, whereas the equal-price calculation shows how the optimized process changes when the metal-price difference is removed.

A layer-wise check localizes the reversal more precisely. At the common 673 K atomistic reference, converting turnover frequency into the plant-throughput active-metal requirement preserves the leading order: Ru requires less active metal than Os, and both require far less than Fe. Multiplying those same catalyst demands by the canonical metal prices and the frozen 10-year lifetime changes the order to Fe < Ru < Os before any reactor, compression, refrigeration or equipment cost is added. As an independent causal check, setting the Ru metal price equal to the Fe price and reoptimizing the complete 14,136-state process leaves Ru less costly than Fe (14.712 versus 15.292 US dollars per tonne of NH₃). The first inversion is therefore triggered at the catalyst-demand-to-lifecycle-economics interface; the catalyst-specific process response subsequently amplifies the gap through fresh-feed compression, compressor capital and refrigeration.

Descriptor uncertainty affects feasibility and ranking differently. Across 1,000 descriptor-uncertainty samples, Fe satisfies the 90 m³ bed-volume limit in 79.9% of samples, ranks first economically in 68.1% (681/1000), and remains within the top three in 94.0%. The atomistic winner remains the economic winner in 28.2% of samples (Fig. 2f,g). A supporting error-dependence analysis keeps the same marginal descriptor uncertainties but couples the Ru, Os and Fe errors through a Gaussian copula. The independent case reproduces the frozen atomistic-winner frequencies, whereas increasing positive correlation changes the top-one probabilities rather than simply cancelling uncertainty; at latent pairwise correlation 0.9, Fe is the atomistic winner in 21.7% of draws compared with 10.7% in the matched independent audit. A separate common-offset test shows why: because all three descriptors move across a nonlinear volcano, a shared energetic bias can itself change their ordering. These tests are reported as uncertainty-model sensitivities and are not used to replace the preregistered downstream probabilities.

Economic uncertainty around the baseline case does not overturn the Fe preference. Across 5,000 preregistered samples that vary metal price, the CAPEX coefficient, electricity price and catalyst lifetime, full process reoptimization gives P(C_Fe < C_Ru) = 1.000, with a minimum sampled Ru-Fe cost difference of 2.382 US dollars per tonne (Fig. 2h). Expressed as normalized selection regret, R = (C_Ru - C_Fe)/C_Fe, the 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles are **30.48%, 40.89% and 54.25%**, respectively, and R remains positive in all 5,000 draws. The Ru activity multiplier required for economic parity is 70.78-fold, 174.27-fold and 462.00-fold at the 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles, respectively (Fig. 3b). The equal-price calculation therefore identifies a condition under which the ranking reverses, whereas the uncertainty analysis establishes both the sign and the practical cost consequence of the Fe preference over the economic range examined around the baseline case.

### Backward design separates the required catalyst-property region from physical reachability

The forward analysis identifies the preferred catalyst but does not show how much Ru would need to improve to reach parity with Fe. We therefore multiplied Ru intrinsic activity by a factor alpha and reoptimized the full process at each value. Under the baseline economic assumptions, Ru reaches the Fe cost only after a 201.22-fold activity increase (Fig. 3a,c).

The accessible range defined by the scaling relation is much smaller. At 673 K, the largest activity increase along the scaling relation is 1.090-fold, and the largest increase found across all allowed process conditions is 2.525-fold. Under strict scaling, the lowest Ru cost is 21.398 US dollars per tonne of NH₃ at *E*_N = -1.215 eV, which remains above the Fe cost (Fig. 3d,e). The 201.22-fold requirement therefore lies far outside the range accessible by moving Ru along the present scaling relation. Even the 5th-percentile requirement under economic uncertainty, 70.78-fold, remains well above the 2.525-fold maximum (Fig. 3b).

Within the model and scaling relation used here, improving Ru activity alone cannot make Ru economically competitive with Fe over the economic range considered.

The same parity calculation can be written as a two-property lifecycle target at fixed Ru intrinsic activity. The fully reoptimized Ru price sweep gives an effective Ru parity price of 163.76 US dollars per kilogram. Because metal recovery and catalyst lifetime enter the Ru replacement-cost term multiplicatively with metal price, the corresponding boundary is \(P_{Ru}(1-r)(10\,\mathrm{y}/L) \le 163.76\). At the baseline Ru price of 53,852.5 US dollars per kilogram, this requires approximately 99.70% recovery for a 10-year lifetime, 99.39% for a 20-year lifetime, or a 32.9-year lifetime at 99% recovery. The prespecified 5–20-year lifetime range and 99% optimistic recovery bound therefore do not reach parity at the current Ru activity.

Allowing the backward variables to move together changes the required target before physical reachability is tested. We conservatively evaluated the 53 distinct process states already visited by the fully reoptimized Ru price sweep, rescaling catalyst demand by a direct Ru activity multiplier and recomputing the volume- and pressure-dependent cost terms at each state. Because this search uses only a subset of the 14,136-state library, the resulting activity requirements are conservative upper bounds. With 99% Ru recovery, extending catalyst life from 10 to 15 and 20 years lowers the required direct activity multiplier from at most 2.418-fold to 1.742-fold and 1.401-fold, respectively; a 20-year lifetime with 98% recovery requires at most 2.418-fold.

We then tested this target region directly on the strict *E*_N scaling manifold across all 14,136 process states. The canonical strict-scaling minimum, 21.398 US dollars per tonne at *E*_N = -1.215 eV, is reproduced before lifecycle changes are introduced. The joint parity boundary is controlled by the catalyst-loss factor \(q=(1-r)/L\), with \(q^*=4.4068\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{y}^{-1}\). Within the prespecified range of catalyst lifetimes up to 20 years and Ru recovery up to 99%, the closest point is 20 years and 99% recovery: its best strict-scaling state occurs at *E*_N = -1.230 eV and costs 15.362 US dollars per tonne, 0.071 US dollars per tonne above Fe. Parity therefore lies just outside the tested property range, requiring 99.119% recovery at a 20-year lifetime or, equivalently, a 22.69-year lifetime at 99% recovery. The backward-design result is thus a narrow property region near the edge of the present scaling-constrained design space rather than a single activity threshold.

### Selectivity, recycle and purge reshape the methanol ranking

The same ranking change appears in CO₂ hydrogenation to methanol through a different process pathway. Four literature-derived Re/TiO₂ catalyst-temperature states were compared (Fig. 4a,b).[13] Using space-time yield per gram of Re as the catalyst metric, the order is 1 wt% Re at 250 °C, 1 wt% Re at 200 °C, 5 wt% Re at 200 °C and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C. After propagation through the recycle and separation model at the 2% purge used in the reference case, the economic order becomes 5 wt% Re at 200 °C, 1 wt% Re at 250 °C, 1 wt% Re at 200 °C and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C, with net production costs of 943, 962, 967 and 1,258 euros per tonne, respectively (Fig. 4c).

The state with the highest space-time yield per gram of Re falls from first to second economically, and the state ranked third by space-time yield is the economic optimum. Selecting the upstream winner would give 961.51 euros per tonne instead of the 943.30 euros per tonne economic optimum, a normalized decision regret of **1.93%**. Across the four states, Spearman rho is 0.40, Kendall tau is 0.33 and two of six pairwise comparisons invert (Fig. 4e). Methane formation consumes reactants and increases gas accumulation, purge, recycle and compression requirements. A local sensitivity analysis at the 5 wt% Re, 250 °C state gives economic leverage values of 0.37579 for methane suppression, 0.05883 for single-pass conversion and 0.00289 for space-time yield (Fig. 4a,g). These sensitivities are consistent with previous CO₂-to-methanol process studies in which recycle, compression and separation make substantial contributions to plant performance.[14,15]

The reordering persists across the full purge range considered. Over 396 purge levels from 0.5% to 40%, the state with the highest space-time yield per gram of Re is never the least-cost option; Spearman rho never exceeds 0.80 and at least one of six pairwise comparisons remains inverted. Optimizing purge separately for each catalyst state changes the detailed order but does not restore the space-time-yield ranking (Fig. 4f).

Propagating each state's own measurement uncertainty separates the robust positions from the unresolved one (Fig. 4d). In 5,000 draws in which the CO₂ conversion and selectivities of every state are sampled independently and the full plant model is re-solved, the economic winner keeps first place in 4,559 draws (91.2%) and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C remains last in all 5,000. The second and third places, 5.5 euros per tonne apart, exchange in 1,054 draws (21.1%). The space-time-yield winner becomes the economic optimum in 413 draws (8.3%), and the complete space-time-yield ranking is recovered in 24 draws (0.5%).

### Catalyst effects on process operation determine whether rankings change

The ammonia and methanol results identify different routes by which catalyst properties affect process cost (Fig. 5a). In ammonia, intrinsic activity and metal price determine catalyst inventory and the preferred operating conditions, which in turn change compression, reactor and equipment costs. In methanol, selectivity changes reactant losses, gas accumulation, purge, recycle and compression. Absolute costs are not compared across the two reactions; the comparison concerns how catalyst properties enter each process model.

Au/TiO₂ CO oxidation provides a control in which the relation between particle size and catalyst requirement is monotonic. Low-temperature CO oxidation on supported Au is well known to depend on particle size and support effects.[16,17] The present calculation uses the absolute-rate anchor reported by Janssens et al.[18] and the particle-size dependence measured by Overbury et al.[19] at the closest Au loading. Candidate states differ only in Au particle size from 2 to 6 nm under the same operating condition and process model.

Under this common mapping, intrinsic activity and required catalyst mass preserve the same order, 2 > 3 > 4 > 5 > 6 nm. Spearman rho and Kendall tau are both 1.000, no pairwise comparison inverts, and all 10,000 parameter samples drawn from ranges reported in the literature preserve the complete order (Fig. 5b–d). The upstream winner is therefore also the minimum-burden downstream choice, giving zero selection regret under this control mapping. When moderate candidate-specific kinetic and operating variations are introduced, the exact order is retained in 92.16% of 10,000 samples over 273.15–293.15 K, with mean rho = 0.99214. Extending the temperature range to 313.15 K reduces exact preservation to 72.62% while retaining mean rho = 0.96802 (Fig. 5e). Process and economic evaluation therefore do not necessarily change a catalyst ranking; changes arise when catalyst properties affect downstream operating requirements strongly enough to alter relative costs (Fig. 5f).

### Adaptive calculation selection makes the multiscale analysis repeatedly executable

We finally tested whether the same ammonia selection and reachability analysis could be executed adaptively rather than through a fixed calculation sequence. The Adaptive Catalyst Screening Agent (ACSA) chooses among 11 predefined calculations, while the kinetic, process and economic models remain deterministic (Fig. 6a). A run is considered successful only when it identifies the economic winner, preserves the Fe-Ru ordering and gives the correct reachability classification; the numerical value of the Ru-Fe parity multiplier is evaluated separately.

The fixed sequence requires 206 compute units (CU) to recover all three conclusions. At a 75-CU allowance, the strongest model succeeds in 20/20 runs, and the conclusions become stable after a median of 52.5 CU. At 50 CU, 13/20 runs recover all three conclusions, although the economic winner and Fe-Ru ordering are correct in all 20 runs (Fig. 6c,e). The weaker model tiers do not reach the same performance at the tested budgets, showing that the ability to allocate calculations selectively depends on model capability as well as the available computation.

The exact parity multiplier converges more slowly than the qualitative conclusions. The baseline Ru-Fe parity multiplier is recovered in 9/20 runs at 75 CU and in 20/20 runs at 225 CU. The catalyst-selection and reachability conclusions can therefore stabilize before every intermediate numerical quantity has converged.

The allocation strategy changes when the budget constraint is removed. At low budgets, the strongest model frequently restricts the process search to windows smaller than the full 14,136-state domain. With a non-binding 5,000-CU allowance, none of 20 runs uses a reduced process window; the median point at which all three conclusions become stable increases to 566 CU, followed by a further median 148 CU before stopping, for a median final cost of 714 CU (Fig. 6d,e). In 14/20 traces, computation continues after the hindsight stable-decision point; summed across all runs, 8,207 of 17,535 CU (46.8%) are spent after that point. Because the stability point is defined retrospectively from the remainder of each trace, this fraction diagnoses stopping inefficiency but is not an online saving estimate. Budget pressure therefore induces search compression. This behavior defines the mechanism by which the Agent closes the decision below the fixed-sequence threshold; it does not change the broader role of ACSA as the reusable execution layer that orchestrates the deterministic scientific calculations across repeated screening tasks.

## Discussion

Atomic-scale rank agreement is not sufficient to guarantee the same industrial choice. In ammonia, the full 15-metal ranking remains strongly correlated while the decision-frontier order Ru > Os > Fe becomes Fe > Ru > Os after process and economic propagation. The layer-wise ablation localizes the first reversal: intrinsic activity and catalyst demand preserve the upstream order, whereas monetizing catalyst inventory through metal price and replacement economics flips Fe and Ru; process reoptimization then amplifies the gap through compression, compressor capital and refrigeration. The same decision consequence is captured by normalized regret: selecting the upstream winner costs 44.1% more than the downstream winner in the baseline ammonia objective, 1.93% more in the methanol objective, and 0% in the Au/TiO2 preservation control. For ammonia, the preregistered cost-side Monte Carlo keeps regret positive in all 5,000 draws, with a median of 40.89% and a 5th–95th percentile range of 30.48–54.25%.

A local criterion makes the inversion condition explicit. For a higher-is-better upstream metric s and lower-is-better downstream objective J, a first-order log expansion gives Δln J ≈ -ε_s Δln s + Σ_k ε_k Δln z_k, where the z_k are catalyst-dependent process or economic variables. For candidates with Δln s > 0, the upstream advantage is preserved when the direct-performance term dominates, but it inverts when the aggregate downstream penalty exceeds it. A finite-difference transfer index, χ_AB = ln(J_A/J_B) / ln(s_A/s_B), provides a model-agnostic sign diagnostic of the same outcome: χ_AB < 0 indicates preservation and χ_AB > 0 inversion. The canonical values are +0.085 for Ru versus Fe in ammonia, +0.015 for the methanol STY winner versus the NPC winner, and approximately -1.00 for 2 nm versus 6 nm Au/TiO2. The elasticities and magnitudes are process-specific; the competition between direct catalyst benefit and downstream penalty is the transferable criterion.

The ammonia mechanism is consistent with independent process studies. Aspen-based analyses of high-activity Ru catalysts report that lower-pressure operation can reduce reactant-gas compression while increased ammonia-separation refrigeration, expensive Ru and catalyst lifetime can offset that benefit.[41] Recent Ru reviews likewise emphasize metal price, loading, lifetime and recycling as practical constraints,[43] while comparative low- and high-pressure Haber–Bosch assessments show that pressure materially changes energy and economic performance.[42] These studies do not reproduce the present reduced cost model or the Fe > Ru result, but they support the same multiscale conclusion: a more active catalyst cannot be evaluated independently of the process regime and lifecycle economics it induces.

Backward design converts that mechanism into a materials target. At the baseline, Ru would require a 201.22-fold activity increase to reach Fe parity, and even the 5th-percentile requirement under economic uncertainty is 70.78-fold, far beyond the 2.525-fold maximum state-specific gain found along the present scaling relation. Allowing catalyst lifetime and Ru recovery to improve jointly changes the target geometry: at 99% recovery, a 20-year lifetime reduces the conservative direct-activity requirement to at most 1.401-fold. Physical reachability must nevertheless be tested on the strict scaling manifold rather than inferred from that direct multiplier. The exact 14,136-state calculation places the best point inside the prespecified 20-year/99%-recovery box only 0.071 US dollars per tonne above Fe; parity lies just outside, at 99.119% recovery for 20 years or 22.69 years at 99% recovery. The design output is therefore a narrow property boundary, not a single activity target.

Uncertainty enters at two different levels. Process and economic perturbations retained in FINAL-1.1 leave the Fe–Ru–Os decision and activity-only reachability sign unchanged, while atomistic rank probabilities remain conditional on the descriptor-error model. The supporting Gaussian-copula and common-bias tests show that correlated or shared energetic errors can change top-rank probabilities on a nonlinear volcano even when marginal uncertainties are unchanged. Thus, decision robustness should be reported against both parameter ranges and error structure rather than summarized by a single uncertainty amplitude.

The methanol and Au/TiO2 systems show why the relevant screening variable is reaction- and process-dependent. In ammonia, activity and metal price act mainly through catalyst inventory and the operating regime. In methanol, methane selectivity has much larger local economic leverage than space-time yield because it changes reactant loss, purge, recycle and compression. In the Au/TiO2 control, a common monotonic mapping preserves the upstream order. Screening should therefore target the catalyst property that has sufficient downstream leverage to alter the industrial decision, rather than assuming that one atomic descriptor is universally transferable across processes.

ACSA provides the execution layer for repeating this decision logic under a computational budget. Under constrained budgets, the strongest model reaches the ammonia selection and reachability conclusions below the 206-CU fixed-sequence completion threshold by restricting the process search. Under a non-binding 5,000-CU allowance, that compression disappears: 14 of 20 traces continue after the hindsight stable-decision point, and 8,207 of 17,535 CU (46.8%) are spent after that point. Because stability is defined retrospectively, this fraction is not an online saving estimate; it instead identifies the absence of a reliable operational completion detector when budget pressure is removed. The Agent result is therefore a claim about decision-aware allocation and stopping behavior, not universal computational economy.

The three systems are constructed to resolve distinct modes of multiscale decision propagation. In ammonia, a common descriptor axis connects intrinsic energetics to microkinetics, catalyst demand, process reoptimization and catalyst-responsive economics, making the displacement between atomic and economic optima directly measurable. In methanol, the same decision logic is transferred through a selectivity–purge–recycle pathway, whereas Au/TiO2 provides the complementary regime in which the upstream ordering is preserved after downstream propagation. ACSA then tests whether this deterministic ranking–parity–reachability geometry can be executed adaptively under finite computational budgets. Together, these cases establish a general workflow for locating where catalyst rankings change, measuring the consequence of selecting on an upstream proxy, mapping economic parity back to catalyst-property targets, and testing whether those targets are physically reachable. The framework is modular: formulation-specific catalyst physics and higher-fidelity kinetic, process and economic descriptions can be introduced without changing the decision logic.

## Methods

### Multiscale decision analysis

Each catalytic system is evaluated with a process-level objective matched to the catalyst-to-process pathway under study. For ammonia, the economic objective contains the terms that respond directly to catalyst identity and catalytic performance: catalyst inventory and replacement, reactor requirements, compression, refrigeration and pressure-dependent equipment. Common upstream H₂/N₂ supply contributions are held fixed so that they do not dilute the catalyst-dependent decision signal. The methanol model evaluates net production cost through its recycle and separation process, while the Au/TiO₂ control maps particle-size-dependent activity to required catalyst mass under a common process model. Cross-system comparison is made at the level of ranking propagation and coupling topology rather than by equating the absolute economic objectives of different reactions.

Within each system, rankings before and after process evaluation are compared using Spearman and Kendall correlations, pairwise inversions, top-rank identity and normalized decision regret. For a lower-is-better downstream objective J, regret is defined as R = (J_upstream-winner - J_downstream-winner) / J_downstream-winner. Analyses of the leading candidates are reported separately from full-set statistics so that changes at the top of the ranking are not obscured by agreement among weaker candidates. Backward design varies a selected catalyst property, reoptimizes the process and identifies the value required for economic parity. This value is then compared with the range accessible under the corresponding catalyst-property relation or scaling relation. Perturbation tests change one predefined input while preserving the other model assumptions and repeating the same downstream optimization.

### Ammonia synthesis

The ammonia calculation includes the 15-metal activity screen, scaling and kinetic relations, catalyst productivity and inventory, candidate-specific process optimization, pressure-dependent equipment capital and seven catalyst-dependent cost components. Temperature, pressure and separator temperature are reoptimized for each candidate over the full 14,136-state process grid. Descriptor uncertainty is propagated through 1,000 samples; Fe feasibility is evaluated against the 90 m³ bed-volume limit, and economic winner, preservation of the atomistic winner and top-three membership are recorded separately. The S1 source contains Fe terrace energetics but no Fe step-site row, so the Fe step descriptor is obtained from the 14-metal terrace-to-step scaling relation. Leave-one-out validation and prediction-interval propagation are reported in the Supplementary Information; the regression residual standard error is 0.2271 eV, which is the Fe descriptor uncertainty used in the NH₃-FINAL-1.1 Monte Carlo analysis. The atomistic reference conditions (673 K, 24.5 bar N₂, 74.25 bar H₂ and 1 bar NH₃) and step-site descriptor framework follow the Wang–Abild-Pedersen Dataset S1 source.[39] Under those conditions, the source study places Fe and Ru close to the activity plateau and undercoordinated Os close to the volcano maximum.[39] The descriptor-level reconstruction recovers the same high-activity metal set, with Ru, Os and Fe as its leading three candidates, providing the ranking fidelity required for the downstream decision analysis. The Fe process optimum (425 °C, 180 bar) lies within the conventional industrial Fe Haber–Bosch operating envelope discussed in the ammonia-catalysis and process literature.[40,41] The resulting catalyst-specific optima quantify how intrinsic energetics propagate into operating severity and economic consequence within a common, controlled process model. Published Fe/Ru loop studies provide independent process-level context for these trends despite differences in catalyst formulation, plant scale and flowsheet design.[42] Pressure-CAPEX and equipment-cost diagnostics in FINAL-1.1 perturb the compressor, vessel, cost-index and capital-recovery assumptions by approximately 10–25%; the Fe > Ru > Os economic order and the activity-only reachability verdict are retained, demonstrating that the central decision is robust to these model-form perturbations.

For the Ru-price test, only the Ru metal purchase price is changed. It is first set equal to the Fe value of 8 US dollars per kilogram, after which Ru is reoptimized over the full process grid. The Ru-price sweep repeats this optimization at 241 logarithmically spaced prices between 1 and 3 × 10^5 US dollars per kilogram together with the two reference prices. Fe-Ru parity is located by bisection in log price. The baseline Fe-Ru cost difference is decomposed into the same seven cost components.

The preregistered economic Monte Carlo analysis uses 5,000 samples with seed 20260920. Fe and Ru metal-price multipliers are sampled independently from a log-uniform distribution over 0.5–2.0. A common CAPEX multiplier is sampled uniformly over 0.8–1.2, electricity price uniformly over 20–100 US dollars per megawatt-hour, and catalyst lifetime uniformly over 5–20 years. Fe and Ru are reoptimized for every sample. The Ru activity multiplier required for parity, α*, is recalculated against the sample-specific reoptimized Fe cost. The complete draw sequence has been reconstructed from the frozen seed and priors and reproduces the previously reported Fe/Ru cost and cost-gap quantiles exactly; normalized regret is then computed draw-by-draw as (C_Ru-C_Fe)/C_Fe. The supporting descriptor-correlation sensitivity does not modify this preregistered Monte Carlo. Instead, it preserves the frozen Fe normal marginal and the ±0.15 eV Ru/Os uniform marginals using a Gaussian copula with latent pairwise correlations from 0 to 0.9, and evaluates atomistic ordering on the frozen 673 K one-descriptor volcano. A separate common-bias sweep applies the same additive shift to Ru, Os and Fe descriptors. Neither sensitivity is used to report a new downstream economic probability.

For backward design, Ru intrinsic activity is varied directly and the full process is reoptimized at each activity multiplier. Reachability is evaluated separately along the descriptor path defined by the ammonia scaling relation and over the full process grid. For the joint activity–lifecycle target analysis, catalyst lifetime and Ru recovery are represented through the effective replacement-metal price, P_eff = P_Ru(1-r)(10 y/L). A conservative target region is constructed from the 53 distinct process states already visited by the independently reoptimized Ru price sweep: for each state, catalyst demand is rescaled by a direct activity multiplier and the volume- and pressure-dependent cost terms are recalculated. Restricting the optimization to this state subset can only overestimate the activity improvement required for parity, so the reported multipliers are upper bounds on the backward target.

Physical reachability of that target is then evaluated on the strict *E*_N scaling manifold using all 14,136 process states. At fixed process state, *E*_N affects cost through TOF and therefore the required active-metal inventory and bed volume; the metal-replacement, reactor-volume and pressure-vessel terms are monotone in that requirement, whereas state-specific compression and refrigeration costs are independent of *E*_N. The cost-minimizing descriptor for each state is therefore its TOF-maximizing value on the canonical 0.005-eV scaling grid. Taking the lower envelope across all states gives the lifecycle parity boundary as a function of \(q=(1-r)/L\). The canonical strict-scaling minimum is reproduced before the lifecycle calculation is accepted. The kinetic and scaling relations follow the established transition-metal ammonia-synthesis literature.[5,11,12,39]

### CO₂-to-methanol

The methanol analysis uses a recycle and separation model applied to four Re/TiO₂ states derived from the 1 and 5 wt% catalysts reported by Gothe et al.[13] The reference comparison uses 2% purge. Upstream rankings are reconstructed from space-time yield per gram of Re, single-pass methanol yield and space-time yield per gram of catalyst; the downstream ranking uses net production cost. Re purchase price and catalyst replacement are not included in the reference objective. Purge is varied from 0.5% to 40% over 396 levels, with an additional optimization in which purge is selected separately for each catalyst state. Local economic leverage for space-time yield, conversion and methane suppression is evaluated at the 5 wt% Re, 250 °C state. The process interpretation follows established CO₂-to-methanol assessments in which recycle, compression and separation materially affect plant performance.[14,15]

The measurement Monte Carlo samples the CO₂ conversion and selectivities of each catalyst state from that state's own uncertainty and re-solves the full methanol plant model in each of 5,000 draws (seed 20261005), with every cost parameter held at its reference value. The source reports single integer values, so the uncertainty is built from the source data: the reporting resolution (±0.5 percentage points, with '<1%' sampled over 0–1%), a 5.2% relative uncertainty in conversion derived from the consistency between space-time yield and conversion across the 21 reported runs, and a 0.85-percentage-point exchange between methanol and methane selectivity derived from the 11 runs of a single catalyst. Space-time yield follows conversion and methanol selectivity. Halving or doubling every width retains the economic winner in 99.3% or 78.4% of draws. The 1 wt% Re, 250 °C state uses the Table 3 selectivities of 97% methanol, 1% CO and 1% CH₄.

### Au/TiO₂ rank-preservation control

The Au/TiO₂ control compares 2–6 nm particles under a common operating condition and process mapping. The reference rate, Au loading and dispersion are anchored to Janssens et al.,[18] and the particle-size dependence is taken from the 4.5 wt% Au/TiO₂ series reported by Overbury et al.[19] The broader physical basis for low-temperature Au/TiO₂ activity and particle-size effects follows the established literature.[16,17] Rank preservation is evaluated in 10,000 parameter samples drawn from ranges reported in these studies. An additional analysis introduces candidate-specific kinetic variation and independently optimizes temperature and O₂/CO ratio within the specified ranges derived from the literature.

### Adaptive Catalyst Screening Agent

The Adaptive Catalyst Screening Agent (ACSA) chooses among 11 predefined calculations. At each step it receives the remaining CU budget and the results available so far, selects an admissible calculation, and receives the corresponding numerical result. The kinetic model, process constraints and economic equations are unchanged by this choice. A run is counted as correct only if it identifies the economic winner, the Fe-Ru ordering and the reachability classification. Accuracy of the backward parity multiplier is evaluated separately.

The fixed calculation sequence reaches all three conclusions at 206 CU. Three language-model tiers are evaluated on the same blinded task. The strongest tier is tested across budgets around this value and under a non-binding 5,000-CU allowance; the weaker tiers are evaluated at selected budgets. A reduced process window is counted only when it contains fewer than the full 14,136 process states and is subsequently used in a successful calculation. Decision-stable CU is defined as the cumulative cost at the first point after which all three conclusions remain correct through the end of the run. This is a hindsight metric used to diagnose allocation efficiency, not an online stopping signal. Post-stability overrun is defined as final CU minus decision-stable CU. The frozen policy is not retrospectively modified; an environment-level guard that enforces the explicit S1–S3 completion conditions would constitute a new policy and requires a separate benchmark.

Two reference calculation costs are also reported. Producing only the scored outputs requires 7 CU. The minimum sequence that includes all scientific steps used in the analysis—construction of a process window, activity screening of all 15 metals, resolution of the minimum Fe/Ru/Os process set, backward design and reachability—requires 22 CU. Reported computational costs are reconstructed from the retained calculation histories using the same CU definition and scoring criteria.
