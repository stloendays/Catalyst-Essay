# Supplementary Information

Laboratory catalyst leaders are often not the plant-level optimum

## Supplementary Notes

The Supplementary Notes give the complete results behind each main-text section, with every number the main text summarises. Figure numbers refer to the main-text figures and Extended Data figures. Supplementary Tables 1–8 follow the notes.

### Supplementary Note 1 | Process and economic evaluation reverse the leading ammonia ranking

The 15-metal ammonia screen ranks Ru, Os and Fe first, second and third in intrinsic activity (Fig. 1c), consistent with the established activity landscape for transition-metal ammonia synthesis.[17,18] After optimizing the operating conditions separately for each catalyst and evaluating catalyst-dependent costs (Fig. 1a,d,e), the order becomes Fe, Ru and Os, with costs of 11.516, 16.633 and 19.747 US dollars per tonne of NH₃, respectively (Fig. 1b,h).

The change is concentrated among the leading candidates. For Ru, Os and Fe, Spearman rho is -0.50 and Kendall tau is -0.33, while the Spearman correlation across all 15 metals remains 0.911 (Fig. 1f,g). The overall screen can therefore remain strongly correlated even when the preferred catalyst changes at the top of the ranking. If the intrinsic-activity winner Ru were selected instead of the economic winner Fe, the catalyst-dependent cost objective would increase from 11.516 to 16.633 US dollars per tonne of NH₃, corresponding to a normalized decision regret of **44.4%**. The 5.117 US dollars per tonne difference is a small fraction of the cost of ammonia from state-of-the-art gas-based plants, about 160 US dollars per tonne,[32] but it is the part of the cost that catalyst selection controls and amounts to 1.77 million US dollars per year for a 1,000 t/d plant, which at a capacity factor of 0.95 produces 346,750 t of NH₃ per year.

In the ammonia model, gas-phase thermodynamics follow one measured basis throughout: the NH₃ formation free energy is taken at each temperature from the Gillespie–Beattie equilibrium constant,[64] in place of the DFT gas energies of the activity source, which place it 0.064–0.065 eV per NH₃ higher, and N₂, H₂ and NH₃ enter the loop equilibrium and the microkinetic gas activities as fugacities with Newton–Cooper coefficients,[46] so that the reactor and the loop share one equilibrium. The plant calibration of catalyst productivity is fixed by the fused-iron bed at 400 °C, 80 bar and a −35 °C separator. The model was compared with 64 values from industrial ammonia plants (Supplementary Table 4), with the converter items computed with the measured rate laws of fused iron and Ru/C: 33 lie within their tolerance, among them 17 of the 28 loop states of 13 industrial loop records. Flows and bed volumes at plant conditions all lie within tolerance and space velocities in five of six (typical deviations 16.4%, 28.4% and 29.8%); the model is low in converter-inlet NH₃ (typically 62.9%, at most 72.6%), separator vapour (39.8%), compressor power (42.3%, at most 77.4%) and the outlet NH₃ and bed volume of cost-optimal converter designs (44.9% and 72.9%), and its cost-optimal designs start the first bed 49–56 °C above the industrial window. Supplementary Note 7 gives the Fe–Ru order under these deviations.

### Supplementary Note 2 | An automated agent extends the analysis to published and computed catalysts

The hand-built analyses compare three ammonia metals, four methanol states and five Au particle sizes. ACSA scales the same analysis to every candidate that publications or databases provide, while the kinetic, process and economic models remain unchanged (Fig. 2a). It performs three tasks. It reads each paper and its Supporting Information and records every catalyst entry with composition, loading, operating conditions, conversion, selectivities and rates, together with the table, figure and page each value comes from. It takes every candidate through the forward chain, with a cheap lower bound deciding which candidates need the full process optimization. Before scoring any new candidate it must reproduce the frozen numbers of the hand-built cases.

Extraction was scored on 50 methanol papers: 28 that one manually curated dataset covers (547 curated entries),[33] 20 that a second curated dataset covers (194 entries with conditions and space-time yield),[34] the Re/TiO₂ study that supplies the hand-built methanol states[19] and one further study. Extracted and curated entries are paired on paper, catalyst and conditions only, never on the values being scored; 197 of the 719 pairs have an equally admissible alternative partner and count for recall but not for value accuracy. The agent recovers 719 of the 741 curated entries (97%). Values printed in tables are extracted almost without error: 44 of 44 CO₂ conversions and 44 of 44 methanol selectivities in main-text tables, and 129 of 130 conversions, 124 of 129 selectivities and 118 of 128 space-time yields in the Supporting Information (Extended Data Fig. 1). Values read from main-text plots fall within the strict tolerance for 168 of 203 conversions and 65 of 181 selectivities. Of all extracted entries, 85% are correct in the first 18 papers, where the entries without a curated counterpart were checked against the source (91 of the 150 reviewed are correct, 29 are not yet reviewed), and an estimated 93% in the 31 papers added later, where 77 of a random 87 such entries are correct; most of the correct ones are entries the curated sets do not list. The same comparison finds curated values that the papers contradict: 26 root causes, one mechanism in one source paper each, in 45 rules, 169 cells and 8 rows, across the two methanol datasets and the ammonia review, plus 24 space-time-yield cells that one dataset derives from an erroneous input (Supplementary Table 8). All 21 Re/TiO₂ entries come out exactly.

The self-check passes in all three systems. Taking the extracted Re/TiO₂ entries through the methanol model reproduces the frozen costs of all 21 entries to 2.3 × 10⁻¹³ euros per tonne, including the four hand-built states at 943.30, 961.51, 966.96 and 1,258.17 euros per tonne. In ammonia, the batch code path reproduces the canonical cost of every pure metal to 10⁻⁹ US dollars per tonne. For Au/TiO₂, the rate anchor and the particle-size exponent extracted from the two source papers[24,25] reproduce the required catalyst masses of the 2–6 nm particles, order preservation in all 10,000 literature-envelope samples and the six operating-window stress tests.

Cheap calculations decide where the expensive ones are spent. For each ammonia candidate a lower bound on cost, built from the best-state activity at its descriptor, the smallest reactor and the cheapest process state, requires no process optimization. Across the 1,695 priced bimetallic surfaces this bound excludes 1,399 candidates (82.5%) from the full 14,510-state optimization. Optimizing all 1,695 in full confirms that no candidate below the Fe cost is excluded. In methanol, the plant economics evaluated on lower-bound loop flows at each purge level, valid for any per-pass conversion of recycled CO, replace the equilibrium-constrained purge optimization; taking each comparison group in order of this bound, with the paper's leader always evaluated, spares the full optimization for 394 of the 632 candidates (62.3%) and finds the plant-cost leader of all 81 comparisons, at 38% of the compute of evaluating every candidate.

### Supplementary Note 3 | Published methanol leaderboards and plant-cost leaderboards often disagree

The methanol comparisons come from two extractions with the same pipeline (Methods): 351 operating points with printed conversion, selectivity, temperature, pressure and H₂/CO₂ ratio from 33 papers of the extraction set (Supplementary Table 1) and 348 from 28 of 40 papers published from November 2025 to October 2026 (Supplementary Table 1b). Within each paper, entries tested at the same pressure, H₂/CO₂ ratio and space velocity form the comparison the paper itself makes: 81 comparisons with 632 entries in 50 papers. With plot-read values the same rule gives 161 groups, of which 33 come from 22 papers without printed values and 47 have fewer than two printed entries (Supplementary Table 7). Each entry was taken through the methanol recycle–economics model at its own conditions, with recycled CO converted no further than reverse-water-gas-shift and CO-hydrogenation equilibrium, single-pass conversion capped at the loop's CO₂-hydrogenation equilibrium and the purge optimized for each entry.

The primary loop limits the reactor-inlet content of species other than H₂ and CO₂ (CO, methane and N₂) to 6.86%, the value of the loop that anchors the cost model. The model holds each entry's single-pass conversion at its laboratory value, so a loop without such a limit lets a diluted feed react as in the laboratory. The published loop designs carry 4.4–9.8% CO, methane and N₂ at the reactor inlet: 6.46% in the anchor loop (1.52% CO, 4.95% N₂; 6.78% with methanol and water; the cost model's limit is 6.86%), 9.85% in its three-reactor variant, and 4.74% and 4.4% in two further designs (4.9–10.2% of all species other than H₂ and CO₂). CO counts toward the limit although recycled CO still reacts up to the reverse-water-gas-shift and CO-hydrogenation bound: at the cost-optimal purge it is converted little further and accumulates. Without the limit the reactor-inlet CO is a median 8.8% (10th and 90th percentiles 0.9% and 26%), against 1.3–4.7% in the published designs.

Compared catalyst by catalyst, each at its best measured operating point (highest space-time yield for the laboratory, lowest plant cost for the plant; catalyst identity as defined in Methods) and with each entry free to load more catalyst and raise its single-pass conversion towards equilibrium at its measured selectivity, the laboratory leader is not the plant-cost leader in 21 of the 63 comparisons with at least two catalysts (33.3%; paper-cluster bootstrap 95% confidence interval 22.1–45.9%), from 19 of their 46 papers; the cost gap exceeds 1%, 5% and 10% in 14, 10 and 8 of them (median 3.5%), and the 10 above 5% come from 9 papers. The counts are the same with conversion raised to 90, 95 or 99% of equilibrium. Merging samples that differ only in synthesis gives 18 of 52 (34.6%; 21.8–47.5%), 9 above 5%. With every entry held at its laboratory conversion, the count is 26 of 63 (41.3%; 29.4–53.3%), from 21 papers, with the cost gap above 1%, 5% and 10% in 23, 19 and 13 (median 9.6%; the 19 above 5% from 16 papers), and 22 of 52 (42.3%; 28.3–55.2%; 15 above 5%) with the stricter identity. The two treatments of catalyst amount bracket the share of comparisons in which the plant selects another catalyst at 33–41%; this range spans two model treatments and is not a confidence interval. Of the 112 catalysts measured at two or more temperatures (43 comparisons, 21 papers), 82 have their lowest plant cost at another temperature than their highest space-time yield at laboratory conversion, in 36 comparisons from 19 papers; the plant temperature is lower for 75 of them (median 50 °C lower) and higher for 7, and running the 82 at their highest-yield temperature costs more than 5% extra for 74 (median 78%). With conversion raised, 83 of the 112 change temperature, 82 of them to a lower one.

Over every operating point of every catalyst, the space-time-yield leader is not the plant-cost leader in 50 of 81 comparisons (61.7%; 48.9–72.2%), 42 with a cost gap above 5%, in 32 of the 50 papers; 1,992 of 5,283 pairwise orderings invert. Of the 50, 26 are the changes of catalyst at laboratory conversion above, 13 are comparisons in which one catalyst leads both rankings at different operating points and 11 are comparisons of a single catalyst. The plant-cost leader is more selective in 40 of the 50 and makes less CO in 39. Ranking by single-pass yield gives 50 of 81, by conversion 54 of 81 and by methanol selectivity 30 of 81. Without the inlet limit the leaders differ in 25 of 81 comparisons (30.9%; 16.9–44.6%), 10 above 5%; selectivity is then the worst proxy (61 of 81) and the plant-cost leader has the higher single-pass conversion in 11 of the 25. With the purge grid extended to 10⁻⁴%, 630 of 632 entries have their unconstrained optimum below 0.5% and the leaders differ in 26 of 81 (5 above 5%). A high-pressure Cu/Al₂O₃ study[36] supplies 8 of the 81 comparisons and the largest regrets without the limit.

Re-measuring every entry 2,000 times keeps 42 of the 50 disagreements of the inert-limited loop in at least 90% of draws (33 and 25 at twice and four times the error; 16 of 25 without the limit); measurement noise alone changes a paper's space-time-yield leader in 9.1 comparisons on average (95% range 5–14), and the response surface disagrees with exact runs in 9 of 1,215 group verdicts. In 26 of the 50 disagreements the paper leader meets the limit only with a large purge and costs more than twice its cost without the limit (near-infeasible); all 26 persist under re-measurement. The other 24 involve no such jump; 17 of them exceed 5% and 16 persist under re-measurement.

Over inlet limits of 4, 5, 6, 6.86, 8, 10, 12, 15, 20, 25 and 30% the space-time-yield leader differs in 54, 49, 49, 50, 47, 42, 41, 34, 32, 27 and 26 comparisons (the first two of 80 feasible groups, the rest of 81); counting only methane and N₂ gives 25–30 (28 at 6.86%, 11 above 5%), and the excess over the unlimited loop stays above zero up to 20% and vanishes at 25% (Extended Data Fig. 10). The result holds without the paper that supplies most comparisons (43 of 73, 37 above 5%), for entries whose products close (30 of 50) and with recycled CO treated as inert (56 of 81). Recycled CO converted up to CO-hydrogenation equilibrium gives 50 of 81 (35 above 5%; 24 without the limit), a catalyst loading more catalyst to raise its conversion 46 of 81 (36 above 5%, from 22 papers; another catalyst at the same temperature above 5% in 8 groups of 7 papers; 31 without the limit), composition-priced catalysts 47 of 81 (42 above 5%; 24 without the limit), unreported CO–methane splits at either bound 48–55 of 81 (23–37 without the limit), plot-read entries added 101 of 160 comparisons in 80 papers (89 above 5%; 58 of 161 without the limit) and the plant-term variants 41–50 of 81 (24–26 without the limit).

The choice of the limit matters little for the catalyst a plant would pick: the plant-cost leader at 6.86% is within 5% of the cheapest operating point at every limit from 5 to 10% in 62 of 81 comparisons, against 63 for an oracle that knows every limit's costs (the leader at 6.86% is that oracle's choice in 70), 45 for the most selective entry and 31 for the paper's space-time-yield leader.

### Supplementary Note 4 | Measured ammonia catalysts rank differently by laboratory rate and by plant cost

ACSA also takes measured catalysts through the ammonia chain. It extracted the 164 catalyst rows tabulated in a review of ammonia-synthesis catalysts;[37] two independent extraction passes agree on 697 of 706 numeric fields, and every value was checked against the printed tables. Checking the rows against the primary papers (below) found ten transcription errors in nine rows, among them a space velocity printed ten times too low and two Ru-free catalysts listed as Ru catalysts; the chain uses the papers' values. Each catalyst's measured rate per gram of metal, at its laboratory temperature, pressure and NH₃ fraction, is inverted into an effective descriptor on its metal's side of the volcano, and the catalyst is then optimized over the full process library with its own metal content. The plant calibration reproduces the laboratory rates of the two fused-iron catalysts in the tables within a factor of 1.9 (α = 0.77 and 0.54).

Of the 73 catalysts in the primary set (54 Ru, 9 Fe, 5 Co and 5 Ni), the one with the highest laboratory rate, Ru/AC-G, costs 13.76 US dollars per tonne, 25% above the plant-cost leader, and laboratory rate and plant cost are uncorrelated (Spearman ρ = 0.07). In both studies that test Fe and Ru on the same support, Ru has the higher rate and Fe the lower plant cost (17.8% and 12.9% regret). Two measured catalysts undercut the fused-iron benchmark without metal recovery, a Cs- and Ba-promoted Ru catalyst (Ru/Cs/Ba/CCHT, 11.01 US dollars per tonne) and a BaH₂-promoted Fe catalyst (20% Fe–BaH₂, 11.19), by 4% and 3%; with 90% Ru recovery four Ru catalysts do.

A review tabulates catalysts measured in different laboratories, whereas a paper's own comparison is between catalysts tested side by side. ACSA therefore extracted 52 primary ammonia-synthesis papers with their Supporting Information: 30 (Supplementary Table 2) and a further 22 of 25 papers screened under a protocol committed before extraction (Supplementary Table 2b). For the 37 entries that the review also tabulates (one review row is printed twice), every extracted temperature, pressure, metal content and space velocity agrees with the paper, space velocity is extracted for 29 of the 34 entries that state one, and the rate in 36 of 37 is correct; a random check of 40 further entries against the papers finds 35 correct. Within each paper, catalysts with printed rate, temperature and outlet that were tested at the same temperature, pressure, H₂/N₂ ratio and space velocity form 44 comparisons in 31 papers. The catalyst with the highest rate per gram of catalyst is not the plant-cost leader in 12 of them (27%; 95% confidence interval 14–42% from resampling papers), in 10 of 31 papers, and 8 of the 12 have a regret above 5% (median 13%). A commercial fused-iron reference is the plant-cost leader in 5 of the 12, a catalyst of another metal in 3 (an Fe catalyst on the same support in all 3), the same metal and content on another support or with another promoter in 2 and the same metal at another content in 2. Without the commercial fused-iron references the leaders differ in 8 of 44; ranking by rate per gram of metal gives 15 of 44, 90% Ru recovery 10 of 44, and with plot-read entries included the leaders differ in 50 of 177 comparisons. In 4 of the 44 comparisons the plant-cost leader undercuts the fused-iron benchmark: the three commercial fused-iron references (9.83–9.84 US dollars per tonne) and a 20 wt% Co@BaO/MgO catalyst at 400 °C and 6.1 MPa (11.23).

### Supplementary Note 5 | Selectivity, recycle and purge reshape the methanol ranking

The same ranking change appears in CO₂ hydrogenation to methanol through a different process pathway. Four literature-derived Re/TiO₂ catalyst-temperature states were compared (Fig. 3a,b).[19] Using space-time yield per gram of Re as the catalyst metric, the order is 1 wt% Re at 250 °C, 1 wt% Re at 200 °C, 5 wt% Re at 200 °C and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C. After propagation through the recycle and separation model at the 2% purge used in the reference case, the economic order becomes 5 wt% Re at 200 °C, 1 wt% Re at 250 °C, 1 wt% Re at 200 °C and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C, with net production costs of 943, 962, 967 and 1,258 euros per tonne, respectively (Fig. 3c).

The state with the highest space-time yield per gram of Re falls from first to second economically, and the state ranked third by space-time yield is the economic optimum. Selecting the upstream winner would give 961.51 euros per tonne instead of the 943.30 euros per tonne economic optimum, a normalized decision regret of **1.93%**. Across the four states, Spearman rho is 0.40, Kendall tau is 0.33 and two of six pairwise comparisons invert (Fig. 3e). Methane formation consumes reactants and increases gas accumulation, purge, recycle and compression requirements. A local sensitivity analysis at the 5 wt% Re, 250 °C state gives economic leverage values of 0.37579 for methane suppression, 0.05883 for single-pass conversion and 0.00289 for space-time yield (Fig. 3a,g). These sensitivities are consistent with previous CO₂-to-methanol process studies in which recycle, compression and separation make substantial contributions to plant performance.[20,21]

The reordering persists across the full purge range considered. Over 396 purge levels from 0.5% to 40%, the state with the highest space-time yield per gram of Re is never the least-cost option; Spearman rho never exceeds 0.80 and at least one of six pairwise comparisons remains inverted. Optimizing purge separately for each catalyst state changes the detailed order but does not restore the space-time-yield ranking (Fig. 3f).

Counterfactual and backward calculations locate the cause. Moving every state's methane selectivity into methanol reverses the correlation to rho = −0.60 and makes the highest-conversion state the least costly, whereas equalizing conversion raises rho to +1.00. For the space-time-yield winner, no increase in space-time yield reaches parity: unlimited productivity still costs 954.64 euros per tonne against 943.30. Raising its single-pass conversion from 0.23 to 0.279, inside the 0.19–0.40 range measured on the same catalyst family, or reaching complete methanol selectivity does. As in ammonia, the catalyst property that ranks the candidates upstream is not the one that can move the industrial decision.

Propagating each state's own measurement uncertainty separates the robust positions from the unresolved one (Fig. 3d). In 5,000 draws in which the CO₂ conversion and selectivities of every state are sampled independently and the full plant model is re-solved, the economic winner keeps first place in 4,559 draws (91.2%) and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C remains last in all 5,000. The second and third places, 5.5 euros per tonne apart, exchange in 1,054 draws (21.1%). The space-time-yield winner becomes the economic optimum in 413 draws (8.3%), and the complete space-time-yield ranking is recovered in 24 draws (0.5%).

### Supplementary Note 6 | Alloy surfaces that undercut Fe pair a cheap 3d metal with a group-6 metal

The 15-metal screen contains only three economically feasible candidates. To test whether the Fe preference survives a much larger candidate space, ACSA took the 1,796 bimetallic and pure-metal surfaces of a high-throughput adsorption database[38,39] through the ammonia chain. The most stable N adsorption energy of each surface is placed on the model's step-site descriptor by the terrace-to-step relation that the workbook already uses for Fe, after a linear bridge between the two datasets over the 14 pure metals they share. A second, element-anchored bridge places each of the 14 metals with a frozen descriptor (Ag, Au, Co, Cu, Fe, Ir, Mo, Os, Pd, Pt, Re, Rh, Ru, W) exactly at that descriptor. Metal prices are the frozen model prices for the 15 model metals and USGS annual averages for 21 further elements (2025; Hg 2024); Tc has no market price.[72]

Among the 372 surfaces that contain only transition metals outside groups 3–5, 137 satisfy the 90 m³ bed-volume limit. Eight undercut Fe under the linear bridge: Cu₃Cr (10.14 US dollars per tonne), Fe₃Mo, Ni₃W, Fe₃W, Cu₃Mo, Co₃Mo, Co₃W and Ni₃Mo. Nine do so under the element-anchored bridge: Cu₃Cr, Cu₃Mo, CuMo, MoNi, CoMo, CoW, Fe₃Mo, Ni₃W and Fe₃W (Extended Data Fig. 6). Every one of them pairs Fe, Co, Ni or Cu with Cr, Mo or W, the combination that descriptor interpolation has identified for ammonia synthesis.[40] The individual alloys depend on the bridge; the family does not. The most active surface ranks 12th economically (Ni₃Re, 28.0% regret) under the linear bridge and 45th (Rh₃Re, 86.9%) under the element-anchored bridge, with Spearman correlations of 0.71 and 0.67 between activity and cost. As in the 15-metal screen, the activity leader is not the economic leader. Counterfactual and backward calculations, run only on these leading surfaces, show the same mechanism as for Ru. At the Fe price both activity leaders would undercut Fe (10.15 and 10.33 US dollars per tonne), whereas at their own prices they need 5.5-fold and 421-fold activity increases to reach Fe parity, and no descriptor on the strict scaling range brings them below 12.95 and 18.84 US dollars per tonne. Cu₃Cr, Fe₃Mo, Ni₃W, Fe₃W and Cu₃Mo remain below Fe under both bridges, and Cu₃Cr keeps its lead with 13% of its activity. Surfaces containing group 3–5 elements, which form stable bulk nitrides, and surfaces containing sp metals, which lie outside the transition-metal scaling relations of the activity model, are reported as separate layers (Supplementary Table 3; Extended Data Fig. 6).

### Supplementary Note 7 | Metal price and process optimization jointly determine the Fe-Ru ranking

The leading metals favor different operating conditions. Fe has an interior optimum near 425 °C and 105 bar, whereas Ru favors approximately 450 °C and 220 bar, both with a 30 °C separator; Os has a broad, shallow minimum at high pressure (Fig. 4b,c). The economic comparison therefore reflects both catalyst properties and the operating conditions favored by each catalyst.

To isolate the effect of metal price, we repeated the Ru optimization after setting its metal price equal to the Fe value of 8 US dollars per kilogram. All other model inputs, constraints and costing assumptions were unchanged. The Ru optimum shifts from 450 °C and 220 bar to 400 °C and 95 bar, and its optimized cost falls to 10.237 US dollars per tonne of NH₃, 1.279 US dollars per tonne below Fe (Fig. 4b–e). Thus removing the Ru-Fe price difference reverses the economic order after process reoptimization.

A full Ru-price sweep, with process reoptimization at every price, places Fe-Ru parity at 403.54 US dollars per kilogram. At this price, Ru operates at 425 °C and 110 bar with a 3.82 m³ catalyst bed (Fig. 4d). Across the sweep, the optimum moves from 95 bar and an 18.2 m³ bed at 1 US dollar per kilogram to 340 bar and a 0.011 m³ bed at 3 × 10^5 US dollars per kilogram. Metal price therefore affects the final ranking directly through catalyst cost and indirectly by changing the preferred process conditions.

A real Ru catalyst holds far less metal than the common fused-iron formulation that the 15-metal model gives every metal. A promoted Ru/C ammonia catalyst exposes 11% of its Ru atoms,[41] against fewer than 1% of the Fe atoms in reduced fused iron;[42] the commercial catalyst carries about 8 wt% Ru,[43] and 90–95.55% of the Ru in spent promoted Ru/C ammonia catalysts is recovered (93.05–95.55%[44] and 91.1–95.4%[45] in the examples with Ba, K and Mg promoters). The supported catalyst is therefore compared with fused iron on its own bed (8 wt% Ru at 490 kg m⁻³) and with the measured rate laws of both catalysts, which also replace the descriptor kinetics where these depart furthest from measurement.

At 90 bar, 400 °C and 2% NH₃ the descriptor kinetics give Ru apparent orders of +2.4 in H₂ and −1.9 in NH₃ and fused iron −0.1 in NH₃. For the two frontier catalysts the rates were therefore replaced by measured rate laws: the Model 3 law of Keestra et al. for commercial fused iron,[48] fitted from 1 to 324 bar, with the activity factor of the Topsoe KM1R catalyst whose data Dyson and Simon fitted[46,47] (apparent orders N₂ +1.00, H₂ +0.71, NH₃ −0.89) and the Ru/C rate equation of Rossetti et al.[41] (N₂ +0.53, H₂ +0.32, NH₃ −0.39); the Ru/C law lowers the hydrogen order but does not make it negative. The Ru/C law reproduces the 125 measured points of its source with a mean error of 10.1%; the fused-iron law gives 9.3% NH₃ against 8% measured for a commercial magnetite catalyst at 100 bar, 430 °C and 30,000 h⁻¹, and at 151–313 bar, where Dyson and Simon fitted the same catalyst, it requires 1.05–2.3 times their bed. Integrated in plug flow, each calibrated on its own measured rate and each optimized only within its fitted pressure range (fused iron 1–324 bar, Ru/C 50–100 bar), the pair gives Fe 15.31 US dollars per tonne at 450 °C, 110 bar and a 15 °C separator and Ru in the benchmark formulation 45.87 at 100 bar; the activity multiple for parity is 15.68 (15.68–45.39 across inlet-rate or plug-flow sizing and own-rate or fused-iron calibration), against 114.67 with the descriptor kinetics. Supported Ru/C (8 wt% Ru, 490 kg m⁻³, 95.55% recovery) has its optimum at 100 bar and costs 3.83 US dollars per tonne less than Fe without metal financing and 2.13 more at 12% per year (KAAP-type loop, inside both fitted ranges: 1.21 less and 1.95 more). It stays cheaper than Fe up to a metal financing rate of 6.49% per year, and in the KAAP-type loop up to 4.57% per year (5.98% and 4.00% at 90% recovery); priced as a supplier lease without deposit, with the operator's other cash flows at 12% per year, the break-even lease rates are 6.81% and 5.00% per year. At the reference rate of 2.10% per year, the median of 32 fixed-rate platinum and palladium catalyst-metal leases that one refiner disclosed for 2013–2022 (1.35–5.10% per year),[50] Ru/C is 1.99 US dollars per tonne cheaper than Fe at the two catalysts' own optima and 0.65 cheaper in the KAAP-type loop; across the disclosed range, 1.35–5.10% per year, it is 2.37–0.58 cheaper at the own optima, and in the KAAP-type loop 0.85 cheaper at 1.35% and 0.14 dearer at 5.10% per year. Across 16 cases that vary the Fe catalyst price, the Ru/C bed density, recovery, the Ru price, catalyst life, the effectiveness factor of either catalyst, the converter pressure premium and the bed sizing, Ru/C is cheaper than Fe without metal financing in all 16 in the main loop and in 15 in the KAAP-type loop, all but inlet-rate sizing, and the break-even financing rate spans 1.23–15.65% per year in the main loop and 0.71–8.43% per year in the KAAP-type loop. At 12% per year Fe is cheaper in 15 of the 16 cases in both loops, all but a halved Fe effectiveness, which in the KAAP-type loop leaves no Fe bed within the 90 m³ limit.

With both catalysts available, a three-bed adiabatic converter with interbed cooling at 9 MPa (fused iron up to 530 °C, Ru/C up to 460 °C, the top of its measured range; 8% inerts; −20 °C separator), with both rate laws inside their fitted pressure ranges, is cheapest as fused iron upstream with Ru/C downstream for Ru financing rates of 0–5.46% per year, the bed order of the Kellogg Advanced Ammonia Process converter[49] and of published Fe + Ru multibed designs,[51] and as all-Fe above 5.46% per year; the Ru/C share of the catalyst volume falls from 69% to 5% across the window, and a converter with Ru/C first is optimal in no case tested. The Ru/C rate advantage over fused iron per kilogram grows downstream, from 9-fold at 2% to 62-fold at 14% NH₃ (90 bar, 400 °C), because ammonia inhibits fused iron more strongly; fused iron takes the first, hottest bed because Ru/C is held to 460 °C. The window moves with recovery (0–4.92% per year at 90%) and with Ru price (0.12–8.21% at −30%, with all-Ru/C cheapest below 0.12%; 0–4.03% at +30%). The disclosed lease rates fall inside the base window, where staging saves 0.53, 0.37 and 0.02 US dollars per tonne over an all-Fe converter at 1.35, 2.10 and 5.10% per year. At the reference 2.10% per year the least-cost converter is fused iron followed by two Ru/C beds (5.8, 1.2 and 1.9 m³; fused iron 65% of the catalyst volume), at 17.95 against 18.32 US dollars per tonne for all-Fe; in the KAAP converter the first, iron bed holds about half of the catalyst.[52]

An independent loop model reproduces the cost model in the KAAP-type loop. It solves a five-component (N₂, H₂, CH₄, Ar, NH₃) Peng–Robinson loop with the temperature-dependent NH₃ binary interaction parameters of Mace and Vrabec,[71] two cold flashes, purge, make-up and a converged recycle, and sizes each bed with the same measured rate laws for a common 1,500 t/d of net NH₃. At 90 bar with a −20 °C separator, where both rate laws are inside their fitted ranges, it gives Ru/C 1.91 US dollars per tonne of decision-relevant cost dearer than fused iron at 12% per year and 95.55% Ru recovery, against 1.95 in the cost model, and a financing break-even of 5.42% per year, against 4.57% (temperature, conversion and purge reselected on the Peng–Robinson grid at each rate). Selected fixed beds integrated forward inside the recycle return 1,500.000 t/d without a prescribed conversion, with component residuals below 10⁻⁹.

The baseline Ru-Fe cost difference of 5.117 US dollars per tonne is distributed across several cost components. Fresh-feed compression contributes +3.988 US dollars per tonne, metal inventory +1.694 and compressor capital +1.024, while vessel pressure, recycle compression and reactor-base terms partly offset these contributions; neither optimum uses refrigeration (Fig. 4a,e).

The ranking does not depend on the recycle-compression boundary of the loop model. The model charges a 3 bar pressure rise to the recycle compressor; the 1,500 t/d Krupp–Uhde flowsheet[70] shows 9.2 bar between the cold recycle stream and the converter inlet. With full reoptimization of all 15 metals over the 14,510 process states, a 6.0 bar rise gives Fe 12.541, Ru 16.878 and Os 19.856 US dollars per tonne and a 9.2 bar rise 13.595, 17.129 and 19.966. Fe stays near 425 °C and 105–110 bar, Ru at 450 °C and 220 bar and Os at 450 °C and 340–345 bar, all with a 30 °C separator; the feasible order is Fe, Ru, Os and the rank order of all 15 metals is identical to the baseline (Spearman 1.000). Ru remains 3.53 US dollars per tonne above Fe at 9.2 bar, and its activity multiple for parity, 67-fold, remains far beyond the gain reachable along its scaling relation.

A layer-wise check localizes the reversal more precisely. At the common 673 K atomistic reference, converting turnover frequency into the plant-throughput active-metal requirement preserves the leading order: Ru requires less active metal than Os, and both require far less than Fe. Multiplying those same catalyst demands by the canonical metal prices and the frozen 10-year lifetime changes the order to Fe < Ru < Os before any reactor, compression, refrigeration or equipment cost is added: the annualized metal replacement is 0.204 US dollars per tonne of NH₃ for Fe, 4.09 for Ru and 22.28 for Os. As an independent causal check, setting the Ru metal price equal to the Fe price and reoptimizing the complete 14,510-state process leaves Ru less costly than Fe (10.237 versus 11.516 US dollars per tonne of NH₃). The inversion is therefore triggered at the catalyst-demand-to-lifecycle-economics interface. Candidate-specific process optimization does not close it: Ru moves to a small, high-pressure bed, the Ru − Fe difference goes from 3.89 US dollars per tonne in metal replacement at the common reference to 5.117 in total, and most of it moves into fresh-feed compression, metal inventory and compressor capital.

The Fe–Ru order does not depend on how closely the model reproduces industrial plants (Supplementary Table 4b). The 32 benchmark items outside tolerance trace to five model components: the separator vapour (converter-inlet NH₃ and the separator table), the approach to equilibrium (outlet NH₃), compression power, catalyst amount (bed volume) and the effective bed temperature. Each was perturbed by the typical and by the largest deviation of its items, in both directions, and Fe, Ru and Os were reoptimized over the perturbed 14,510-state library. With the descriptor kinetics no single perturbation reverses Fe and Ru (28 cases; smallest margin +1.34 US dollars per tonne, with compression power ×0.226) or Fe and Os. With all five components at their typical deviation, Fe stays ahead in every one of the 64 sign combinations (smallest margin +2.21) and by +2.36 when each moves in the direction that favours Ru; at the largest deviations that combination leaves +0.26, while 18 of 64 sign combinations reverse the order, every one of them with the Fe bed multiplied by 5.61, the largest deviation of a cost-optimal design volume (B15), and with the largest deviation of a bed at plant conditions (×1.68) the margin is +4.74. With the measured rate laws, each inside its fitted pressure range, Ru in the benchmark bed stays behind fused iron in every single case and every combination at the typical deviations (smallest margin +15.2 US dollars per tonne); at the largest deviations it is ahead only where the Fe bed multiplied by 7.58, the largest deviation of a cost-optimal design volume, no longer fits the 90 m³ limit. Supported Ru/C with recovery is 3.83 US dollars per tonne cheaper than fused iron without metal financing and 2.13 dearer at 12% per year; typical deviations move these differences by −2.31 to +1.18 and −1.23 to +1.16 singly (−1.38 to +0.41 and −0.63 to +1.20 in the KAAP-type loop), and no single deviation of typical size reverses the order at either rate. At 12% per year, 5 of the 64 combinations of typical deviations put Ru/C ahead (8 of 64 in the KAAP-type loop).

Descriptor uncertainty affects feasibility and ranking differently. Across 1,000 descriptor-uncertainty samples, Fe satisfies the 90 m³ bed-volume limit in 79.2% of samples, ranks first economically in 65.3% (653/1000), and remains within the top three in 92.3%. The atomistic winner remains the economic winner in 27.1% of samples (Fig. 4f,g).

Economic uncertainty around the baseline case does not overturn the Fe preference. Across 5,000 samples that vary metal price, the CAPEX coefficient, electricity price and catalyst lifetime, full process reoptimization gives P(C_Fe < C_Ru) = 1.000, with a minimum sampled Ru-Fe cost difference of 1.995 US dollars per tonne (Fig. 4h). Expressed as normalized selection regret, R = (C_Ru − C_Fe)/C_Fe, the 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles are **31.52%, 42.21% and 56.99%**, respectively, and R remains positive in all 5,000 draws. The Ru activity multiplier required for economic parity is 43.52-fold, 104.97-fold and 262.28-fold at the 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles, respectively (Fig. 6a).

### Supplementary Note 8 | Lifecycle engineering, not activity, brings Ru to parity

The forward analysis identifies the preferred catalyst but does not show how much Ru would need to improve to reach parity with Fe. We therefore multiplied Ru intrinsic activity by a factor α and reoptimized the full process at each value. Under the baseline economic assumptions, Ru reaches the Fe cost only after a 114.67-fold activity increase (Fig. 6a).

The accessible range defined by the scaling relation is much smaller. At 673 K, the largest activity increase along the scaling relation is 1.023-fold, and the largest increase found across all allowed process conditions is 1.575-fold. Under strict scaling, the lowest Ru cost is 16.455 US dollars per tonne of NH₃ at *E*_N = −1.180 eV, which remains above the Fe cost (Fig. 6b). The 114.67-fold requirement therefore lies far outside the range accessible by moving Ru along the present scaling relation. Even the 5th-percentile requirement under economic uncertainty, 43.52-fold, remains well above the 1.575-fold maximum (Fig. 6a).

Measured catalysts show which modifications supply gains of the required size (Fig. 6a, bottom). Against unpromoted Ru on the same support, Ba and Cs promotion of Ru/C raise the turnover frequency per surface Ru 75- and 65-fold,[53] Ba promotion of Ru/BN raises the rate more than 100-fold at 5 MPa,[54] and Cs promotion of Ru/MgO more than 134-fold at 350 °C (more than 30-fold at 300 °C, a lower bound);[55] all four lie within the 43.52–262-fold parity band, and the 134-fold Cs–Ru/MgO gain exceeds the 114.67-fold central requirement. Electride and oxyhydride supports give gains of about tenfold (9.8-fold for Ru/C12A7:e⁻ and 8.4-fold for Ru/BaTiO₂.₅H₀.₅ at 5 MPa),[56,57] a Ru–Ba core–shell on calcium amide at least 33.5-fold against Cs–Ru/MgO,[73] and confinement inside carbon nanotubes lowers activity to 0.5-fold.[58] Electronic promotion, which changes the energetics beyond a single scaling relation, is the measured route that reaches the parity band. Most of these gains were measured at 0.1–1 MPa; the Ba–Ru/BN and oxyhydride results show the effect at 5 MPa.

The same parity calculation can be written as a two-property lifecycle target at fixed Ru intrinsic activity. The fully reoptimized Ru price sweep gives an effective Ru parity price of 403.54 US dollars per kilogram. Because metal recovery and catalyst lifetime enter the Ru replacement-cost term multiplicatively with metal price, the corresponding boundary is \(P_{Ru}(1-r)(10\,\mathrm{y}/L) \le 403.54\). At the baseline Ru price of 53,852.5 US dollars per kilogram, this requires approximately 99.25% recovery for a 10-year lifetime, 98.50% for a 20-year lifetime, or a 13.3-year lifetime at 99% recovery. The prespecified 5–20-year lifetime range and 99% optimistic recovery bound therefore reach parity at the current Ru activity for lifetimes above 13.3 years.

Allowing the backward variables to move together changes the required target before physical reachability is tested. We conservatively evaluated the 42 distinct process states already visited by the fully reoptimized Ru price sweep, rescaling catalyst demand by a direct Ru activity multiplier and recomputing the volume- and pressure-dependent cost terms at each state. Because this search uses only a subset of the 14,510-state library, the resulting activity requirements are conservative upper bounds. With 99% Ru recovery, a 10-year catalyst life requires at most a 1.291-fold activity increase, whereas 15- and 20-year lives require none (direct activity multipliers of at most 0.904 and 0.710); a 20-year lifetime with 98% recovery requires at most 1.291-fold.

We then tested this target region directly on the strict *E*_N scaling manifold across all 14,510 process states. The canonical strict-scaling minimum, 16.455 US dollars per tonne at *E*_N = −1.180 eV, is reproduced before lifecycle changes are introduced. The joint parity boundary is controlled by the catalyst-loss factor \(q=(1-r)/L\), with \(q^*=8.2510\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{y}^{-1}\). The prespecified range of catalyst lifetimes up to 20 years and Ru recovery up to 99% crosses this boundary: at 20 years and 99% recovery the best strict-scaling state occurs at *E*_N = −1.190 eV and costs 11.199 US dollars per tonne, 0.317 US dollars per tonne below Fe, and at 15 years and 99% recovery it costs 11.361, 0.154 below Fe. Parity requires 98.350% recovery at a 20-year lifetime or, equivalently, a 12.12-year lifetime at 99% recovery. Lifecycle engineering thus reaches parity inside the present scaling-constrained design space, where activity alone cannot.

### Supplementary Note 9 | Catalyst effects on process operation determine whether rankings change

The ammonia and methanol results identify different routes by which catalyst properties affect process cost. In ammonia, intrinsic activity and metal price determine catalyst inventory and the preferred operating conditions, which in turn change compression, reactor and equipment costs. In methanol, selectivity changes reactant losses, gas accumulation, purge, recycle and compression.

Au/TiO₂ CO oxidation provides a control in which the relation between particle size and catalyst requirement is monotonic. Low-temperature CO oxidation on supported Au is well known to depend on particle size and support effects.[22,23] The present calculation uses the absolute-rate anchor reported by Janssens et al.[24] and the particle-size dependence measured by Overbury et al.[25] at the closest Au loading. Candidate states differ only in Au particle size from 2 to 6 nm under the same operating condition and process model.

The particles share one metal and one loading, so a common Au price and a support cost proportional to catalyst mass leave every cost ratio equal to the ratio of required catalyst mass: 1, 2.16, 3.73, 5.70 and 8.06 for 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 nm at the nominal size exponents (n = 0.9 for the particle-size dependence, m = 1.0 for dispersion; Extended Data Fig. 8a). A Monte Carlo that draws one pair (n, m) and applies it to all five sizes keeps the order in every draw by construction, because the requirement is then a monotone power of the diameter; the evidence about the catalyst system comes from giving each size its own activity. With an independent log-normal scatter of σ = 0.25 (natural-log units) on top of shared exponents drawn from n ~ U(0.7, 2.0) and m ~ U(0.8, 1.2), the full order 2 > 3 > 4 > 5 > 6 nm holds in 77.70% of 10,000 draws (mean ρ = 0.9754) and 2 nm particles remain the least-cost catalyst in 99.41%; adjacent pairs invert in 0.59% (3/2 nm), 3.56% (4/3), 7.74% (5/4) and 11.13% (6/5) of draws, as the nominal gaps shrink with size, and no draw is fully inverted. At σ = 0.10, 0.50 and 0.69 the full order holds in 99.66%, 35.44% and 21.20% and the 2 nm leader in 100%, 90.02% and 80.63% (Extended Data Fig. 8b). With candidate-specific kinetic and operating variation (temperature and O₂/CO ratio optimized per candidate), the exact order is retained in 92.16% of 10,000 samples over 273.15–293.15 K (mean ρ = 0.99214) and in 72.62% over 273.15–313.15 K (mean ρ = 0.96802).

Catalyst life is the one channel that can reverse the order. For sizes i < j the larger particles become the cheaper catalyst when τ_j/τ_i exceeds m_j/m_i, that is, for adjacent pairs at life ratios of 2.161 (3/2 nm), 1.727 (4/3), 1.528 (5/4) and 1.414 (6/5). If life scales as τ ∝ d^λ, cost scales as d^(n+m−λ) and every pair inverts at λ = n + m = 1.9 (1.5–3.2 over the exponent ranges). With per-size scatter the deterministic line broadens into a band (Extended Data Fig. 8c); with the shared exponents sampled, 2 nm particles remain the least-cost catalyst in 58.1% of draws at λ = 1.8 and 45.7% at λ = 2.0, and the order is fully inverted in more than half of the draws only beyond λ ≈ 3.8. Combined with the candidate-specific operating variation over 273–293 K, the full order holds in 92.16%, 77.48%, 47.36%, 8.99% and 0.11% of draws at λ = 0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5 and 1.9, the 2 nm leader in 100%, 97.56% (λ = 1.0), 61.61% (1.5) and 5.21% (1.9), and the order is fully inverted in 64.50% at λ = 2.5 and 90.08% at λ = 3.0 (Extended Data Fig. 8d).

No lifetime–size exponent has been measured for Au/TiO₂, so λ is a model parameter; the literature fixes the loss mechanism. On powder Au/TiO₂ at 273–313 K and atmospheric pressure, activity is lost through carbonate-like species that accumulate at the Au–support perimeter: deactivation is a loss of active sites rather than of per-site activity, particle growth is excluded, and activity is restored by heating or by H₂ in the feed.[59,60] The fractional loss shows no resolved size dependence, which corresponds to λ ≈ 0; the rate anchor of the control is itself the activity after a 10–15 h stabilization at 273 K.[24] Where Ostwald ripening controls life, the classical ripening laws (d³ or d⁴ ∝ t) give λ = 3–4, and the chemical potential of Au on TiO₂(110), which rises with decreasing size more steeply than the Gibbs–Thomson relation, makes sub-3 nm particles the least stable;[61] in that regime the larger particles become the cheaper catalyst. The upstream winner is therefore the plant-level optimum wherever activity loss is reversible and size-independent, and the boundary of that statement is λ = n + m.

**Observed pairwise transfer: definition and evidential scope.** For a defined comparison A versus B in which A has the larger upstream property \(s_A>s_B\), we summarize the *already computed* lower-is-better downstream objective \(J\) by the signed finite-difference slope

\[
\chi_{AB}^{\mathrm{obs}}=\frac{\ln(J_A/J_B)}{\ln(s_A/s_B)}.
\]

The input \(s\) and the downstream objective \(J\) must be specified separately for each physical system. Here \(\chi^{\mathrm{obs}}<0\) means that A's upstream lead accompanies a lower downstream burden; \(\chi^{\mathrm{obs}}>0\) means that A is more costly despite its upstream lead; and \(\chi^{\mathrm{obs}}=0\) means that the two compared downstream objectives are equal. A value of \(-1\) follows *specifically* from an inverse-proportional downstream mapping \(J\propto s^{-1}\), as assumed in the fixed-condition Au/TiO₂ control.

| Comparison (A relative to B) | Upstream metric \(s_A/s_B\) | Downstream burden \(J_A/J_B\) | Realized \(\chi_{AB}^{\mathrm{obs}}\) | Decision under the stated model |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Au/TiO₂, 2 nm versus 6 nm | Mass activity ≈8.1× | Required catalyst mass ≈0.124× | ≈−1.00 | Rank preserved under the stipulated inverse-activity mapping |
| CO₂-to-methanol, the reported high-STY and lower-STY states | Space-time yield ≈3.6× | Modeled total production cost ≈1.019× | ≈+0.015 | Upstream lead is reversed, with a **1.9%** cost penalty |
| NH₃, Ru versus Fe | Intrinsic TOF ≈607× | Modeled **catalyst-dependent** cost ≈1.44× | ≈+0.057 | Upstream lead is reversed, with a **44%** penalty in the stated cost component |

The three systems use different upstream quantities s, and the Au objective is catalyst requirement rather than full plant cost, so χ compares the direction and strength of propagation within each system rather than across reactions. ### Supplementary Note 10 | Ru against Ni in CO₂ methanation

Moioli and Züttel[26] compared 0.5 wt% Ru/Al₂O₃ with Ni/Al₂O₃ for CO₂ methanation in their own reactor model: Ru/Al₂O₃ lights off 10–20 °C below Ni/Al₂O₃ and reaches 99.5% conversion in one stage, Ni/Al₂O₃ needs 1.89 times the catalyst volume and an intermediate water-condensation stage between two reactive stages, and the Ru bed costs 2.64 times the Ni bed on a commercial-catalyst basis and 5.20 times on a metal basis (their Table 6). They recommended Ru for small and Ni for large plants. Every input taken from the source is listed with its location in `analysis/sabatier_ru_ni_2026_10_19/SOURCE_LEDGER.csv`.

Before the model was written, the hypotheses and the criterion were committed (commit 8b9a573, 2026-10-19 07:54 UTC; model and results commit c8b845e, 08:04 UTC): Ru is cheaper when r_V(1 + φ_proc(S)) > Π, where r_V = V_Ni/V_Ru is the inventory advantage of Ru, Π the Ru-to-Ni catalyst price ratio per kilogram (metal loading × metal price × manufacturing basis, net of recovery) and φ_proc(S) the capital of the second Ni stage with its condensation set per unit of annual Ni catalyst cost at plant scale S. The catalyst inventory follows from the design space velocity and the source's volume ratio; replacement uses the metal prices of the ammonia case; reactor-stage capital uses the vessel correlation and financing of the ammonia model; the condensation set is a bracketed multiplier κ = 0.5–3 on a second vessel, since the source does not cost it. Costs are catalyst-dependent US dollars per MWh of SNG (LHV); feed, compression and heat integration are common to both catalysts and are not included.

At the central parameters (precursor cost basis, 5-year life, κ = 1.5) Π = 5.18 and r_V = 1.89, so the inventory term alone favours Ni at every scale (A_Ni/A_Ru = 0.365). φ_proc falls with scale because inventory grows linearly with throughput while vessel capital grows as S^0.52 plus a fixed part: 9.13 at 0.1 MW, 1.74 at the parity scale, 0.17 at 10 MW and 0.038 at 100 MW. Ru costs 19.69 against 42.41 US dollars per MWh at 0.1 MW and 8.47 against 3.24 at 100 MW; parity lies at 0.595 MW (about 75 Nm³ h⁻¹ of CH₄). The full-model parity and the root of the criterion agree at all 382 points of the price–life boundary map. The boundary moves towards Ni with Ru price and shorter catalyst life: at a 5-year life it lies at 0.854, 0.595 and 0.142 MW for 0.1, 1 and 10 times the Ru price, at a 10-year life at 0.30–1.91 MW, and at a 1-year life at 0.10–0.15 MW, with Ni cheaper at every scale above 1.26 times the Ru price. Every deterministic variant (metal-only or commercial-catalyst basis, the source's 2019 Ru price, Ru recovery 90%, life 2 or 10 years, κ 0.5 or 3, r_V 1.5 or 2.5, r_V from isothermal integration of the tabulated rate laws, design space velocity 1,500 or 6,000 h⁻¹, Ru price ×0.1 or ×10) keeps Ru at 0.1 MW and Ni at 100 MW, with the boundary between 0.14 and 37.6 MW; only the source's own 2019 Ru price on the metal-only basis, the lowest Π, moves it beyond 10 MW. Over 10,000 draws of the bracketed parameters (seed 20261019) the parity scale has a median of 0.86 MW (5–95% range 0.18–7.0 MW); Ru is cheaper in 98.3%, 83.2%, 44.0%, 15.7%, 2.9%, 0.54% and 0.05% of draws at 0.1, 0.3, 1, 3, 10, 30 and 100 MW (Extended Data Fig. 9).

Isothermal plug-flow integration at 300 °C, 10 bar and 90% conversion gives V_Ni/V_Ru = 2.77 with the printed value, consistent with the source's non-isothermal 3.11, and 0.28 with the alternative, which contradicts the source's light-off order.
