# Supplementary Information

Process economics reorder catalyst rankings across computed and published catalysts

## Supplementary Notes

The Supplementary Notes give the complete results behind each main-text section, with every number the main text summarises. Figure numbers refer to the main-text figures and Extended Data figures. Supplementary Tables 1–7 follow the notes.

### Supplementary Note 1 | Process and economic evaluation reverse the leading ammonia ranking

The 15-metal ammonia screen ranks Ru, Os and Fe first, second and third in intrinsic activity (Fig. 1c), consistent with the established activity landscape for transition-metal ammonia synthesis.[17,18] After optimizing the operating conditions separately for each catalyst and evaluating catalyst-dependent costs (Fig. 1a,d,e), the order becomes Fe, Ru and Os, with costs of 11.516, 16.633 and 19.747 US dollars per tonne of NH₃, respectively (Fig. 1b,h).

The change is concentrated among the leading candidates. For Ru, Os and Fe, Spearman rho is -0.50 and Kendall tau is -0.33, while the Spearman correlation across all 15 metals remains 0.911 (Fig. 1f,g). The overall screen can therefore remain strongly correlated even when the preferred catalyst changes at the top of the ranking. With three leading candidates these coefficients describe the reordering rather than test it statistically; the decision-relevant quantities are the identity of the winner and the cost of choosing wrongly. Agreement among the leading candidates is thus more relevant to catalyst selection than full-set correlation alone. Agreement can also be expressed in decision terms. If the intrinsic-activity winner Ru were selected instead of the economic winner Fe, the catalyst-dependent cost objective would increase from 11.516 to 16.633 US dollars per tonne of NH₃, corresponding to a normalized decision regret of **44.4%**. The 5.117 US dollars per tonne difference is a small fraction of the cost of ammonia from state-of-the-art gas-based plants, about 160 US dollars per tonne,[31] but it is the part of the cost that catalyst selection controls and amounts to 1.77 million US dollars per year for a 1,000 t/d plant, which at a capacity factor of 0.95 produces 346,750 t of NH₃ per year.

In the ammonia model, gas-phase thermodynamics follow one measured basis throughout: the NH₃ formation free energy is taken at each temperature from the Gillespie–Beattie equilibrium constant,[57] in place of the DFT gas energies of the activity source, which place it 0.064–0.065 eV per NH₃ higher, and N₂, H₂ and NH₃ enter the loop equilibrium and the microkinetic gas activities as fugacities with Newton–Cooper coefficients,[46] so that the reactor and the loop share one equilibrium. The plant calibration of catalyst productivity is fixed by the fused-iron bed at 400 °C, 80 bar and a −35 °C separator. The model passes 32 of 64 preregistered comparisons with industrial ammonia plants, among them 18 of the 28 comparisons with the loop states of 13 industrial loop records.

### Supplementary Note 2 | An automated agent extends the analysis to published and computed catalysts

The hand-built analyses compare three ammonia metals, four methanol states and five Au particle sizes. ACSA scales the same analysis to every candidate that publications or databases provide, while the kinetic, process and economic models remain unchanged (Fig. 2a). It performs three tasks. It reads each paper and its Supporting Information and records every catalyst entry with composition, loading, operating conditions, conversion, selectivities and rates, together with the table, figure and page each value comes from. It takes every candidate through the forward chain, with a cheap lower bound deciding which candidates need the full process optimization. Before scoring any new candidate it must reproduce the frozen numbers of the hand-built cases.

Extraction was scored on 50 methanol papers: 28 that one manually curated dataset covers (547 curated entries),[32] 20 that a second curated dataset covers (194 entries with conditions and space-time yield),[33] the Re/TiO₂ study that supplies the hand-built methanol states[19] and one further study. Extracted and curated entries are paired on paper, catalyst and conditions only, never on the values being scored; 197 of the 719 pairs have an equally admissible alternative partner and count for recall but not for value accuracy. The agent recovers 719 of the 741 curated entries (97%). Values printed in tables are extracted almost without error: 44 of 44 CO₂ conversions and 44 of 44 methanol selectivities in main-text tables, and 129 of 130 conversions, 124 of 129 selectivities and 118 of 128 space-time yields in the Supporting Information (Extended Data Fig. 1). Values read from main-text plots fall within the strict tolerance for 168 of 203 conversions and 65 of 181 selectivities. Of all extracted entries, 85% are correct in the first 18 papers, where the entries without a curated counterpart were checked against the source (91 of the 150 reviewed are correct, 29 are not yet reviewed), and an estimated 93% in the 31 papers added later, where 77 of a random 87 such entries are correct; most of the correct ones are entries the curated sets do not list. The same comparison finds curated values that the papers contradict: 26 root causes, one mechanism in one source paper each, in 45 rules, 169 cells and 8 rows, across the two methanol datasets and the ammonia review, plus 24 space-time-yield cells that one dataset derives from an erroneous input (Supplementary Table 7). All 21 Re/TiO₂ entries come out exactly.

The self-check passes in all three systems. Taking the extracted Re/TiO₂ entries through the methanol model reproduces the frozen costs of all 21 entries to 2.3 × 10⁻¹³ euros per tonne, including the four hand-built states at 943.30, 961.51, 966.96 and 1,258.17 euros per tonne. In ammonia, the batch code path reproduces the canonical cost of every pure metal to 10⁻⁹ US dollars per tonne. For Au/TiO₂, the rate anchor and the particle-size exponent extracted from the two source papers[24,25] reproduce the required catalyst masses of the 2–6 nm particles, order preservation in all 10,000 literature-envelope samples and the six operating-window stress tests.

Cheap calculations decide where the expensive ones are spent. For each ammonia candidate a lower bound on cost, built from the best-state activity at its descriptor, the smallest reactor and the cheapest process state, requires no process optimization. Across the 1,695 priced bimetallic surfaces this bound excludes 1,399 candidates (82.5%) from the full 14,510-state optimization. Optimizing all 1,695 in full confirms that no candidate below the Fe cost is excluded. In methanol, the plant economics evaluated on lower-bound loop flows at each purge level, valid for any per-pass conversion of recycled CO, replace the equilibrium-constrained purge optimization; taking each comparison group in order of this bound, with the paper's leader always evaluated, needs the full optimization for 111 of the 310 candidates (199 excluded, 64.2%) and finds the plant-cost leader of all 40 groups, at 36% of the compute of evaluating every candidate. Extraction takes about 0.15 million language-model tokens per paper across the main-text, figure and Supporting-Information passes.

### Supplementary Note 3 | Published methanol leaderboards and plant-cost leaderboards often disagree

Methanol papers rank catalysts by laboratory performance, most often by space-time yield. ACSA extracted 351 operating points with printed conversion, selectivity, temperature, pressure and H₂/CO₂ ratio from 33 studies. Within each paper, entries tested at the same pressure, H₂/CO₂ ratio and space velocity form the comparison the paper itself makes, giving 40 comparisons with 310 entries in 28 papers. With plot-read values the same rule gives 83 comparisons in 44 papers; 20 of them come from 11 papers without printed values and 23 have fewer than two printed entries (Supplementary Table 6). Each entry was taken through the methanol recycle–economics model at its own conditions, with recycled CO converted no further than reverse-water-gas-shift and CO-hydrogenation equilibrium, single-pass conversion capped at the loop's CO₂-hydrogenation equilibrium and the purge optimized for each entry.

The primary loop limits the reactor-inlet content of species other than H₂ and CO₂ (CO, methane and N₂) to 6.86%, the value of the loop that anchors the cost model. The model holds each entry's single-pass conversion at its laboratory value, so a loop without such a limit lets a diluted feed react as in the laboratory. The published loop designs carry 4.4–9.8% CO, methane and N₂ at the reactor inlet: 6.46% in the anchor loop (1.52% CO, 4.95% N₂; 6.78% with methanol and water; the cost model's limit is 6.86%), 9.85% in its three-reactor variant, and 4.74% and 4.4% in two further designs (4.9–10.2% of all species other than H₂ and CO₂). CO counts toward the limit although recycled CO still reacts up to the reverse-water-gas-shift and CO-hydrogenation bound: at the cost-optimal purge it is converted little further and accumulates. Without the limit the reactor feed carries a median 7.1% CO (10th–90th percentile 0.5–22%), against 1.3–4.7% in the published designs; at the limited optimum CO makes up more than 80% of the limited quantity and the median purge rises from 0.5% to 2.6%.

In the inert-limited loop the space-time-yield leader is not the plant-cost leader in 28 of 40 comparisons (70%; 95% confidence interval 54–83% from resampling papers), 22 of them with a cost gap above 5%, spread over 21 of the 28 papers (18 with a gap above 5%), and 707 of 1,997 pairwise orderings invert (Fig. 2b). Without the limit the leaders differ in 15 of 40 (38%; 15–57%), 7 above 5%, in 9 papers, and 304 of 1,997 orderings invert. The regret exceeds 1%, 5% and 10% in 25, 22 and 20 of the 28 disagreements (12, 7 and 6 of the 15 without the limit). Of the 32 comparisons that contain at least two catalysts, 16 have another catalyst as plant-cost leader (11 above 5%; without the limit 10, 4 above 5%), 12 of them at the paper leader's temperature (8 above 5%, from 8 papers; none above 5% without the limit); of the 22 that contain a temperature series, 12 have the same catalyst at another temperature as plant-cost leader (11 above 5%; without the limit 5, 3 above 5%). Ranking by methanol selectivity disagrees in 17 of 40 comparisons with a median regret of 51% (31 of 40 and 20% without the limit), and ranking by conversion in 27 of 40 (12 without the limit).

The inlet limit matters as a range, not only as one value (Extended Data Fig. 8). At limits of 4, 5, 6, 6.86, 8, 10, 12, 15, 20, 25 and 30% the leaders differ in 28, 26, 27, 28, 27, 24, 24, 18, 19, 17 and 16 of 40 comparisons, with 25, 25, 24, 22, 22, 18, 13, 12, 10, 9 and 8 above 5%; in the paper-resampling interval the excess over the unlimited loop stays above zero up to 12% for all disagreements and up to 10% for those above 5%, and vanishes at 15%. With plot-read entries the inert-limited loop scores 82 of the 83 groups, because one group keeps fewer than two entries that can meet the limit. Limiting methane and N₂ alone, with CO unlimited, gives 15–19 of 40 at every limit (18 at 6.86%, 8 above 5%), close to the unlimited loop, so the effect of the limit comes from CO build-up. Treating recycled CO as inert gives 21 of 40 without the limit (13 above 5%) and 28 with it (24 above 5%). Converting recycled CO per pass up to CO-hydrogenation equilibrium, the upper end of its window, gives 14 of 40 without the limit (6 above 5%) and 27 with it (19 above 5%, 19 papers); the median inlet CO at the limited optimum then falls to 0.9%, but the paper leaders still carry more CO than the plant-cost leaders, because they make more of it and CO-hydrogenation equilibrium caps its conversion.

The disagreement is neither a sampling nor a measurement artefact. Re-measuring every entry 2,000 times with the conversion and selectivity uncertainty derived for the Re/TiO₂ states keeps 24 of the 28 disagreements in at least 90% of re-measurements (11 of 15 without the limit), whereas measurement noise alone changes a paper's space-time-yield leader in 4 comparisons (95% range 1–8); at twice the noise 20 of 28 stay. In 11 of the 28 disagreements the paper leader meets the limit only with a large purge and costs more than twice its cost without the limit (near-infeasible); the other 17 (12 above 5%, 13 of them robust to re-measurement) involve no such jump. Counts rather than the size of the largest regrets are therefore reported. The disagreement holds without the study that supplies most comparisons (21 of 32, 17 above 5%; 8 of 32 without the limit), when entries with plot-read values are added (54 of 82 comparisons; 35 of 83 without the limit), for entries whose reported products close to methanol, CO and methane (17 of 28), with unreported CO and methane selectivities set to either bound (26–29 of 40), and when each catalyst is priced from its composition and its replacement charged per tonne of inventory over three years (25 of 40, 22 above 5%). Allowing each catalyst to load more catalyst and raise its conversion towards equilibrium gives 23 of 40, 16 above 5% from 12 papers, with another catalyst at the same temperature above 5% in 5 comparisons from 4 papers; without the limit the same allowance gives 14 of 40, 3 above 5%.

Without the limit the purge optimum lies at the 0.5% floor of the purge grid for every entry; extending the grid to 10⁻⁴% moves it below 0.5% for 309 of 310 entries (median 0.048%), where the reactor inlet carries a median 40% of species other than H₂ and CO₂ at a recycle ratio of 44, against 2.85 in the anchor loop, and the disagreement is 14 of 40, 2 above 5%.

The plant ranking follows from what each by-product does in a recycle loop (Fig. 2d,e). Under the limit the plant-cost leader is more selective to methanol than the paper leader in 19 of the 28 disagreements, makes less CO in 18 and runs at higher single-pass conversion in 7; without the limit it runs at higher conversion in 9 of 15. A high-pressure Cu/Al₂O₃ study[35] supplies 8 of the 40 comparisons and the largest regrets in both loops; without it the leaders differ in 21 of 32 comparisons with the limit and in 8 of 32 without it.

### Supplementary Note 4 | Measured ammonia catalysts rank differently by laboratory rate and by plant cost

ACSA also takes measured catalysts through the ammonia chain. It extracted the 164 catalyst rows tabulated in a review of ammonia-synthesis catalysts;[36] two independent extraction passes agree on 697 of 706 numeric fields, and every value was checked against the printed tables. Checking the rows against the primary papers (below) found ten transcription errors in nine rows, among them a space velocity printed ten times too low and two Ru-free catalysts listed as Ru catalysts; the chain uses the papers' values. Each catalyst's measured rate per gram of metal, at its laboratory temperature, pressure and NH₃ fraction, is inverted into an effective descriptor on its metal's side of the volcano, and the catalyst is then optimized over the full process library with its own metal content. The plant calibration reproduces the laboratory rates of the two fused-iron catalysts in the tables within a factor of 1.9 (α = 0.77 and 0.54).

Of the 73 catalysts in the primary set (54 Ru, 9 Fe, 5 Co and 5 Ni), the one with the highest laboratory rate, Ru/AC-G, costs 13.76 US dollars per tonne, 25% above the plant-cost leader, and laboratory rate and plant cost are uncorrelated (Spearman ρ = 0.07). In both studies that test Fe and Ru on the same support, Ru has the higher rate and Fe the lower plant cost (17.8% and 12.9% regret). Two measured catalysts undercut the fused-iron benchmark without metal recovery, a Cs- and Ba-promoted Ru catalyst (Ru/Cs/Ba/CCHT, 11.01 US dollars per tonne) and a BaH₂-promoted Fe catalyst (20% Fe–BaH₂, 11.19), by 4% and 3%; with 90% Ru recovery four Ru catalysts do, the route identified in Fig. 3d.

A review tabulates catalysts measured in different laboratories, whereas a paper's own comparison is between catalysts tested side by side. ACSA therefore extracted 30 primary ammonia-synthesis papers, 28 of them with their Supporting Information. For the 37 entries that the review also tabulates (one review row is printed twice), every extracted temperature, pressure, metal content and space velocity agrees with the paper, space velocity is extracted for 29 of the 34 entries that state one, and the rate in 36 of 37 is correct; a random check of 40 further entries against the papers finds 35 correct. Within each paper, catalysts with printed rate, temperature and outlet that were tested at the same temperature, pressure, H₂/N₂ ratio and space velocity form 33 comparisons in 23 papers. The catalyst with the highest rate per gram of catalyst is not the plant-cost leader in 7 of them (21%; 95% confidence interval 7–38% from resampling papers), in 6 of 23 papers, with a median regret of 14% where the leaders differ. Two situations account for most disagreements: in 3 comparisons a catalyst of another metal is the plant-cost leader, in 3 of them an Fe catalyst on the same support with the lower rate, and in 2 a commercial fused-iron catalyst that the paper includes as a reference is the plant-cost leader. Three printed entries in this set undercut the fused-iron benchmark: the commercial fused-iron reference of one paper (9.84 US dollars per tonne) and a 20 wt% Co@BaO/MgO catalyst at 300 °C and 1 MPa (11.19) and at 400 °C and 6.1 MPa (11.23). With 90% Ru recovery the disagreement is 5 of 33; ranking by rate per gram of metal raises it to 9. With plot-read entries included the comparisons number 124 and the disagreement 40.

A second batch of papers was screened, extracted with Supporting Information and scored under a protocol committed before extraction: of 25 screened papers, 22 were included, and 8 of them supply 11 comparisons with printed values. In 5 of the 11 the laboratory leader is not the plant-cost leader (45%; 95% confidence interval 12.5–76.9%), in 4 of the 8 papers, with a median regret of 12% where the leaders differ; in 3 of the 5 a fused-iron reference catalyst is the plant-cost leader. Across both batches the leaders differ in 12 of 44 comparisons (27%; 95% confidence interval 14–42%), in 10 of 31 papers, and 8 of the 12 have a regret above 5%. Without the commercial fused-iron references the disagreement is 6 of 33 in the first batch and 8 of 44 across both; ranking by rate per gram of metal gives 15 of 44, 90% Ru recovery 10 of 44, and with plot-read entries included the leaders differ in 50 of 177 comparisons. In 4 of the 44 comparisons the plant-cost leader undercuts the fused-iron benchmark: the three commercial fused-iron references (9.83–9.84 US dollars per tonne) and the Co@BaO/MgO catalyst at 400 °C and 6.1 MPa (11.23).

### Supplementary Note 5 | Outside nitride formers and sp metals, surfaces that undercut Fe pair a cheap 3d metal with a group-6 metal

The 15-metal screen contains only three economically feasible candidates. To test whether the Fe preference survives a much larger candidate space, ACSA took the 1,796 bimetallic and pure-metal surfaces of a high-throughput adsorption database[37,38] through the ammonia chain. The most stable N adsorption energy of each surface is placed on the model's step-site descriptor by the terrace-to-step relation that the workbook already uses for Fe, after a linear bridge between the two datasets over the 14 pure metals they share. A second, element-anchored bridge places each of the 14 metals with a frozen descriptor (Ag, Au, Co, Cu, Fe, Ir, Mo, Os, Pd, Pt, Re, Rh, Ru, W) exactly at that descriptor. Metal prices are the frozen model prices for the 15 model metals and USGS annual averages for 21 further elements (2025; Hg 2024); Tc has no market price.[63]

Among the 372 surfaces that contain only transition metals outside groups 3–5, 137 satisfy the 90 m³ bed-volume limit. Eight undercut Fe under the linear bridge: Cu₃Cr (10.14 US dollars per tonne), Fe₃Mo, Ni₃W, Fe₃W, Cu₃Mo, Co₃Mo, Co₃W and Ni₃Mo. Nine do so under the element-anchored bridge: Cu₃Cr, Cu₃Mo, CuMo, MoNi, CoMo, CoW, Fe₃Mo, Ni₃W and Fe₃W (Extended Data Fig. 6). Every one of them pairs Fe, Co, Ni or Cu with Cr, Mo or W, the combination that descriptor interpolation has identified for ammonia synthesis.[39] The individual alloys depend on the bridge; the family does not. The most active surface ranks 12th economically (Ni₃Re, 28.0% regret) under the linear bridge and 45th (Rh₃Re, 86.9%) under the element-anchored bridge, with Spearman correlations of 0.71 and 0.67 between activity and cost. As in the 15-metal screen, the activity leader is not the economic leader. Counterfactual and backward calculations, run only on these leading surfaces, show the same mechanism as for Ru. At the Fe price both activity leaders would undercut Fe (10.15 and 10.33 US dollars per tonne), whereas at their own prices they need 5.5-fold and 421-fold activity increases to reach Fe parity, and no descriptor on the strict scaling range brings them below 12.95 and 18.84 US dollars per tonne. Cu₃Cr, Fe₃Mo, Ni₃W, Fe₃W and Cu₃Mo remain below Fe under both bridges, and Cu₃Cr keeps its lead with 13% of its activity. Surfaces containing group 3–5 elements, which form stable bulk nitrides, and surfaces containing sp metals, which lie outside the transition-metal scaling relations of the activity model, are reported as separate layers (Supplementary Table 3; Extended Data Fig. 6).

### Supplementary Note 6 | Metal price and process optimization jointly determine the Fe-Ru ranking

The leading metals favor different operating conditions. Fe has an interior optimum near 425 °C and 105 bar, whereas Ru favors approximately 450 °C and 220 bar, both with a 30 °C separator; Os has a broad, shallow minimum at high pressure (Fig. 3b,c). The economic comparison therefore reflects both catalyst properties and the operating conditions favored by each catalyst.

To isolate the effect of metal price, we repeated the Ru optimization after setting its metal price equal to the Fe value of 8 US dollars per kilogram. All other model inputs, constraints and costing assumptions were unchanged. The Ru optimum shifts from 450 °C and 220 bar to 400 °C and 95 bar, and its optimized cost falls to 10.237 US dollars per tonne of NH₃, 1.279 US dollars per tonne below Fe (Fig. 3b–e). Thus removing the Ru-Fe price difference reverses the economic order after process reoptimization.

A full Ru-price sweep, with process reoptimization at every price, places Fe-Ru parity at 403.54 US dollars per kilogram. At this price, Ru operates at 425 °C and 110 bar with a 3.82 m³ catalyst bed (Fig. 3d). Across the sweep, the optimum moves from 95 bar and an 18.2 m³ bed at 1 US dollar per kilogram to 340 bar and a 0.011 m³ bed at 3 × 10^5 US dollars per kilogram. Metal price therefore affects the final ranking directly through catalyst cost and indirectly by changing the preferred process conditions.

The model gives every metal the formulation of the industrial fused-iron catalyst, so the same curve can be read at the cost of a real Ru catalyst (Fig. 3d). A promoted Ru/C ammonia catalyst exposes 11% of its Ru atoms,[40] against fewer than 1% of the Fe atoms in reduced fused iron;[41] the commercial catalyst carries about 8 wt% Ru,[42] and 90–95.55% of the Ru in spent promoted Ru/C ammonia catalysts is recovered (93.05–95.55%[43] and 91.1–95.4%[44] in the examples with Ba, K and Mg promoters). Dispersion and recovery divide the metal charge in the same way as the metal price, so the catalyst enters at an effective price p(1 − r)/u: 218–490 US dollars per kilogram for 90–95.55% recovery at a dispersion gain u = 11 (exposed Ru 11%, against fewer than 1% of the Fe atoms in fused iron). At that price Ru costs 11.15–11.69 US dollars per tonne of NH₃, from 0.37 below to 0.17 above Fe, at 425 °C and 110 bar, and the activity multiple required for parity falls from 115-fold to 0.6–1.2-fold. In a KAAP-type loop at about 9 MPa with a −20 °C refrigerated condenser,[45] this reading gives 16.27–16.39 US dollars per tonne against 16.98 for fused iron in that loop. With the supported catalyst's own bed (8 wt% Ru at 430–550 kg m⁻³), Ru costs 11.46–12.05 in the main loop, from 0.05 below to 0.53 above Fe (parity at an activity multiple of 0.94–1.66), and 16.35–16.44 in the KAAP-type loop, below fused iron at both recoveries in every bed (parity at 0.067–0.121) and even without recovery (16.90). Across 5–10 wt% Ru the main-loop cost spans 11.38–12.20 and the KAAP-loop cost 16.33–16.46. Metal recovery is an optional Ru/C scenario, not a requirement of the baseline 15-metal screening. These readings charge the Ru inventory, as the benchmark model does, only for the metal lost at each replacement. Pricing every purchase and recovered-metal credit at its date over the 25-year plant life, with recovered Ru credited at replacement and at closure, gives the same result when nothing is discounted (−0.61 US dollars per tonne against Fe in the KAAP-type loop at 8 wt% Ru, 490 kg m⁻³ and 95.55% recovery). Financing the inventory at the 12% rate applied to equipment puts Ru at 16.99 against 17.00 for Fe in that loop, and Ru stays cheaper at any metal financing rate below 12.33% per year (a lease rent below 12.31% per year for a supplier-owned lease without deposit). The only disclosed industrial rates are those of 32 fixed-rate platinum-group catalyst-metal leases of one US refiner (2013–2022, 1.35–5.10% per year, median 2.10%; 11 of the 32 do not name the metal); no Ru lease rate is published, so applying them to Ru is an analogy. At these rates Ru is 0.26–0.48 US dollars per tonne below Fe when owned and 0.27–0.51 when leased. Metal economy through dispersion and recovery therefore brings Ru to the Fe decision boundary in the high-pressure loop and across it in the low-pressure loop of the Kellogg Advanced Ammonia Process.

At 90 bar, 400 °C and 2% NH₃ the descriptor kinetics give Ru apparent orders of +2.4 in H₂ and −1.9 in NH₃ and fused iron −0.1 in NH₃. For the two frontier catalysts the rates were therefore replaced by measured rate laws: the Temkin–Pyzhev form with the Dyson–Simon parameters for an industrial magnetite catalyst[46,47] (apparent orders N₂ +1.00, H₂ +1.51, NH₃ −1.01) and the Ru/C rate equation of Rossetti et al.[40] (N₂ +0.53, H₂ +0.32, NH₃ −0.39); the Ru/C law lowers the hydrogen order but does not make it negative. The Ru/C law reproduces the 125 measured points of its source with a mean error of 10.1%. Integrated in plug flow and each calibrated on its own measured rate, the pair gives Fe 14.93 US dollars per tonne at 450 °C, 140 bar and a 30 °C separator and Ru in the benchmark formulation 33.07 at a broad optimum around 735 bar, within 0.64 of it from 400 to 890 bar (38.26 at best between 100 and 200 bar); the activity multiple for parity is 13.95 (13.95–25.07 across inlet-rate or plug-flow sizing and own-rate or fused-iron calibration), against 114.67 with the descriptor kinetics. Supported Ru/C (8 wt% Ru, 490 kg m⁻³, 95.55% recovery) has its optimum at 105 bar and costs 3.46 US dollars per tonne less than Fe without metal financing and 1.45 more at 12% per year (KAAP-type loop: 1.41 less and 1.73 more). It stays cheaper than Fe up to a metal financing rate of 7.23% per year, and in the KAAP-type loop up to 5.37% per year (6.75% and 4.82% at 90% recovery). Across 16 cases that vary the Fe catalyst price, the Ru/C bed density, recovery, the Ru price, catalyst life, the effectiveness factor of either catalyst, the converter pressure premium and the bed sizing, Ru/C is cheaper than Fe without metal financing in all 16 in both loops, and the break-even financing rate spans 1.33–17.67% per year in the main loop and 0.37–9.89% per year in the KAAP-type loop. At 12% per year Fe is cheaper in 14 of the 16 cases in the main loop, all but a halved Fe effectiveness and a doubled converter premium, and in 15 of 16 in the KAAP-type loop, where a halved Fe effectiveness leaves no Fe bed within the 90 m³ limit.

With both catalysts available, a three-bed adiabatic converter with interbed cooling at 9 MPa (fused iron up to 530 °C, Ru/C up to 460 °C, the top of its measured range; 8% inerts; −20 °C separator) is cheapest as all-Ru/C for Ru financing rates of 0–0.82% per year, as fused iron upstream with Ru/C downstream for 0.82–6.65% per year, the bed order of the Kellogg Advanced Ammonia Process converter[45] and of published Fe + Ru multibed designs,[48] and as all-Fe above 6.65% per year; the Ru/C share of the catalyst volume falls from 54% to about 4% across the window, and a converter with Ru/C first is optimal in no case tested. The Ru/C rate advantage over fused iron per kilogram grows downstream, from 16-fold at 2% to 52-fold at 14% NH₃ (90 bar, 400 °C), because ammonia inhibits fused iron more strongly; fused iron takes the first, hottest bed because Ru/C is held to 460 °C. The window moves with recovery (0.13–6.14% per year at 90%) and with Ru price (1.42–9.98% at −30%, 0.50–4.92% at +30%). The disclosed lease rates fall inside the base window, where staging saves 0.78, 0.59 and 0.10 US dollars per tonne over an all-Fe converter at 1.35, 2.10 and 5.10% per year; in the 150 and 200 bar loops with a −5 °C separator the staged converter is optimal up to 4.66% and 3.20% per year and all-Ru/C never wins. These margins are small next to the kinetic uncertainty: the fused-iron law was fitted at 149–309 atm and the Ru/C law at 50–100 bar, so no loop has both inside their fitted pressure ranges.

The baseline Ru-Fe cost difference of 5.117 US dollars per tonne is distributed across several cost components. Fresh-feed compression contributes +3.988 US dollars per tonne, metal inventory +1.694 and compressor capital +1.024, while vessel pressure, recycle compression and reactor-base terms partly offset these contributions; neither optimum uses refrigeration (Fig. 3a,e). This decomposition identifies the main contributions to the baseline difference, whereas the equal-price calculation shows how the optimized process changes when the metal-price difference is removed.

A layer-wise check localizes the reversal more precisely. At the common 673 K atomistic reference, converting turnover frequency into the plant-throughput active-metal requirement preserves the leading order: Ru requires less active metal than Os, and both require far less than Fe. Multiplying those same catalyst demands by the canonical metal prices and the frozen 10-year lifetime changes the order to Fe < Ru < Os before any reactor, compression, refrigeration or equipment cost is added: the annualized metal replacement is 0.204 US dollars per tonne of NH₃ for Fe, 4.09 for Ru and 22.28 for Os. As an independent causal check, setting the Ru metal price equal to the Fe price and reoptimizing the complete 14,510-state process leaves Ru less costly than Fe (10.237 versus 11.516 US dollars per tonne of NH₃). The inversion is therefore triggered at the catalyst-demand-to-lifecycle-economics interface. Candidate-specific process optimization does not close it: Ru moves to a small, high-pressure bed, the Ru − Fe difference goes from 3.89 US dollars per tonne in metal replacement at the common reference to 5.117 in total, and most of it moves into fresh-feed compression, metal inventory and compressor capital.

Descriptor uncertainty affects feasibility and ranking differently. Across 1,000 descriptor-uncertainty samples, Fe satisfies the 90 m³ bed-volume limit in 79.2% of samples, ranks first economically in 65.3% (653/1000), and remains within the top three in 92.3%. The atomistic winner remains the economic winner in 27.1% of samples (Fig. 3f,g). A supporting error-dependence analysis keeps the same marginal descriptor uncertainties but couples the Ru, Os and Fe errors through a Gaussian copula. The independent case gives atomistic-winner frequencies of 45.0% for Ru, 45.5% for Os and 9.5% for Fe, against 47.4%, 43.5% and 9.1% in the 1,000 frozen draws, whereas increasing positive correlation changes the top-one probabilities rather than simply cancelling uncertainty; at latent pairwise correlation 0.9, Fe is the atomistic winner in 16.9% of draws compared with 9.5% in the matched independent audit. A separate common-offset test shows why: because all three descriptors move across a nonlinear volcano, a shared energetic bias can itself change their ordering. These tests are reported as uncertainty-model sensitivities and are not used to replace the preregistered downstream probabilities.

Economic uncertainty around the baseline case does not overturn the Fe preference. Across 5,000 preregistered samples that vary metal price, the CAPEX coefficient, electricity price and catalyst lifetime, full process reoptimization gives P(C_Fe < C_Ru) = 1.000, with a minimum sampled Ru-Fe cost difference of 1.995 US dollars per tonne (Fig. 3h). Expressed as normalized selection regret, R = (C_Ru − C_Fe)/C_Fe, the 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles are **31.52%, 42.21% and 56.99%**, respectively, and R remains positive in all 5,000 draws. The Ru activity multiplier required for economic parity is 43.52-fold, 104.97-fold and 262.28-fold at the 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles, respectively (Fig. 4a). The equal-price calculation therefore identifies a condition under which the ranking reverses, whereas the uncertainty analysis establishes both the sign and the practical cost consequence of the Fe preference over the economic range examined around the baseline case.

These draws charge Ru as the benchmark bed of pure metal. Sampling the actual Ru catalyst turns the decision into a probability and shows what sets it. With the Ru dispersion ratio u (11–50) and Ru recovery r (90–95.55%) added to the same 5,000 draws, Ru is cheaper in 90.8% of draws when it is read at the effective price p(1 - r)/u in the benchmark bed, as in Fig. 3d, and in 79.3% when a commercial Ru/C bed (5–10 wt% Ru, 430–550 kg m⁻³) sets the reactor volume. In that bed the dispersion ratio decides the outcome: Ru wins in 49% of draws with u in its lowest tercile, 99% with u in its top tercile and 100% with u and r both in their top terciles; recovery moves it from 71% to 88% between its lowest and top terciles, and the Ru content from 76% below 7.5 wt% to 83% above. Drawing the Ru activity and Ru content from the 54 measured Ru catalysts instead, Fe is cheaper in 93.0% of draws with recovery and 98.1% without, and the Ru wins come from five of the 54 catalysts (Ru on Ca(NH₂)₂, Ba–Ca(NH₂)₂ and BaO–CaH₂, Ba–Cs-promoted Ru/CCHT and Ru/AC-G).

### Supplementary Note 7 | Backward design separates the required catalyst-property region from physical reachability

The forward analysis identifies the preferred catalyst but does not show how much Ru would need to improve to reach parity with Fe. We therefore multiplied Ru intrinsic activity by a factor α and reoptimized the full process at each value. Under the baseline economic assumptions, Ru reaches the Fe cost only after a 114.67-fold activity increase (Fig. 4a).

The accessible range defined by the scaling relation is much smaller. At 673 K, the largest activity increase along the scaling relation is 1.023-fold, and the largest increase found across all allowed process conditions is 1.575-fold. Under strict scaling, the lowest Ru cost is 16.455 US dollars per tonne of NH₃ at *E*_N = −1.180 eV, which remains above the Fe cost (Fig. 4b). The 114.67-fold requirement therefore lies far outside the range accessible by moving Ru along the present scaling relation. Even the 5th-percentile requirement under economic uncertainty, 43.52-fold, remains well above the 1.575-fold maximum (Fig. 4a).

Within the model and scaling relation used here, improving Ru activity alone cannot make Ru economically competitive with Fe over the economic range considered.

Measured catalysts show which modifications supply gains of the required size (Fig. 4a, bottom). Against unpromoted Ru on the same support, Ba and Cs promotion of Ru/C raise the turnover frequency per surface Ru 75- and 65-fold,[49] Ba promotion of Ru/BN raises the rate more than 100-fold at 5 MPa,[50] and Cs promotion of Ru/MgO more than 134-fold at 350 °C (more than 30-fold at 300 °C, a lower bound);[51] all four lie within the 43.52–262-fold parity band, and the 134-fold Cs–Ru/MgO gain exceeds the 114.67-fold central requirement. Electride and oxyhydride supports give gains of about tenfold (9.8-fold for Ru/C12A7:e⁻ and 8.4-fold for Ru/BaTiO₂.₅H₀.₅ at 5 MPa),[52,53] a Ru–Ba core–shell on calcium amide at least 33.5-fold against Cs–Ru/MgO,[64] and confinement inside carbon nanotubes lowers activity to 0.5-fold.[54] Electronic promotion, which changes the energetics beyond a single scaling relation, is the measured route that reaches the parity band. Most of these gains were measured at 0.1–1 MPa; the Ba–Ru/BN and oxyhydride results show the effect at 5 MPa.

The same parity calculation can be written as a two-property lifecycle target at fixed Ru intrinsic activity. The fully reoptimized Ru price sweep gives an effective Ru parity price of 403.54 US dollars per kilogram. Because metal recovery and catalyst lifetime enter the Ru replacement-cost term multiplicatively with metal price, the corresponding boundary is \(P_{Ru}(1-r)(10\,\mathrm{y}/L) \le 403.54\). At the baseline Ru price of 53,852.5 US dollars per kilogram, this requires approximately 99.25% recovery for a 10-year lifetime, 98.50% for a 20-year lifetime, or a 13.3-year lifetime at 99% recovery. The prespecified 5–20-year lifetime range and 99% optimistic recovery bound therefore reach parity at the current Ru activity for lifetimes above 13.3 years.

Allowing the backward variables to move together changes the required target before physical reachability is tested. We conservatively evaluated the 42 distinct process states already visited by the fully reoptimized Ru price sweep, rescaling catalyst demand by a direct Ru activity multiplier and recomputing the volume- and pressure-dependent cost terms at each state. Because this search uses only a subset of the 14,510-state library, the resulting activity requirements are conservative upper bounds. With 99% Ru recovery, a 10-year catalyst life requires at most a 1.291-fold activity increase, whereas 15- and 20-year lives require none (direct activity multipliers of at most 0.904 and 0.710); a 20-year lifetime with 98% recovery requires at most 1.291-fold.

We then tested this target region directly on the strict *E*_N scaling manifold across all 14,510 process states. The canonical strict-scaling minimum, 16.455 US dollars per tonne at *E*_N = −1.180 eV, is reproduced before lifecycle changes are introduced. The joint parity boundary is controlled by the catalyst-loss factor \(q=(1-r)/L\), with \(q^*=8.2510\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{y}^{-1}\). The prespecified range of catalyst lifetimes up to 20 years and Ru recovery up to 99% crosses this boundary: at 20 years and 99% recovery the best strict-scaling state occurs at *E*_N = −1.190 eV and costs 11.199 US dollars per tonne, 0.317 US dollars per tonne below Fe, and at 15 years and 99% recovery it costs 11.361, 0.154 below Fe. Parity requires 98.350% recovery at a 20-year lifetime or, equivalently, a 12.12-year lifetime at 99% recovery. Lifecycle engineering thus reaches parity inside the present scaling-constrained design space, where activity alone cannot.

### Supplementary Note 8 | Selectivity, recycle and purge reshape the methanol ranking

The same ranking change appears in CO₂ hydrogenation to methanol through a different process pathway. Four literature-derived Re/TiO₂ catalyst-temperature states were compared (Fig. 5a,b).[19] Using space-time yield per gram of Re as the catalyst metric, the order is 1 wt% Re at 250 °C, 1 wt% Re at 200 °C, 5 wt% Re at 200 °C and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C. After propagation through the recycle and separation model at the 2% purge used in the reference case, the economic order becomes 5 wt% Re at 200 °C, 1 wt% Re at 250 °C, 1 wt% Re at 200 °C and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C, with net production costs of 943, 962, 967 and 1,258 euros per tonne, respectively (Fig. 5c).

The state with the highest space-time yield per gram of Re falls from first to second economically, and the state ranked third by space-time yield is the economic optimum. Selecting the upstream winner would give 961.51 euros per tonne instead of the 943.30 euros per tonne economic optimum, a normalized decision regret of **1.93%**. Across the four states, Spearman rho is 0.40, Kendall tau is 0.33 and two of six pairwise comparisons invert (Fig. 5e). Methane formation consumes reactants and increases gas accumulation, purge, recycle and compression requirements. A local sensitivity analysis at the 5 wt% Re, 250 °C state gives economic leverage values of 0.37579 for methane suppression, 0.05883 for single-pass conversion and 0.00289 for space-time yield (Fig. 5a,g). These sensitivities are consistent with previous CO₂-to-methanol process studies in which recycle, compression and separation make substantial contributions to plant performance.[20,21]

The reordering persists across the full purge range considered. Over 396 purge levels from 0.5% to 40%, the state with the highest space-time yield per gram of Re is never the least-cost option; Spearman rho never exceeds 0.80 and at least one of six pairwise comparisons remains inverted. Optimizing purge separately for each catalyst state changes the detailed order but does not restore the space-time-yield ranking (Fig. 5f).

Counterfactual and backward calculations locate the cause. Moving every state's methane selectivity into methanol reverses the correlation to rho = −0.60 and makes the highest-conversion state the least costly, whereas equalizing conversion raises rho to +1.00. For the space-time-yield winner, no increase in space-time yield reaches parity: unlimited productivity still costs 954.64 euros per tonne against 943.30. Raising its single-pass conversion from 0.23 to 0.279, inside the 0.19–0.40 range measured on the same catalyst family, or reaching complete methanol selectivity does. As in ammonia, the catalyst property that ranks the candidates upstream is not the one that can move the industrial decision.

Propagating each state's own measurement uncertainty separates the robust positions from the unresolved one (Fig. 5d). In 5,000 draws in which the CO₂ conversion and selectivities of every state are sampled independently and the full plant model is re-solved, the economic winner keeps first place in 4,559 draws (91.2%) and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C remains last in all 5,000. The second and third places, 5.5 euros per tonne apart, exchange in 1,054 draws (21.1%). The space-time-yield winner becomes the economic optimum in 413 draws (8.3%), and the complete space-time-yield ranking is recovered in 24 draws (0.5%).

### Supplementary Note 9 | Catalyst effects on process operation determine whether rankings change

The ammonia and methanol results identify different routes by which catalyst properties affect process cost (Fig. 6a). In ammonia, intrinsic activity and metal price determine catalyst inventory and the preferred operating conditions, which in turn change compression, reactor and equipment costs. In methanol, selectivity changes reactant losses, gas accumulation, purge, recycle and compression. Absolute costs are not compared across the two reactions; the comparison concerns how catalyst properties enter each process model.

Au/TiO₂ CO oxidation provides a control in which the relation between particle size and catalyst requirement is monotonic. Low-temperature CO oxidation on supported Au is well known to depend on particle size and support effects.[22,23] The present calculation uses the absolute-rate anchor reported by Janssens et al.[24] and the particle-size dependence measured by Overbury et al.[25] at the closest Au loading. Candidate states differ only in Au particle size from 2 to 6 nm under the same operating condition and process model.

Under this common mapping, intrinsic activity and required catalyst mass preserve the same order, 2 > 3 > 4 > 5 > 6 nm. Spearman rho and Kendall tau are both 1.000, no pairwise comparison inverts, and all 10,000 parameter samples drawn from ranges reported in the literature preserve the complete order (Fig. 6b–d), as required of a control in which catalyst burden is the inverse of activity. The upstream winner is therefore also the minimum-burden downstream choice, giving zero selection regret under this control mapping. When moderate candidate-specific kinetic and operating variations are introduced, the exact order is retained in 92.16% of 10,000 samples over 273.15–293.15 K, with mean rho = 0.99214. Extending the temperature range to 313.15 K reduces exact preservation to 72.62% while retaining mean rho = 0.96802 (Fig. 6e). Process and economic evaluation therefore do not necessarily change a catalyst ranking; changes arise when catalyst properties affect downstream operating requirements strongly enough to alter relative costs (Fig. 6f).
