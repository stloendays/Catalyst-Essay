# Supplementary Information

Process economics reorder catalyst rankings across computed and published catalysts

## Supplementary Notes

The Supplementary Notes give the complete results behind each main-text section, with every number the main text summarises. Figure numbers refer to the main-text figures and Extended Data figures. Supplementary Tables 1–6 follow the notes.

### Supplementary Note 1 | Process and economic evaluation reverse the leading ammonia ranking

The 15-metal ammonia screen ranks Ru, Os and Fe first, second and third in intrinsic activity (Fig. 1c), consistent with the established activity landscape for transition-metal ammonia synthesis.[11,12] After optimizing the operating conditions separately for each catalyst and evaluating catalyst-dependent costs (Fig. 1a,d,e), the order becomes Fe, Ru and Os, with costs of 15.292, 22.031 and 25.832 US dollars per tonne of NH₃, respectively (Fig. 1b,h).

The change is concentrated among the leading candidates. For Ru, Os and Fe, Spearman rho is -0.50 and Kendall tau is -0.33, while the Spearman correlation across all 15 metals remains 0.929 (Fig. 1f,g). The overall screen can therefore remain strongly correlated even when the preferred catalyst changes at the top of the ranking. With three leading candidates these coefficients describe the reordering rather than test it statistically; the decision-relevant quantities are the identity of the winner and the cost of choosing wrongly. Agreement among the leading candidates is thus more relevant to catalyst selection than full-set correlation alone. Agreement can also be expressed in decision terms. If the intrinsic-activity winner Ru were selected instead of the economic winner Fe, the catalyst-dependent cost objective would increase from 15.292 to 22.031 US dollars per tonne of NH₃, corresponding to a normalized decision regret of **44.1%**. The 6.739 US dollars per tonne difference is a small fraction of the cost of ammonia from state-of-the-art gas-based plants, about 160 US dollars per tonne,[49] but it is the part of the cost that catalyst selection controls and amounts to 2.34 million US dollars per year for a 1,000 t/d plant.

### Supplementary Note 2 | Metal price and process optimization jointly determine the Fe-Ru ranking

The leading metals favor different operating conditions. Fe has an interior optimum near 425 °C and 180 bar with a 30 °C separator, whereas Ru favors approximately 450 °C and 425 bar with a 25 °C separator; Os has a broad, shallow minimum at high pressure (Fig. 3b,c). The economic comparison therefore reflects both catalyst properties and the operating conditions favored by each catalyst.

To isolate the effect of metal price, we repeated the Ru optimization after setting its metal price equal to the Fe value of 8 US dollars per kilogram. All other model inputs, constraints and costing assumptions were unchanged. The Ru optimum shifts from 450 °C and 425 bar to 425 °C and 170 bar, and its optimized cost falls to 14.712 US dollars per tonne of NH₃, 0.580 US dollars per tonne below Fe (Fig. 3b–e). Thus removing the Ru-Fe price difference reverses the economic order after process reoptimization.

A full Ru-price sweep, with process reoptimization at every price, places Fe-Ru parity at 163.76 US dollars per kilogram. At this price, Ru operates at 425 °C and 185 bar with a 6.24 m³ catalyst bed (Fig. 3d). Across the sweep, the optimum moves from 170 bar and a 9.4 m³ bed at 1 US dollar per kilogram to 420–430 bar, a 0 °C separator and a 0.014 m³ bed at 3 × 10^5 US dollars per kilogram. Metal price therefore affects the final ranking directly through catalyst cost and indirectly by changing the preferred process conditions.

The model gives every metal the formulation of the industrial fused-iron catalyst, so the same curve can be read at the cost of a real Ru catalyst (Fig. 3d). A promoted Ru/C ammonia catalyst exposes 11% of its Ru atoms,[45] against fewer than 1% of the Fe atoms in reduced fused iron,[46] and more than 94% of the Ru is recovered from spent catalyst.[47] Dispersion and recovery divide the metal charge in the same way as the metal price, so the catalyst enters at an effective price p(1 − r)/u: 294–490 US dollars per kilogram for 90–94% recovery at a dispersion gain u = 11. At that price Ru costs 15.49–15.67 US dollars per tonne of NH₃, 0.19–0.38 above Fe, at 450 °C and 195–200 bar, and the activity multiple required for parity falls from 201-fold to 1.5–2.2-fold. In a KAAP-type loop at 9.1 MPa with a −20 °C condenser,[48] the same supported and recovered catalyst costs 18.53–18.91 US dollars per tonne against 19.07 for fused iron in that loop. Metal economy through dispersion and recovery is therefore the route that brings Ru to the Fe decision boundary, the route taken industrially by the Kellogg Advanced Ammonia Process.

The baseline Ru-Fe cost difference of 6.739 US dollars per tonne is distributed across several cost components. Fresh-feed compression contributes +4.632 US dollars per tonne, metal inventory +1.763, compressor capital +1.181 and refrigeration +0.629, while vessel pressure, recycle compression and reactor-base terms partly offset these contributions (Fig. 3a,e). This decomposition identifies the main contributions to the baseline difference, whereas the equal-price calculation shows how the optimized process changes when the metal-price difference is removed.

A layer-wise check localizes the reversal more precisely. At the common 673 K atomistic reference, converting turnover frequency into the plant-throughput active-metal requirement preserves the leading order: Ru requires less active metal than Os, and both require far less than Fe. Multiplying those same catalyst demands by the canonical metal prices and the frozen 10-year lifetime changes the order to Fe < Ru < Os before any reactor, compression, refrigeration or equipment cost is added: the annualized metal replacement is 0.199 US dollars per tonne of NH₃ for Fe, 33.57 for Ru and 185.27 for Os. As an independent causal check, setting the Ru metal price equal to the Fe price and reoptimizing the complete 14,136-state process leaves Ru less costly than Fe (14.712 versus 15.292 US dollars per tonne of NH₃). The inversion is therefore triggered at the catalyst-demand-to-lifecycle-economics interface. Candidate-specific process optimization then narrows it: Ru moves to a small, high-pressure bed, which reduces the Ru − Fe difference from 33.37 US dollars per tonne in metal replacement at the common reference to 6.739 in total, and moves most of the remaining difference into fresh-feed compression, compressor capital and refrigeration.

Descriptor uncertainty affects feasibility and ranking differently. Across 1,000 descriptor-uncertainty samples, Fe satisfies the 90 m³ bed-volume limit in 79.9% of samples, ranks first economically in 68.1% (681/1000), and remains within the top three in 94.0%. The atomistic winner remains the economic winner in 28.2% of samples (Fig. 3f,g). A supporting error-dependence analysis keeps the same marginal descriptor uncertainties but couples the Ru, Os and Fe errors through a Gaussian copula. The independent case reproduces the frozen atomistic-winner frequencies, whereas increasing positive correlation changes the top-one probabilities rather than simply cancelling uncertainty; at latent pairwise correlation 0.9, Fe is the atomistic winner in 21.7% of draws compared with 10.7% in the matched independent audit. A separate common-offset test shows why: because all three descriptors move across a nonlinear volcano, a shared energetic bias can itself change their ordering. These tests are reported as uncertainty-model sensitivities and are not used to replace the preregistered downstream probabilities.

Economic uncertainty around the baseline case does not overturn the Fe preference. Across 5,000 preregistered samples that vary metal price, the CAPEX coefficient, electricity price and catalyst lifetime, full process reoptimization gives P(C_Fe < C_Ru) = 1.000, with a minimum sampled Ru-Fe cost difference of 2.382 US dollars per tonne (Fig. 3h). Expressed as normalized selection regret, R = (C_Ru − C_Fe)/C_Fe, the 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles are **30.48%, 40.89% and 54.25%**, respectively, and R remains positive in all 5,000 draws. The Ru activity multiplier required for economic parity is 70.78-fold, 174.27-fold and 462.00-fold at the 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles, respectively (Fig. 4b). The equal-price calculation therefore identifies a condition under which the ranking reverses, whereas the uncertainty analysis establishes both the sign and the practical cost consequence of the Fe preference over the economic range examined around the baseline case.

These draws charge Ru as the benchmark bed of pure metal. Sampling the actual Ru catalyst turns the decision into a probability and shows what sets it. With the Ru dispersion ratio u (11–50) and Ru recovery r (90–94%) added to the same 5,000 draws, Fe is cheaper in 24.0% of draws when the supported catalyst occupies the benchmark bed volume, and in 80.0% when its own Ru content and bed density set the reactor volume. Ru then wins only when both the dispersion ratio and the Ru content are high: in 1.3% of draws with u in its lowest tercile and 50% with u and r in their top terciles, and in 0.5% of draws below 2.5 wt% Ru against 39% at 5 wt% or more; recovery within its range matters little. Drawing the Ru activity and Ru content from the 54 measured Ru catalysts instead, Fe is cheaper in 94.6% of draws with recovery and 98.9% without, and the Ru wins come from five of the 54 catalysts (Ru on Ca(NH₂)₂, Ba–Ca(NH₂)₂ and BaO–CaH₂, Ba–Cs-promoted Ru/CCHT and Ru/AC-G).

### Supplementary Note 3 | Backward design separates the required catalyst-property region from physical reachability

The forward analysis identifies the preferred catalyst but does not show how much Ru would need to improve to reach parity with Fe. We therefore multiplied Ru intrinsic activity by a factor α and reoptimized the full process at each value. Under the baseline economic assumptions, Ru reaches the Fe cost only after a 201.22-fold activity increase (Fig. 4a,c).

The accessible range defined by the scaling relation is much smaller. At 673 K, the largest activity increase along the scaling relation is 1.090-fold, and the largest increase found across all allowed process conditions is 2.525-fold. Under strict scaling, the lowest Ru cost is 21.398 US dollars per tonne of NH₃ at *E*_N = −1.215 eV, which remains above the Fe cost (Fig. 4d,e). The 201.22-fold requirement therefore lies far outside the range accessible by moving Ru along the present scaling relation. Even the 5th-percentile requirement under economic uncertainty, 70.78-fold, remains well above the 2.525-fold maximum (Fig. 4b).

Within the model and scaling relation used here, improving Ru activity alone cannot make Ru economically competitive with Fe over the economic range considered.

Measured catalysts show which modifications supply gains of the required size (Fig. 4a, bottom). Against unpromoted Ru on the same support, Ba and Cs promotion of Ru/C raise the turnover frequency per surface Ru 75- and 65-fold,[50] Ba promotion of Ru/BN raises the rate more than 100-fold at 5 MPa,[51] and Cs promotion of Ru/MgO more than 134-fold at 350 °C,[52] all within the 70.78–462-fold parity band. Electride and oxyhydride supports give gains of about tenfold (9.8-fold for Ru/C12A7:e⁻ and 8.4-fold for Ru/BaTiO₂.₅H₀.₅ at 5 MPa),[53,54] a Ru–Ba core–shell on calcium amide at least 33.5-fold against Cs–Ru/MgO,[55] and confinement inside carbon nanotubes lowers activity to 0.5-fold.[56] Electronic promotion, which changes the energetics beyond a single scaling relation, is the measured route that reaches the parity band. Most of these gains were measured at 0.1–1 MPa; the Ba–Ru/BN and oxyhydride results show the effect at 5 MPa.

The same parity calculation can be written as a two-property lifecycle target at fixed Ru intrinsic activity. The fully reoptimized Ru price sweep gives an effective Ru parity price of 163.76 US dollars per kilogram. Because metal recovery and catalyst lifetime enter the Ru replacement-cost term multiplicatively with metal price, the corresponding boundary is \(P_{Ru}(1-r)(10\,\mathrm{y}/L) \le 163.76\). At the baseline Ru price of 53,852.5 US dollars per kilogram, this requires approximately 99.70% recovery for a 10-year lifetime, 99.39% for a 20-year lifetime, or a 32.9-year lifetime at 99% recovery. The prespecified 5–20-year lifetime range and 99% optimistic recovery bound therefore do not reach parity at the current Ru activity.

Allowing the backward variables to move together changes the required target before physical reachability is tested. We conservatively evaluated the 53 distinct process states already visited by the fully reoptimized Ru price sweep, rescaling catalyst demand by a direct Ru activity multiplier and recomputing the volume- and pressure-dependent cost terms at each state. Because this search uses only a subset of the 14,136-state library, the resulting activity requirements are conservative upper bounds. With 99% Ru recovery, extending catalyst life from 10 to 15 and 20 years lowers the required direct activity multiplier from at most 2.418-fold to 1.742-fold and 1.401-fold, respectively; a 20-year lifetime with 98% recovery requires at most 2.418-fold.

We then tested this target region directly on the strict *E*_N scaling manifold across all 14,136 process states. The canonical strict-scaling minimum, 21.398 US dollars per tonne at *E*_N = −1.215 eV, is reproduced before lifecycle changes are introduced. The joint parity boundary is controlled by the catalyst-loss factor \(q=(1-r)/L\), with \(q^*=4.4068\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{y}^{-1}\). Within the prespecified range of catalyst lifetimes up to 20 years and Ru recovery up to 99%, the closest point is 20 years and 99% recovery: its best strict-scaling state occurs at *E*_N = -1.230 eV and costs 15.362 US dollars per tonne, 0.071 US dollars per tonne above Fe. Parity therefore lies just outside the tested property range, requiring 99.119% recovery at a 20-year lifetime or, equivalently, a 22.69-year lifetime at 99% recovery. The backward-design result is thus a narrow property region near the edge of the present scaling-constrained design space rather than a single activity threshold.

### Supplementary Note 4 | Selectivity, recycle and purge reshape the methanol ranking

The same ranking change appears in CO₂ hydrogenation to methanol through a different process pathway. Four literature-derived Re/TiO₂ catalyst-temperature states were compared (Fig. 5a,b).[13] Using space-time yield per gram of Re as the catalyst metric, the order is 1 wt% Re at 250 °C, 1 wt% Re at 200 °C, 5 wt% Re at 200 °C and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C. After propagation through the recycle and separation model at the 2% purge used in the reference case, the economic order becomes 5 wt% Re at 200 °C, 1 wt% Re at 250 °C, 1 wt% Re at 200 °C and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C, with net production costs of 943, 962, 967 and 1,258 euros per tonne, respectively (Fig. 5c).

The state with the highest space-time yield per gram of Re falls from first to second economically, and the state ranked third by space-time yield is the economic optimum. Selecting the upstream winner would give 961.51 euros per tonne instead of the 943.30 euros per tonne economic optimum, a normalized decision regret of **1.93%**. Across the four states, Spearman rho is 0.40, Kendall tau is 0.33 and two of six pairwise comparisons invert (Fig. 5e). Methane formation consumes reactants and increases gas accumulation, purge, recycle and compression requirements. A local sensitivity analysis at the 5 wt% Re, 250 °C state gives economic leverage values of 0.37579 for methane suppression, 0.05883 for single-pass conversion and 0.00289 for space-time yield (Fig. 5a,g). These sensitivities are consistent with previous CO₂-to-methanol process studies in which recycle, compression and separation make substantial contributions to plant performance.[14,15]

The reordering persists across the full purge range considered. Over 396 purge levels from 0.5% to 40%, the state with the highest space-time yield per gram of Re is never the least-cost option; Spearman rho never exceeds 0.80 and at least one of six pairwise comparisons remains inverted. Optimizing purge separately for each catalyst state changes the detailed order but does not restore the space-time-yield ranking (Fig. 5f).

Counterfactual and backward calculations locate the cause. Moving every state's methane selectivity into methanol reverses the correlation to rho = −0.80 and makes the highest-conversion state the least costly, whereas equalizing conversion raises rho to +0.80. For the space-time-yield winner, no increase in space-time yield reaches parity: unlimited productivity still costs 954.64 euros per tonne against 943.30. Raising its single-pass conversion from 0.23 to 0.281, inside the 0.19–0.40 range measured on the same catalyst family, or reaching complete methanol selectivity does. As in ammonia, the catalyst property that ranks the candidates upstream is not the one that can move the industrial decision.

Propagating each state's own measurement uncertainty separates the robust positions from the unresolved one (Fig. 5d). In 5,000 draws in which the CO₂ conversion and selectivities of every state are sampled independently and the full plant model is re-solved, the economic winner keeps first place in 4,559 draws (91.2%) and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C remains last in all 5,000. The second and third places, 5.5 euros per tonne apart, exchange in 1,054 draws (21.1%). The space-time-yield winner becomes the economic optimum in 413 draws (8.3%), and the complete space-time-yield ranking is recovered in 24 draws (0.5%).

### Supplementary Note 5 | Catalyst effects on process operation determine whether rankings change

The ammonia and methanol results identify different routes by which catalyst properties affect process cost (Fig. 6a). In ammonia, intrinsic activity and metal price determine catalyst inventory and the preferred operating conditions, which in turn change compression, reactor and equipment costs. In methanol, selectivity changes reactant losses, gas accumulation, purge, recycle and compression. Absolute costs are not compared across the two reactions; the comparison concerns how catalyst properties enter each process model.

Au/TiO₂ CO oxidation provides a control in which the relation between particle size and catalyst requirement is monotonic. Low-temperature CO oxidation on supported Au is well known to depend on particle size and support effects.[16,17] The present calculation uses the absolute-rate anchor reported by Janssens et al.[18] and the particle-size dependence measured by Overbury et al.[19] at the closest Au loading. Candidate states differ only in Au particle size from 2 to 6 nm under the same operating condition and process model.

Under this common mapping, intrinsic activity and required catalyst mass preserve the same order, 2 > 3 > 4 > 5 > 6 nm. Spearman rho and Kendall tau are both 1.000, no pairwise comparison inverts, and all 10,000 parameter samples drawn from ranges reported in the literature preserve the complete order (Fig. 6b–d), as required of a control in which catalyst burden is the inverse of activity. The upstream winner is therefore also the minimum-burden downstream choice, giving zero selection regret under this control mapping. When moderate candidate-specific kinetic and operating variations are introduced, the exact order is retained in 92.16% of 10,000 samples over 273.15–293.15 K, with mean rho = 0.99214. Extending the temperature range to 313.15 K reduces exact preservation to 72.62% while retaining mean rho = 0.96802 (Fig. 6e). Process and economic evaluation therefore do not necessarily change a catalyst ranking; changes arise when catalyst properties affect downstream operating requirements strongly enough to alter relative costs (Fig. 6f).

### Supplementary Note 6 | An automated agent extends the analysis to published and computed catalysts

The hand-built analyses compare three ammonia metals, four methanol states and five Au particle sizes. ACSA scales the same analysis to every candidate that publications or databases provide, while the kinetic, process and economic models remain unchanged (Fig. 2a). It performs three tasks. It reads each paper and its Supporting Information and records every catalyst entry with composition, loading, operating conditions, conversion, selectivities and rates, together with the table, figure and page each value comes from. It takes every candidate through the forward chain, with a cheap lower bound deciding which candidates need the full process optimization. Before scoring any new candidate it must reproduce the frozen numbers of the hand-built cases.

Extraction was scored on 50 methanol papers: 28 that one manually curated dataset covers (547 curated entries),[62] 20 that a second curated dataset covers (194 entries with conditions and space-time yield),[67] the Re/TiO₂ study that supplies the hand-built methanol states[13] and one further study. The agent recovers 718 of the 741 curated entries (97%). Values printed in tables are extracted exactly: 74 of 74 CO₂ conversions and 74 of 74 methanol selectivities in main-text tables, and 131 of 132 conversions, 126 of 131 selectivities and 119 of 124 space-time yields in the Supporting Information (Extended Data Fig. 1). Values read from plots fall within the strict tolerance for 181 of 218 conversions and 73 of 196 selectivities. Of the extracted entries that have no curated counterpart, 89% are correct in the first 18 papers, where every one was checked against the source, and 79 of a random 90 (88%) in the 31 papers added later; most of them are entries the curated sets do not list. The same comparison identifies 339 curated cells that differ from the values printed in the papers (Supplementary Table 6). All 21 Re/TiO₂ entries come out exactly.

The self-check passes in all three systems. Taking the extracted Re/TiO₂ entries through the methanol model reproduces the frozen costs of all 21 entries to 2.3 × 10⁻¹³ euros per tonne, including the four hand-built states at 943.30, 961.51, 966.96 and 1,258.17 euros per tonne. In ammonia, the batch code path reproduces the canonical cost of every pure metal to 10⁻⁹ US dollars per tonne. For Au/TiO₂, the rate anchor and the particle-size exponent extracted from the two source papers[18,19] reproduce the required catalyst masses of the 2–6 nm particles, order preservation in all 10,000 literature-envelope samples and the six operating-window stress tests.

Cheap calculations decide where the expensive ones are spent. For each ammonia candidate a lower bound on cost, built from the best-state activity at its descriptor, the smallest reactor and the cheapest process state, requires no process optimization. Across the 1,695 priced bimetallic surfaces this bound excludes 1,397 candidates (82.4%) from the full 14,136-state optimization. Optimizing all 1,695 in full confirms that no candidate below the Fe cost is excluded. In methanol, the plant economics evaluated on lower-bound loop flows at each purge level, valid for any per-pass conversion of recycled CO, replace the equilibrium-constrained purge optimization; taking each comparison group in order of this bound, with the paper's leader always evaluated, needs the full optimization for 206 of the 906 candidates (700 excluded, 77.3%) and finds the plant-cost leader of all 83 groups, at 23% of the compute of evaluating every candidate. Extraction takes about 0.15 million language-model tokens per paper across the main-text, figure and Supporting-Information passes.

### Supplementary Note 7 | Bimetallic surfaces that undercut Fe pair a cheap 3d metal with a group-6 metal

The 15-metal screen contains only three economically feasible candidates. To test whether the Fe preference survives a much larger candidate space, ACSA took the 1,796 bimetallic and pure-metal surfaces of a high-throughput adsorption database[60,61] through the ammonia chain. The most stable N adsorption energy of each surface is placed on the model's step-site descriptor by the terrace-to-step relation that the workbook already uses for Fe, after a linear bridge between the two datasets over the 14 pure metals they share. A second, element-anchored bridge places every pure metal exactly at its frozen descriptor. Metal prices are the frozen model prices for the 15 model metals and 2025 annual averages for the remaining elements.[63]

Among the 372 surfaces that contain only transition metals outside groups 3–5, 138 satisfy the 90 m³ bed-volume limit. Five undercut Fe under the linear bridge: Cu₃Cr (14.36 US dollars per tonne), Fe₃Mo, Cu₃Mo, Fe₃W and Ni₃W. Six do so under the element-anchored bridge: Cu₃Cr, Cu₃Mo, CuMo, MoNi, CoMo and CoW (Extended Data Fig. 6). Every one of them pairs Fe, Co, Ni or Cu with Cr, Mo or W, the combination that descriptor interpolation has identified for ammonia synthesis.[64] The individual alloys depend on the bridge; the family does not. The most active surface ranks 43rd economically (Os₃Pt, 70.9% regret) under the linear bridge and 40th (AuW, 62.9%) under the element-anchored bridge, with Spearman correlations of 0.72 and 0.67 between activity and cost. As in the 15-metal screen, the activity leader is not the economic leader. Counterfactual and backward calculations, run only on these leading surfaces, show the same mechanism as for Ru. At the Fe price both activity leaders would undercut Fe (14.96 and 14.99 US dollars per tonne), whereas at their own prices they need 689-fold and 405-fold activity increases to reach Fe parity, and no descriptor on the strict scaling range brings them below 24.54 and 23.20 US dollars per tonne. Cu₃Cr and Cu₃Mo remain below Fe under both bridges, and Cu₃Cr keeps its lead with 24% of its activity. Surfaces containing group 3–5 elements, which form stable bulk nitrides, and surfaces containing sp metals, which lie outside the transition-metal scaling relations of the activity model, are reported as separate layers (Supplementary Table 3; Extended Data Fig. 6).

### Supplementary Note 8 | Measured ammonia catalysts rank differently by laboratory rate and by plant cost

ACSA also takes measured catalysts through the ammonia chain. It extracted the 164 catalyst rows tabulated in a review of ammonia-synthesis catalysts;[48] two independent extraction passes agree on 697 of 706 numeric fields, and every value was checked against the printed tables. Checking the rows against the primary papers (below) found ten transcription errors in nine rows, among them a space velocity printed ten times too low and two Ru-free catalysts listed as Ru catalysts; the chain uses the papers' values. Each catalyst's measured rate per gram of metal, at its laboratory temperature, pressure and NH₃ fraction, is inverted into an effective descriptor on its metal's side of the volcano, and the catalyst is then optimized over the full process library with its own metal content. The plant calibration reproduces the laboratory rates of the two fused-iron catalysts in the tables within a factor of 2.2 (α = 0.64 and 0.45).

Of the 73 catalysts in the primary set (54 Ru, 9 Fe, 5 Co and 5 Ni), the one with the highest laboratory rate, Ru/AC-G, costs 17.53 US dollars per tonne, 14% above the plant-cost leader, and laboratory rate and plant cost are nearly uncorrelated (Spearman ρ = 0.16). In both studies that test Fe and Ru on the same support, Ru has the higher rate and Fe the lower plant cost (15.2% and 13.0% regret). No measured catalyst undercuts the fused-iron benchmark without metal recovery (lowest 15.32 US dollars per tonne); with 90% Ru recovery three do, the route identified in Fig. 3d.

A review tabulates catalysts measured in different laboratories, whereas a paper's own comparison is between catalysts tested side by side. ACSA therefore extracted 30 primary ammonia-synthesis papers with their Supporting Information. For the 38 entries that the review also tabulates, temperature, pressure, metal content and space velocity are extracted correctly, and the rate in 37 of 38; a random check of 40 further entries against the papers finds 35 correct. Within each paper, catalysts tested at the same temperature, pressure, H₂/N₂ ratio and space velocity form 124 comparisons. The catalyst with the highest rate per gram of catalyst is not the plant-cost leader in 45 of them (36%; 95% confidence interval 19–53% from resampling papers), in 13 of 28 papers, with a median regret of 11% where the leaders differ. Two situations account for most disagreements: in 14 comparisons a catalyst of another metal is the plant-cost leader, in 12 of them an Fe catalyst on the same support with the lower rate, and in 18 a commercial fused-iron catalyst that the paper includes as a reference is the plant-cost leader. Every catalyst in this set that undercuts the fused-iron benchmark is an iron catalyst. With 90% Ru recovery the disagreement is 43 of 124; ranking by rate per gram of metal raises it to 55.

### Supplementary Note 9 | Published methanol leaderboards and plant-cost leaderboards often disagree

Methanol papers rank catalysts by laboratory performance, most often by space-time yield. ACSA extracted 991 operating points with conversion, selectivity, temperature, pressure, H₂/CO₂ ratio and productivity from 45 studies. Within each paper, entries tested at the same pressure, H₂/CO₂ ratio and space velocity form the comparison the paper itself makes, giving 83 comparisons with 906 entries. Each entry was taken through the methanol recycle–economics model at its own conditions, with recycled CO converted to reverse-water-gas-shift equilibrium and the purge optimized for each entry.

The space-time-yield leader is not the plant-cost leader in 33 of 83 comparisons (40%), spread over 19 of the 44 papers, and 1,019 of 8,458 pairwise orderings (12%) invert (Fig. 2b). Ranking by methanol selectivity disagrees more often and at greater cost: in 63 of 83 comparisons (76%) across 40 papers, with a median regret of 15% where the leaders differ. Ranking by conversion disagrees in 16 of 83. Treating recycled CO as inert instead, the opposite bound, raises the space-time-yield disagreement to 46 of 83 comparisons across 37 papers. The disagreement holds for printed values alone (17 of 50 comparisons) and for entries whose reported products close to methanol, CO and methane (22 of 61).

The disagreement is neither a sampling nor a measurement artefact. Resampling papers gives a 95% confidence interval of 24–55% for the share of comparisons whose leader differs, and 23 of the 33 disagreements cost at least 1%. Re-measuring every entry 2,000 times with the conversion and selectivity uncertainty derived for the Re/TiO₂ states keeps 15 of the 33 disagreements in at least 90% of re-measurements, all with a regret of at least 1% (median 5.9%), whereas measurement noise alone changes a paper's space-time-yield leader in 13 comparisons (95% range 8–19). Resampling the agent's measured plot-reading errors leaves 38.5 disagreeing comparisons on average (95% range 31–45). Pricing each catalyst from its composition and charging its replacement per tonne of inventory over three years gives 31 of 83 (37%).

The plant ranking follows from what each by-product does in a recycle loop (Fig. 2d,e). Recycled CO is converted further, so CO selectivity costs little, whereas methane leaves only with the purge. In the Re/TiO₂ study, the space-time-yield leader ranks third because its 3% methane selectivity outweighs its productivity. In a high-pressure Cu/Al₂O₃ study,[65] the space-time-yield leader at 10 MPa (Cu–Ba/Al₂O₃ at 473 K, 62% methanol selectivity, 2.8% conversion) costs 1,170 euros per tonne, against 1,002 for Cu–K/Al₂O₃ at 553 K (0.9% selectivity, 20.7% conversion). The largest regrets, up to 182%, occur in that study, whose productivity leaders run at 1.3–9.3% single-pass conversion and would need a very large recycle; in the other 43 papers the regret stays below 10%. Selectivity leaders are costly because the most selective entry of a comparison usually runs at low conversion and needs a large catalyst inventory and recycle.
