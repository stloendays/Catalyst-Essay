# Supplementary Information

Laboratory catalyst leaders are often not the plant-level optimum

## Supplementary Notes

### Supplementary Note 1 | Process and economic evaluation reverse the leading ammonia ranking

The 15-metal ammonia screen ranks Ru, Os and Fe first, second and third in intrinsic activity (Fig. 1c), consistent with the established activity landscape for transition-metal ammonia synthesis.[11,12] With the operating conditions optimized separately for each catalyst and the catalyst-dependent costs evaluated (Fig. 1a,d,e), the order becomes Fe, Ru and Os, at 11.516, 16.633 and 19.747 US dollars per tonne of NH₃ (Fig. 1b,h). Among the three leaders Spearman ρ is −0.50 and Kendall τ is −0.33, while across all 15 metals ρ remains 0.911 (Fig. 1f,g). The screen stays strongly correlated while the preferred catalyst changes at its top.

Selecting the activity winner Ru instead of Fe raises the catalyst-dependent cost by 5.117 US dollars per tonne, a normalized decision regret of **44.4%**. This is a small fraction of the cost of ammonia from state-of-the-art gas-based plants, about 160 US dollars per tonne,[24] but it is the part that catalyst selection controls. For a 1,000 t/d plant, which at a capacity factor of 0.95 produces 346,750 t of NH₃ per year, it amounts to 1.77 million US dollars per year.

The model was compared with 64 values from industrial ammonia plants (Supplementary Table 4). Of these, 33 lie within their tolerance, among them 17 of the 28 loop states of 13 industrial loop records and every flow and bed volume at plant conditions. The model is low in NH₃ at the converter inlet, separator vapour and compressor power, and its cost-optimal designs start the first bed 49–56 °C above the industrial window. Supplementary Note 7 shows that the Fe–Ru order survives these deviations.

### Supplementary Note 2 | An automated agent extends the analysis to catalysts from papers and databases

ACSA scales the analysis of the hand-built cases to every candidate that papers or databases provide, with the kinetic, process and economic models unchanged (Fig. 2a). It records each catalyst entry of a paper and its Supporting Information with composition, loading, operating conditions, conversion, selectivities and rates, together with the table, figure and page of every value. It takes every candidate through the forward chain and uses a lower bound on cost to decide which candidates need the full process optimization.

Extraction was scored on 50 methanol papers against two curated datasets (547 and 194 entries)[25,26] and the 21 Re/TiO₂ entries of the hand-built case.[13] Extracted and curated entries are paired on paper, catalyst and conditions, never on the values being scored. The agent recovers 719 of the 741 curated entries (97%). Values printed in tables are extracted almost without error: 44 of 44 CO₂ conversions and 44 of 44 methanol selectivities in tables of the main text, and 129 of 130 conversions, 124 of 129 selectivities and 118 of 128 space-time yields (STYs) in Supporting Information tables (Extended Data Fig. 1). Values read from plots of the main text fall within the strict tolerance for 168 of 203 conversions and 65 of 181 selectivities, and plot readings enter only the sensitivity analyses. Of all extracted entries, 85% are correct in the first 18 papers and an estimated 93% in the 31 papers added later, and 98 of 100 sampled values are correct (Supplementary Table 9). All 21 Re/TiO₂ entries come out exactly. The comparison also exposes curated values that the papers contradict: 26 root causes, one mechanism in one source paper each, in 45 rules, 169 cells and 8 rows across the two methanol datasets and the ammonia review (Supplementary Table 8).

From the extracted inputs ACSA reproduces the three hand-built cases, including the four Re/TiO₂ states at 943.30, 961.51, 966.96 and 1,258.17 euros per tonne, before it scores any new candidate. The lower bound on cost then spares most of the expensive optimizations. Across the 1,695 priced bimetallic surfaces it excludes 1,399 (82.5%) from the full 14,510-state optimization, and none of the excluded surfaces would undercut Fe. In methanol it spares the full optimization for 394 of the 632 candidates (62.3%) and still finds the plant-cost leader of all 81 comparisons, at 38% of the compute of evaluating every candidate.

### Supplementary Note 3 | Paper leaderboards and plant-cost leaderboards often disagree in methanol

The methanol comparisons come from two extractions with the same pipeline: 351 operating points with printed conversion, selectivity, temperature, pressure and H₂/CO₂ ratio from 33 papers (Supplementary Table 1) and 348 from 28 of 40 papers that appeared from November 2025 to October 2026 (Supplementary Table 1b). Entries of one paper tested at the same pressure, H₂/CO₂ ratio and space velocity form the comparison the paper itself makes: 81 comparisons with 632 entries in 50 papers (161 groups with values read from plots, Supplementary Table 7). Each entry was taken through the methanol recycle–economics model at its own conditions (Supplementary Methods).

The primary loop limits the content of CO, methane and N₂ at the reactor inlet to 6.86%, the value of the loop that anchors the cost model, because the model holds each entry's single-pass conversion at its laboratory value and a diluted feed would otherwise react as in the laboratory. Industrial loops purge to keep inert gases from accumulating, because they dilute the reactants, slow the synthesis and lower the productivity of the catalyst,[28,29] and loop designs carry 4.4–9.8% CO, methane and N₂ at the reactor inlet. Without the limit the CO at the reactor inlet is a median 8.8% (10th and 90th percentiles 0.9% and 26%), against 1.3–4.7% in the industrial designs.

Compared catalyst by catalyst, each at its best measured operating point and free to load more catalyst and raise its single-pass conversion towards equilibrium at its measured selectivity, the laboratory leader is not the plant-cost leader in 21 of the 63 comparisons with at least two catalysts (33.3%; paper-cluster bootstrap 95% confidence interval 22.1–45.9%), from 19 of their 46 papers. The cost gap exceeds 1%, 5% and 10% in 14, 10 and 8 of them (median 3.5%), and the 10 above 5% come from 9 papers. The counts are the same with conversion raised to 90, 95 or 99% of equilibrium, and merging samples that differ only in synthesis gives 18 of 52 (34.6%; 21.8–47.5%). With every entry held at its laboratory conversion, the count is 26 of 63 (41.3%; 29.4–53.3%) from 21 papers, with the cost gap above 1%, 5% and 10% in 23, 19 and 13 (median 9.6%), and 22 of 52 with the stricter identity. The two treatments of catalyst amount bracket the share at 33–41%, a range over two model treatments rather than a confidence interval. Of the 112 catalysts measured at two or more temperatures, 82 have their lowest plant cost at another temperature than their highest STY at laboratory conversion. The plant temperature is lower for 75 of them (median 50 °C lower) and higher for 7, and running the 82 at the temperature of their highest STY costs more than 5% extra for 74 (median 78%).

Over every operating point of every catalyst, the STY leader is not the plant-cost leader in 50 of 81 comparisons (61.7%; 48.9–72.2%), 42 with a cost gap above 5%, in 32 of the 50 papers, and 1,992 of 5,283 pairwise orderings invert. Of the 50, 26 are changes of catalyst, 13 are comparisons in which one catalyst leads both rankings at different operating points and 11 are comparisons of a single catalyst. The plant-cost leader is more selective in 40 of the 50 and makes less CO in 39. Ranking by single-pass yield gives 50 of 81, by conversion 54 of 81 and by methanol selectivity 30 of 81. Without the inlet limit the leaders differ in 25 of 81 comparisons (30.9%; 16.9–44.6%), 10 above 5%.

Re-measuring every entry 2,000 times within its measurement error keeps 42 of the 50 disagreements in at least 90% of draws, whereas measurement noise alone changes a paper's STY leader in 9.1 comparisons on average (95% range 5–14). In 26 of the 50 disagreements the paper leader meets the inlet limit only with a large purge and costs more than twice its cost without the limit. All 26 persist under re-measurement, and of the other 24, 17 exceed 5% and 16 persist. As the inlet limit is raised from 4% to 30% the count falls from 54 to 26 of 81, against 25 without a limit (Extended Data Fig. 10). Between catalysts, with more catalyst loaded, the leaders differ in 29, 25, 21, 19 and 15 of 62–63 comparisons at limits of 4, 5, 6.86, 8 and 10%, and in 8 without a limit. At laboratory conversion the counts are 31, 28, 26, 25 and 23, and 16 without a limit. Removing the limit removes the accumulation that plants purge to avoid, and with it most of the disagreements. Across the other variants (recycled CO inert or converted up to CO-hydrogenation equilibrium, more catalyst loaded to raise conversion, catalysts priced by composition, unreported CO–methane splits at either bound and variants of the plant terms) the count stays between 41 and 56 of 81. It is 43 of 73 without the paper that supplies most comparisons and 101 of 160 with entries read from plots.

The choice of the limit matters little for the catalyst a plant would pick. The plant-cost leader at 6.86% is within 5% of the cheapest operating point at every limit from 5 to 10% in 62 of 81 comparisons, against 63 for an oracle that knows every limit's costs, 45 for the most selective entry and 31 for the paper's STY leader.

### Supplementary Note 4 | Measured ammonia catalysts rank differently by laboratory rate and by plant cost

ACSA extracted the 164 catalyst rows tabulated in a review of ammonia synthesis catalysts[31] and checked them against the primary papers, which corrected ten transcription errors in nine rows. Each catalyst's measured rate per gram of metal is inverted into an effective descriptor on its metal's side of the volcano, and the catalyst is optimized over the full process library with its own metal content. The plant calibration reproduces the laboratory rates of the two fused-iron catalysts in the tables within a factor of 1.9.

Of the 73 catalysts in the primary set (54 Ru, 9 Fe, 5 Co and 5 Ni), the one with the highest laboratory rate, Ru/AC-G, costs 13.76 US dollars per tonne, 25% above the plant-cost leader, and laboratory rate and plant cost are uncorrelated (Spearman ρ = 0.07). In both studies that test Fe and Ru on the same support, Ru has the higher rate and Fe the lower plant cost (17.8% and 12.9% regret). Two measured catalysts undercut the fused-iron benchmark without metal recovery, a Cs- and Ba-promoted Ru catalyst (Ru/Cs/Ba/CCHT, 11.01 US dollars per tonne) and a BaH₂-promoted Fe catalyst (20% Fe–BaH₂, 11.19), and with 90% Ru recovery four Ru catalysts do.

A paper's own comparison is between catalysts tested side by side, so ACSA also extracted 52 primary ammonia synthesis papers with their Supporting Information (Supplementary Tables 2 and 2b). For the 37 entries that the review also tabulates, every extracted temperature, pressure, metal content and space velocity agrees with the paper and the rate is correct in 36 of 37, and 35 of 40 further entries checked at random are correct. Catalysts with printed rate, temperature and outlet that one paper tested at the same temperature, pressure, H₂/N₂ ratio and space velocity form 44 comparisons in 31 papers. The catalyst with the highest rate per gram of catalyst is not the plant-cost leader in 12 of them (27%; 95% confidence interval 14–42%), in 10 of 31 papers, and 8 of the 12 have a regret above 5% (median 13%). The plant-cost leader is a commercial fused-iron reference in 5 of the 12, a catalyst of another metal in 3 (an Fe catalyst on the same support in all 3), the same metal on another support or with another promoter in 2 and the same metal at another content in 2. Ranking by rate per gram of metal gives 15 of 44 and 90% Ru recovery 10 of 44. In 4 of the 44 comparisons the plant-cost leader undercuts the fused-iron benchmark: the three commercial fused-iron references (9.83–9.84 US dollars per tonne) and a 20 wt% Co@BaO/MgO catalyst at 400 °C and 6.1 MPa (11.23).

**Kinetic basis.** The comparison carries each laboratory rate to plant conditions on the descriptor kinetics. Of the 237 entries, 167 were measured outside the fitted range of every measured rate law, most at 0.1–1 MPa and 0.1–0.5% NH₃. Carried instead on the measured laws of commercial fused iron[42] and promoted Ru/C,[35] each calibrated on the entry's printed rate and optimized inside its fitted pressure range (Co and Ni at their descriptor cost), 98 entries need more than the 90 m³ bed at every state, because a Temkin-type rate at trace NH₃ calibrates a small activity factor. In the 25 comparisons that keep two buildable entries the leaders differ in 4, all above 5%. Ranking unbuildable entries last, calibrating over the laboratory bed or scaling each comparison so that its fused-iron reference runs at commercial activity gives 4 of 44. The Ru/CeO₂ law[67] gives 5 of 27, although its printed parameters give a 1.21-fold rate gain from H₂/N₂ = 3 to 1.5, against more than 1.8-fold measured. On the fused-iron law the commercial references of these papers run at 0.5–6% of commercial KM1R activity.

### Supplementary Note 5 | Selectivity, recycle and purge reshape the methanol ranking

Four Re/TiO₂ catalyst–temperature states from the literature were compared (Fig. 3a,b).[13] By STY per gram of Re, the order is 1 wt% Re at 250 °C, 1 wt% Re at 200 °C, 5 wt% Re at 200 °C and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C. Through the recycle and separation model at the 2% purge of the reference case, the economic order becomes 5 wt% Re at 200 °C, 1 wt% Re at 250 °C, 1 wt% Re at 200 °C and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C, at net production costs of 943, 962, 967 and 1,258 euros per tonne (Fig. 3c). Selecting the STY winner would cost 961.51 euros per tonne instead of 943.30, a normalized decision regret of **1.93%**. Across the four states Spearman ρ is 0.40, Kendall τ is 0.33 and two of six pairwise comparisons invert (Fig. 3e).

Methane formation consumes reactants and increases gas accumulation, purge, recycle and compression. At the 5 wt% Re, 250 °C state the economic leverages are 0.37579 for methane suppression, 0.05883 for single-pass conversion and 0.00289 for STY (Fig. 3a,g), consistent with CO₂-to-methanol process studies in which recycle, compression and separation make substantial contributions to plant performance.[14,15] Over 396 purge levels from 0.5% to 40%, the STY winner is never the least-cost option, Spearman ρ never exceeds 0.80 and at least one of six pairwise comparisons remains inverted. Optimizing purge separately for each state changes the detailed order but does not restore the STY ranking (Fig. 3f).

Counterfactual and backward calculations locate the cause. Moving every state's methane selectivity into methanol reverses the correlation to ρ = −0.60, whereas equalizing conversion raises it to +1.00. For the STY winner no increase in STY reaches parity, since unlimited productivity still costs 954.64 euros per tonne against 943.30. Raising its single-pass conversion from 0.23 to 0.279, inside the 0.19–0.40 range measured on the same catalyst family, or reaching complete methanol selectivity does.

In 5,000 draws in which the CO₂ conversion and selectivities of every state are sampled from their measurement uncertainty and the plant model is re-solved, the economic winner keeps first place in 4,559 draws (91.2%), and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C remains last in all 5,000 (Fig. 3d). The second and third places, 5.5 euros per tonne apart, exchange in 1,054 draws (21.1%). The STY winner becomes the economic optimum in 413 draws (8.3%), and the complete STY ranking is recovered in 24 draws (0.5%).

### Supplementary Note 6 | Alloy surfaces that undercut Fe pair a cheap 3d metal with a group-6 metal

To test whether the Fe preference survives a much larger candidate space, ACSA took the 1,796 bimetallic and pure-metal surfaces of a high-throughput adsorption database[32,33] through the ammonia chain. The most stable N adsorption energy of each surface is placed on the model's step-site descriptor through a linear bridge over the 14 pure metals the two datasets share, or through a bridge anchored to each element that places each of the 14 metals exactly at its reference descriptor (Supplementary Methods). Metal prices are the model prices for the 15 model metals and USGS annual averages for 21 further elements.[66]

Among the 372 surfaces that contain only transition metals outside groups 3–5, 137 satisfy the 90 m³ limit on bed volume. Eight undercut Fe under the linear bridge: Cu₃Cr (10.14 US dollars per tonne), Fe₃Mo, Ni₃W, Fe₃W, Cu₃Mo, Co₃Mo, Co₃W and Ni₃Mo. Nine do so under the bridge anchored to each element: Cu₃Cr, Cu₃Mo, CuMo, MoNi, CoMo, CoW, Fe₃Mo, Ni₃W and Fe₃W (Extended Data Fig. 6). Every one pairs Fe, Co, Ni or Cu with Cr, Mo or W, the combination that descriptor interpolation has identified for ammonia synthesis.[34] The individual alloys depend on the bridge, whereas the family does not, and Cu₃Cr, Fe₃Mo, Ni₃W, Fe₃W and Cu₃Mo remain below Fe under both.

The most active surface ranks 12th economically (Ni₃Re, 28.0% regret) under the linear bridge and 45th (Rh₃Re, 86.9%) under the bridge anchored to each element, with Spearman correlations of 0.71 and 0.67 between activity and cost. At the Fe price both activity leaders would undercut Fe (10.15 and 10.33 US dollars per tonne), whereas at their own prices they need 5.5-fold and 421-fold activity increases to reach Fe parity, and no descriptor on the strict scaling range brings them below 12.95 and 18.84 US dollars per tonne. Surfaces containing group 3–5 elements, which form stable bulk nitrides, and surfaces containing sp metals, which lie outside the transition-metal scaling relations of the activity model, are reported as separate layers (Supplementary Table 3).

### Supplementary Note 7 | Metal price and process optimization jointly determine the Fe–Ru ranking

Fe has an interior optimum near 425 °C and 105 bar, whereas Ru favours about 450 °C and 220 bar, both with a 30 °C separator, and Os has a broad, shallow minimum at high pressure (Fig. 4b,c). With the Ru price set to the Fe value of 8 US dollars per kilogram and everything else unchanged, the Ru optimum shifts to 400 °C and 95 bar and its cost falls to 10.237 US dollars per tonne, 1.279 below Fe (Fig. 4b–e). A full Ru price sweep with process reoptimization at every price places Fe–Ru parity at 403.54 US dollars per kilogram, where Ru operates at 425 °C and 110 bar with a 3.82 m³ bed (Fig. 4d). Across the sweep the optimum moves from 95 bar and an 18.2 m³ bed at 1 US dollar per kilogram to 340 bar and a 0.011 m³ bed at 3 × 10⁵ US dollars per kilogram.

The reversal arises where catalyst demand meets lifecycle economics. Converting the turnover frequency (TOF) into the active metal required at plant throughput preserves the activity order, with Ru requiring less metal than Os and both far less than Fe. Multiplying by the metal prices and the 10-year lifetime gives an annualized metal replacement of 0.204, 4.09 and 22.28 US dollars per tonne for Fe, Ru and Os, and the order becomes Fe < Ru < Os before any reactor or compression cost is added. Process optimization does not close the gap. Ru moves to a small, high-pressure bed, and the Ru − Fe difference grows from 3.89 US dollars per tonne in metal replacement to 5.117 in total, of which compression of fresh feed contributes +3.988, metal inventory +1.694 and compressor capital +1.024 (Fig. 4a,e).

**Measured rate laws.** At 90 bar, 400 °C and 2% NH₃ the descriptor kinetics give Ru apparent orders of +2.4 in H₂ and −1.9 in NH₃ and fused iron −0.1 in NH₃. For the two frontier catalysts the rates were therefore replaced by measured rate laws: Model 3 of Keestra et al. for commercial fused iron,[42] fitted from 1 to 324 bar, with the activity factor of the Topsoe KM1R catalyst fitted by Dyson and Simon[40,41] (apparent orders N₂ +1.00, H₂ +0.71, NH₃ −0.89), and the Ru/C rate equation of Rossetti et al.[35] (N₂ +0.53, H₂ +0.32, NH₃ −0.39). The Ru/C law reproduces the 125 measured points of its source with a mean error of 10.1%. The fused-iron law gives 9.3% NH₃ against 8% measured for a commercial magnetite catalyst at 100 bar, 430 °C and 30,000 h⁻¹, and at 151–313 bar it requires 1.05–2.3 times the bed that Dyson and Simon fitted. With each law optimized only within its fitted pressure range, Fe costs 15.31 US dollars per tonne at 450 °C, 110 bar and a 15 °C separator and Ru in the benchmark formulation 45.87 at 100 bar. The activity multiple for parity is 15.68 (15.68–45.39 across sizing and calibration choices), against 114.67 with the descriptor kinetics.

**Supported Ru/C.** A promoted Ru/C ammonia catalyst exposes 11% of its Ru atoms,[35] against fewer than 1% of the Fe atoms in reduced fused iron,[36] carries about 8 wt% Ru,[37] and 90–95.55% of the Ru in spent promoted Ru/C catalysts is recovered (93.05–95.55%[38] and 91.1–95.4%[39]). Supported Ru/C (8 wt% Ru, 490 kg m⁻³, 95.55% recovery) has its optimum at 100 bar and costs 3.83 US dollars per tonne less than Fe without metal financing and 2.13 more at 12% per year, and 1.21 less and 1.95 more in a loop of the Kellogg Advanced Ammonia Process (KAAP) type. Ru/C stays cheaper than Fe up to a metal financing rate of 6.49% per year, and in the KAAP-type loop up to 4.57% per year (5.98% and 4.00% at 90% recovery). Priced as a supplier lease without deposit, the break-even lease rates are 6.81% and 5.00% per year. At the reference rate of 2.10% per year, the median of 32 fixed-rate leases of platinum and palladium catalyst metal that one refiner disclosed for 2013–2022 (1.35–5.10% per year),[44] Ru/C is 1.99 US dollars per tonne cheaper than Fe at the two catalysts' own optima and 0.65 cheaper in the KAAP-type loop. Across the disclosed range it is 2.37–0.58 cheaper at the own optima, and in the KAAP-type loop 0.85 cheaper at 1.35% and 0.14 dearer at 5.10% per year. Across 16 cases that vary prices, bed density, recovery, catalyst life, effectiveness factors, the converter pressure premium and bed sizing, the break-even financing rate spans 1.23–15.65% per year in the main loop and 0.71–8.43% per year in the KAAP-type loop (Extended Data Fig. 7).

**Staged converter.** With both catalysts available, a three-bed adiabatic converter at 9 MPa (fused iron up to 530 °C, Ru/C up to 460 °C, the top of its measured range) is cheapest as fused iron upstream with Ru/C downstream for Ru financing rates of 0–5.46% per year and as all-Fe above. This is the bed order of the KAAP converter[43] and of earlier Fe + Ru multibed designs.[45] The Ru/C share of the catalyst volume falls from 69% to 5% across the window, and a converter with Ru/C first is optimal in no case tested. The Ru/C rate advantage over fused iron per kilogram grows downstream, from 9-fold at 2% to 62-fold at 14% NH₃ (90 bar, 400 °C), because ammonia inhibits fused iron more strongly, and fused iron takes the hottest bed because Ru/C is held to 460 °C. The window moves with recovery (0–4.92% per year at 90%) and with the Ru price (0.12–8.21% at −30%, 0–4.03% at +30%). At the reference 2.10% per year the least-cost converter is fused iron followed by two Ru/C beds (5.8, 1.2 and 1.9 m³, fused iron 65% of the catalyst volume), at 17.95 against 18.32 US dollars per tonne for all-Fe. In the KAAP converter the first, iron bed holds about half of the catalyst.[46]

An independent five-component Peng–Robinson loop model[65] with two cold flashes, purge and a converged recycle, sizing each bed with the same rate laws for 1,500 t/d of NH₃, gives Ru/C 1.91 US dollars per tonne dearer than fused iron at 12% per year in the KAAP-type loop, against 1.95 in the cost model, and a financing break-even of 5.42% per year, against 4.57%.

**Robustness.** Raising the recycle pressure rise from 3 bar to the 9.2 bar of the 1,500 t/d Krupp–Uhde flowsheet[62] gives Fe 13.595, Ru 17.129 and Os 19.966 US dollars per tonne with the rank order of all 15 metals unchanged (Spearman ρ = 1.000), and Ru still needs a 67-fold activity gain. The 31 benchmark items outside tolerance trace to five model components (separator vapour, approach to equilibrium, compression power, catalyst amount and effective bed temperature). Perturbing each by its typical and largest deviation in both directions (Supplementary Table 4b), no single perturbation reverses Fe and Ru with the descriptor kinetics (28 cases, smallest margin +1.34 US dollars per tonne), and Fe stays ahead in all 64 sign combinations at the typical deviations (smallest margin +2.39). At the largest deviations 18 of 64 combinations reverse the order, every one with the catalyst amount multiplied by 7.58, the largest deviation of a cost-optimal design volume. With the measured rate laws, typical deviations move the Ru/C − Fe difference by −2.31 to +1.18 US dollars per tonne without financing and −1.23 to +1.16 at 12% per year, and no single typical deviation reverses the order. Across 1,000 samples of descriptor uncertainty, Fe satisfies the 90 m³ bed limit in 79.2%, ranks first in 65.3% and stays in the top three in 92.3%, while the atomistic winner remains the economic winner in 27.1% (Fig. 4f,g). Across 5,000 samples of metal price, capital cost, electricity price and catalyst lifetime, \(P(C_{\mathrm{Fe}} < C_{\mathrm{Ru}}) = 1.000\), with a smallest Ru − Fe difference of 1.995 US dollars per tonne (Fig. 4h). The regret has 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles of **31.52%, 42.21% and 56.99%**, and the activity multiple for parity of 43.52-, 104.97- and 262.28-fold (Fig. 6a).

**Measured separator vapour and multistage compression.** The separator vapour p_sat/P lies 40% below the measured NH₃ content of saturated 3:1 H₂/N₂ at 10–30 MPa.[61] Taking it from those measurements (64 points, −30 to 40 °C, 5–100 MPa), compressing fresh gas in intercooled polytropic stages and the recycle across a 9.2-bar loop pressure drop, and recalibrating catalyst productivity (NH3-FINAL-1.3) predicts the two separator vapours of the Uhde stream table[62] within −6.2% and −3.3% (against −42% and −43%), brings converter-inlet NH₃ from typically 63% to 11–47% below the plant values and leaves compression power 38–46% low. Fe, Ru and Os cost 12.64, 17.81 and 21.50 US dollars per tonne (Ru − Fe 5.17), and Ru matches Fe at 527 US dollars per kilogram or with an 87-fold activity gain. On the measured laws bulk Ru needs 27.5-fold or 1,894 US dollars per kilogram, and Ru/C is 0.70 and 0.77 US dollars per tonne cheaper than fused iron at 2.10% per year (break-even 3.24% and 4.84% per year).

### Supplementary Note 8 | Lifecycle engineering, not activity, brings Ru to parity

With Ru intrinsic activity multiplied by a factor α and the full process reoptimized at each value, Ru reaches the Fe cost only after a 114.67-fold increase (Fig. 6a). Along the scaling relation at 673 K the largest increase is 1.023-fold, and the largest across all process conditions 1.575-fold. Under strict scaling the lowest Ru cost is 16.455 US dollars per tonne at \(E_{\mathrm{N}} = -1.180\) eV (Fig. 6b), and even the 5th-percentile requirement under economic uncertainty, 43.52-fold, lies far above the reachable range.

Measured catalysts show which modifications supply gains of that size (Fig. 6a, bottom). Against unpromoted Ru on the same support, Ba and Cs promotion of Ru/C raise the TOF per surface Ru 75- and 65-fold,[47] Ba promotion of Ru/BN raises the rate more than 100-fold at 5 MPa,[48] and Cs promotion of Ru/MgO more than 134-fold at 350 °C.[49] All four lie within the 43.52–262-fold parity band. Electride and oxyhydride supports give about tenfold (9.8-fold for Ru/C12A7:e⁻ and 8.4-fold for Ru/BaTiO₂.₅H₀.₅ at 5 MPa),[50,51] a Ru–Ba core–shell on calcium amide at least 33.5-fold against Cs–Ru/MgO,[68] and confinement inside carbon nanotubes 0.5-fold.[52] Electronic promotion, which changes the energetics beyond a single scaling relation, is the measured route that reaches the parity band.

At fixed activity the same parity can be written as a lifecycle target. Metal recovery \(r\) and catalyst lifetime \(L\) enter the replacement cost multiplicatively with the metal price, so with the parity price of 403.54 US dollars per kilogram the boundary is

$$P_{\mathrm{Ru}}\,(1-r)\,\frac{10\ \mathrm{y}}{L}\le 403.54\ \mathrm{US\ dollars\ per\ kilogram}.$$

At the Ru price of 53,852.5 US dollars per kilogram this requires about 99.25% recovery for a 10-year lifetime, 98.50% for a 20-year lifetime, or a 13.3-year lifetime at 99% recovery. Letting activity and lifecycle move together on the process states of the price sweep, a 10-year life with 99% recovery needs at most a 1.291-fold activity increase, and 15- and 20-year lives need none. Tested directly on the strict \(E_{\mathrm{N}}\) scaling manifold across all 14,510 process states, the parity boundary is set by the loss factor \(q=(1-r)/L\), at \(q^*=8.2510\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{y}^{-1}\). At 20 years and 99% recovery the best state on the strict scaling relation, at \(E_{\mathrm{N}} = -1.190\) eV, costs 11.199 US dollars per tonne, 0.317 below Fe, and at 15 years 11.361, 0.154 below Fe. Parity requires 98.350% recovery at a 20-year lifetime or a 12.12-year lifetime at 99% recovery. Lifecycle engineering thus reaches parity inside the present design space constrained by the scaling relation, where activity alone cannot.

### Supplementary Note 9 | Catalyst effects on process operation determine whether rankings change

In ammonia, intrinsic activity and metal price set the catalyst inventory and the preferred operating conditions, which change compression, reactor and equipment costs. In methanol, selectivity changes reactant losses, gas accumulation, purge, recycle and compression. Au/TiO₂ CO oxidation, in which the catalyst requirement falls monotonically with decreasing particle size, is the control.[16,17] The calculation uses the anchor of the absolute rate of Janssens et al.[18] and the dependence on particle size of Overbury et al.[19] for 2–6 nm particles under one operating condition.

The particles share one metal and loading, so every cost ratio equals the ratio of required catalyst mass: 1, 2.16, 3.73, 5.70 and 8.06 for 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 nm at the nominal size exponents \(n = 0.9\) and \(m = 1.0\) (Extended Data Fig. 8c). Shared exponents alone keep the order by construction, so each size is also given its own activity scatter. With a log-normal scatter of \(\sigma = 0.25\) on top of \(n\sim U(0.7,\,2.0)\) and \(m\sim U(0.8,\,1.2)\), the full order 2 > 3 > 4 > 5 > 6 nm holds in 77.70% of 10,000 draws and 2 nm particles remain the least-cost catalyst in 99.41% (Extended Data Fig. 8d). With temperature and O₂/CO ratio optimized per candidate, the exact order is retained in 92.16% of samples over 273.15–293.15 K and 72.62% over 273.15–313.15 K.

Catalyst life is the one channel that can reverse the order. For sizes \(i < j\) the larger particles become cheaper when \(\tau_j/\tau_i\) exceeds \(m_j/m_i\), at adjacent life ratios of 2.161, 1.727, 1.528 and 1.414. If life scales as \(\tau\propto d^{\lambda}\), cost scales as \(d^{\,n+m-\lambda}\), and every pair inverts at \(\lambda = n + m = 1.9\) (1.5–3.2 over the exponent ranges). With scatter for each size, 2 nm particles remain the least-cost catalyst in 58.1% of draws at λ = 1.8 and 45.7% at λ = 2.0 (Extended Data Fig. 8e). With the operating variation over 273–293 K, the full order holds in 92.16%, 47.36% and 0.11% of draws at λ = 0, 1.0 and 1.9, and it is fully inverted in 64.50% at λ = 2.5 and 90.08% at λ = 3.0 (Extended Data Fig. 8b).

No lifetime–size exponent has been measured for Au/TiO₂, so λ is a model parameter, and the literature fixes the loss mechanism. On powder Au/TiO₂ at 273–313 K, activity is lost through carbonate-like species at the Au–support perimeter, as a loss of active sites without particle growth, and is restored by heating or by H₂ in the feed.[53,54] The loss shows no resolved size dependence, which corresponds to λ ≈ 0, and the rate anchor of the control is itself the activity after a 10–15 h stabilization at 273 K.[18] Where Ostwald ripening controls life, the classical ripening laws (\(d^3\) or \(d^4 \propto t\)) give λ = 3–4, and the chemical potential of Au on TiO₂(110) makes sub-3 nm particles the least stable.[55] The upstream winner is therefore the plant-level optimum wherever activity loss is reversible and independent of size, and the boundary of that statement is \(\lambda = n + m\).

**Observed pairwise transfer.** For a comparison of A with B in which A has the larger upstream property, \(s_A>s_B\), the lower-is-better downstream objective \(J\) is summarized by

$$\chi_{AB}=\frac{\ln\left(J_A/J_B\right)}{\ln\left(s_A/s_B\right)}.$$

A value \(\chi_{AB}<0\) means that the upstream lead of A comes with a lower downstream burden, \(\chi_{AB}>0\) that A is more costly despite its lead. A value of −1 follows from an inverse-proportional mapping \(J\propto s^{-1}\), as in the Au/TiO₂ control under fixed conditions.

| Comparison (A relative to B) | Upstream metric \(s_A/s_B\) | Downstream burden \(J_A/J_B\) | \(\chi_{AB}\) | Decision under the stated model |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Au/TiO₂, 2 nm versus 6 nm | Mass activity ≈8.1× | Required catalyst mass ≈0.124× | ≈−1.00 | Rank preserved under the inverse-activity mapping |
| CO₂-to-methanol, the STY winner and the economic optimum | STY ≈3.6× | Modeled total production cost ≈1.019× | ≈+0.015 | Upstream lead is reversed, with a **1.9%** cost penalty |
| NH₃, Ru versus Fe | Intrinsic TOF ≈607× | Modeled **catalyst-dependent** cost ≈1.44× | ≈+0.057 | Upstream lead is reversed, with a **44%** penalty in the stated cost component |

The three systems use different upstream quantities, so χ compares the direction and strength of propagation within each system rather than across reactions.

### Supplementary Note 10 | Ru against Ni in CO₂ methanation

Moioli and Züttel[20] compared 0.5 wt% Ru/Al₂O₃ with Ni/Al₂O₃ for CO₂ methanation in their own reactor model. Ru/Al₂O₃ reaches 99.5% conversion in one stage, whereas Ni/Al₂O₃ needs 1.89 times the catalyst volume and an intermediate stage of water condensation between two reactive stages. The Ru bed costs 2.64 times the Ni bed on a commercial catalyst basis and 5.20 times on a metal basis, and they recommended Ru for small and Ni for large plants. The hypotheses and the criterion were fixed before the model was written. Ru is cheaper when

$$r_V\left(1+\varphi_{\mathrm{proc}}(S)\right)>\Pi,$$

where \(r_V = V_{\mathrm{Ni}}/V_{\mathrm{Ru}}\) is the inventory advantage of Ru, \(\Pi\) the Ru-to-Ni catalyst price ratio per kilogram (metal loading × metal price × manufacturing basis, net of recovery) and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{proc}}(S)\) the capital of the second Ni stage with its condensation set per unit of annual Ni catalyst cost at plant scale \(S\). Costs are catalyst-dependent US dollars per MWh of synthetic natural gas (SNG, LHV).

At the central parameters, Π = 5.18 and \(r_V\) = 1.89, so inventory alone favours Ni at every scale. The process penalty \(\varphi_{\mathrm{proc}}\) falls with scale because inventory grows linearly with throughput while vessel capital grows as \(S^{0.52}\) plus a fixed part: 9.13 at 0.1 MW, 1.74 at the parity scale and 0.038 at 100 MW. Ru costs 19.69 against 42.41 US dollars per MWh at 0.1 MW and 8.47 against 3.24 at 100 MW, and parity lies at 0.595 MW (about 75 Nm³ h⁻¹ of CH₄). The boundary moves towards Ni with Ru price and shorter catalyst life: at a 5-year life it lies at 0.854, 0.595 and 0.142 MW for 0.1, 1 and 10 times the Ru price. Every deterministic variant keeps Ru at 0.1 MW and Ni at 100 MW, with the boundary between 0.14 and 37.6 MW. Over 10,000 draws of the bracketed parameters the parity scale has a median of 0.86 MW (5–95% range 0.18–7.0 MW), and Ru is cheaper in 98.3%, 44.0%, 2.9% and 0.05% of draws at 0.1, 1, 10 and 100 MW (Extended Data Fig. 9).

The source tabulates the Ni rate constant of Koschany et al. as 3.46 × 10⁻⁵ mol bar⁻¹ s⁻¹ g⁻¹, whereas two other tabulations give 3.46 × 10⁻⁴. Isothermal plug-flow integration gives \(V_{\mathrm{Ni}}/V_{\mathrm{Ru}}\) = 2.77 with the printed value, consistent with the source's non-isothermal 3.11, and 0.28 with the alternative. The model takes \(r_V\) from the source's reactor simulation and brackets it at 1.5–2.5, and neither isothermal value changes the winner at either end of the scale range.

## Supplementary Methods

### Multiscale decision analysis

Each catalytic system is evaluated with a process-level objective matched to its catalyst-to-process pathway. For ammonia, the objective contains the terms that respond to catalyst identity and performance: catalyst inventory and replacement, reactor requirements, compression, refrigeration and pressure-dependent equipment. Common upstream H₂/N₂ supply is held fixed so that it does not dilute the catalyst-dependent signal. The methanol model evaluates net production cost through its recycle and separation process, and the Au/TiO₂ control maps activity, which depends on particle size, to the required catalyst mass. The plant-level optimum of a set of catalysts is the catalyst with the lowest objective, each at the operating point that minimizes it: over the full process library for descriptor-based and measured ammonia catalysts, within the fitted pressure range for a measured rate law and over its measured operating points and the catalyst inventory a plant would choose for a methanol catalyst from the literature.

Rankings before and after process evaluation are compared using Spearman and Kendall correlations, pairwise inversions, top-rank identity and the normalized decision regret

$$R=\frac{J_{\mathrm{up}}-J_{\mathrm{opt}}}{J_{\mathrm{opt}}},$$

where \(J_{\mathrm{up}}\) is the lower-is-better objective of the upstream winner and \(J_{\mathrm{opt}}\) that of the downstream winner. The leading candidates are analysed separately from the full set so that changes at the top are not hidden by agreement among weaker candidates. Backward design varies one catalyst property, reoptimizes the process and finds the value required for parity, which is compared with the range accessible under the corresponding scaling relation.

### Ammonia synthesis

The ammonia calculation combines the 15-metal activity screen, scaling and kinetic relations, catalyst productivity and inventory, process optimization for each candidate, pressure-dependent equipment capital and seven catalyst-dependent cost components. The NH₃ formation free energy is taken at each temperature from the Gillespie–Beattie equilibrium constant,[58] in place of the DFT gas energies of the activity source, and N₂, H₂ and NH₃ enter the loop equilibrium and the microkinetic gas activities as fugacities with Newton–Cooper coefficients,[40] so that the reactor and the loop share one equilibrium. The atomistic reference conditions (673 K, 24.5 bar N₂, 74.25 bar H₂ and 1 bar NH₃) and the step-site descriptors follow the Wang–Abild-Pedersen Dataset S1,[59] whose leading three metals, Ru, Os and Fe, the reconstruction recovers. The source has no Fe step-site row, so the Fe step descriptor comes from the 14-metal terrace-to-step relation, whose residual standard error, 0.2271 eV, is the Fe descriptor uncertainty (Supplementary Table 5). Catalyst productivity is calibrated on the fused-iron bed at 400 °C, 80 bar and a −35 °C separator. Temperature, pressure and separator temperature are reoptimized for each candidate over 14,510 process states within a 90 m³ limit on bed volume. The Fe optimum (425 °C, 105 bar) lies within the pressure range of industrial Fe loops, which have been built from about 80 bar upwards.[43] Perturbing the compressor, vessel, cost index and capital recovery inputs by 10–25% retains the Fe > Ru > Os order.

The plant benchmark compares the model with 64 values from industrial ammonia synthesis: loop states of 13 industrial loop records, converter runs and designs, compressor power, separator vapour and the Fe optimum (Supplementary Table 4). Point values are compared as (model − industrial)/industrial, temperatures as differences in °C and reported ranges by the distance to the nearer edge, and an item passes when its deviation lies within its tolerance. The typical deviation of a quantity is the median absolute deviation of its items and the largest is their maximum. Each of the five model components behind the failing items was perturbed by its typical and largest deviation, in both directions, alone and in all 32 sign combinations, and Fe, Ru and Os were reoptimized.

The Ru price sweep reoptimizes Ru at 241 logarithmically spaced prices between 1 and 3 × 10⁵ US dollars per kilogram, and Fe–Ru parity is located by bisection in log price. The economic Monte Carlo (5,000 samples) draws Fe and Ru price multipliers log-uniformly over 0.5–2.0, a common capital multiplier over 0.8–1.2, the electricity price over 20–100 US dollars per megawatt-hour and catalyst lifetime over 5–20 years, reoptimizes Fe and Ru in every sample and computes the regret draw by draw as \(R=(C_{\mathrm{Ru}}-C_{\mathrm{Fe}})/C_{\mathrm{Fe}}\). Backward design varies Ru intrinsic activity directly. For the joint activity–lifecycle target, recovery \(r\) and lifetime \(L\) enter through the effective replacement price \(P_{\mathrm{eff}}=P_{\mathrm{Ru}}(1-r)(10\ \mathrm{y}/L)\), evaluated on the 42 process states visited by the price sweep, which gives upper bounds on the required activity. Reachability is then tested on the strict \(E_{\mathrm{N}}\) scaling manifold over all 14,510 states. At a fixed state the metal, reactor and pressure-vessel terms are monotone in the required metal inventory and the compression and refrigeration terms do not depend on \(E_{\mathrm{N}}\), so the cost-minimizing descriptor of each state is its TOF-maximizing value, and the lower envelope over states gives the parity boundary as a function of \(q=(1-r)/L\).[2,11,12,59]

Supported Ru/C is compared with fused iron on measured rate laws: Model 3 of Keestra et al. for commercial fused iron, fitted from 1 to 324 bar, with the activity factor (0.350) of the Topsoe KM1R catalyst,[40–42] and the rate equation of a Ba–Cs–K-promoted Ru/C catalyst fitted at 50–100 bar.[35] Catalyst requirement is integrated along the bed in plug flow, each law is calibrated on its own measured rate and each catalyst is optimized only within its fitted pressure range, so every comparison, including the KAAP-type loop (90 bar, −20 °C separator) and the staged converter, lies inside both fits. The supported bed holds 8 wt% Ru[37] at 490 kg m⁻³, the centre of the 430–550 kg m⁻³ of a promoted Ru/C laboratory bed,[63] with 95.55% recovery. Its Ru dispersion, 11% for the Ba–Cs–K-promoted catalyst[35] and up to about 50% for 2 nm Ru particles in Ba-promoted Ru/C,[64] enters through the measured rate per kilogram of catalyst.

Metal cash flows price every purchase and credit for recovered metal at its date over a 25-year plant life (purchases at 0, 10 and 20 years, recovered Ru credited at replacement and at closure), discounted at a metal financing rate of 0–12% per year. The reference rate is the median of the 32 fixed annual rates that one refiner disclosed for its leases of platinum and palladium catalyst metal in its filings for 2013–2022 (1.35–5.10% per year, terms of 8 months to 3 years).[44] Under these contracts the refiner sold the metal to banks, leased it back for an annual fee on its value and repurchased it at the end of the term, so the fee is the annual cost of holding catalyst metal. No Ru lease rate is disclosed, and the rate is applied to Ru by analogy. In the supplier lease variant the supplier keeps title, the operator pays the annual fee on the Ru value and settles the shortfall at each return, and the operator's cash flows are discounted at 12% per year.

The staged converter has three adiabatic beds with interbed cooling, each filled with fused iron (up to 530 °C) or Ru/C (up to 460 °C), at 9 MPa with a −20 °C separator and 8% inerts. For every arrangement of the two catalysts, including Ru/C upstream of fused iron, bed temperatures and catalyst amounts are optimized, and the least-cost arrangement is read at each financing rate. The 16 robustness cases vary the Fe catalyst price (×0.5, ×2), the Ru/C bed density (430, 550 kg m⁻³), Ru recovery (90%), the Ru price (×0.7, ×1.3), catalyst life (5, 15 years), the effectiveness factor of either catalyst (0.5), the converter pressure premium (×0, ×0.5, ×2) and bed sizing at the inlet rate. The check of the recycle boundary raises the recycle pressure rise from 3 bar to 6.0 and 9.2 bar, the rise in the 1,500 t/d Krupp–Uhde flowsheet,[62] and reoptimizes all 15 metals. The independent loop model is a five-component (N₂, H₂, CH₄, Ar, NH₃) Peng–Robinson loop with the NH₃ binary interaction parameters of Mace and Vrabec.[65]

### Bimetallic surface extension

N adsorption energies (0.5 N₂(g) + * → N*) for the 1,796 surfaces of Mamun et al.[32] were retrieved from Catalysis-Hub,[33] and the most stable site of each surface defines its terrace descriptor. A linear fit over the 14 pure metals present in both this database and the model's descriptor source (slope 0.845, R² = 0.973, RMS 0.22 eV) maps it onto that source's terrace scale, and the 14-metal relation already used for Fe,

$$E_{\mathrm{N}}^{\mathrm{step}}=1.1547\,E_{\mathrm{N}}^{\mathrm{terrace}}-0.1701\ \mathrm{eV},$$

gives the step-site descriptor. The bridge anchored to each element adds each element's offset between its reference and bridged descriptors, weighted by atomic fraction. The cost uses the alloy's mole-weighted molar mass and mass-weighted metal price and is minimized over all states within the bed limit. The 15 model metals keep their model prices, and 21 other elements take USGS annual average prices per kilogram of contained metal (2025, Hg 2024),[66] with oxide, ore and ferroalloy quotations divided by the metal mass fraction. Tc has no market price and its 101 surfaces are not costed. Surfaces are labelled transition-metal, group 3–5 (Sc, Y, La, Ti, Zr, Hf, V, Nb, Ta) or sp-metal (Al, Zn, Cd, Hg, Ga, In, Tl, Sn, Pb, Bi) and scored per layer. The lower bound from the descriptor alone combines a candidate's activity at the best state, the smallest reactor term and the cheapest process state.

### Measured ammonia catalysts

Tables 1–6 of the review[31] were extracted page by page in two independent passes, and rows were checked against the primary papers. Rates are taken per gram of catalyst as printed or derived from the printed outlet NH₃ fraction and space velocity. Catalysts with one model metal and a stated metal content enter the chain (83 of 161 rows). The model's rate per gram of metal is \(F_{\mathrm{CAL}}\,\mathrm{TOF}(E_{\mathrm{N}})/M\), with \(F_{\mathrm{CAL}}\) the fused-iron plant calibration, and each catalyst's measured rate fixes the effective descriptor \(E_{\mathrm{eff}}\) at which the model reproduces it at the laboratory condition, on the side of the volcano where its metal lies. The catalyst is then optimized over the 14,510 states with \(E_{\mathrm{eff}}\), the molar mass and price of its metal, its own metal content and a bed density of 1,000 kg m⁻³ (fused iron: 71.51 wt% and 2,500 kg m⁻³), without metal recovery unless stated. The primary set contains catalysts measured at 300–500 °C under steady thermal operation with an outlet below 90% of equilibrium.

The set of primary papers contains 30 papers on thermal ammonia synthesis that compare at least two catalysts or conditions with a stated metal content, and a further 22 of 25 papers screened under a protocol fixed before extraction (Supplementary Table 2b). Comparison groups contain the entries of one paper at the same temperature, pressure, H₂/N₂ ratio and space velocity. A catalyst is the fused-iron reference only when it is a commercial fused or wüstite iron catalyst without a stated support or added component. Only entries whose rate, temperature and outlet NH₃ fraction are printed enter the primary comparison (179 entries in 44 groups), and plot readings give a sensitivity. The paper leaderboard is the rate per gram of catalyst, and the confidence interval is a paper-cluster bootstrap with 10,000 resamples.

### CO₂-to-methanol

The methanol analysis uses a recycle and separation model, calibrated on a reference CO₂-to-methanol loop,[27] applied to four Re/TiO₂ states derived from the 1 and 5 wt% catalysts of Gothe et al.[13] at 2% purge. Upstream rankings use STY per gram of Re, single-pass methanol yield and STY per gram of catalyst, and the downstream ranking uses net production cost. Purge is varied from 0.5% to 40% over 396 levels and also optimized for each state. The plant model was checked against process assessments and pilot plant data (Supplementary Table 6, Extended Data Fig. 4). At 22–33% per-pass conversion its hydrogen and CO₂ consumption (0.193–0.208 and 1.41–1.47 tonnes per tonne of methanol), carbon efficiency (0.93–0.98) and recycle ratio (2.6–5.4) lie within the reported ranges, and at the operating point and prices of Pérez-Fortes et al.[14] it reproduces the break-even methanol price to within 2.4%. The measurement Monte Carlo samples the CO₂ conversion and selectivities of each state from the reporting resolution (±0.5 percentage points), a 5.2% relative uncertainty in conversion and a 0.85-percentage-point exchange between methanol and methane selectivity, both derived from the source data, and re-solves the plant in each of 5,000 draws. Counterfactuals remove one difference at a time (methane selectivity moved to methanol, or conversion set to the mean of the four states), and backward design changes one property of the STY winner until its cost equals the economic optimum.

### Methanol comparisons within papers

Every extracted record with CO₂ conversion, methanol selectivity, temperature, pressure and H₂/CO₂ ratio is a candidate operating point. The primary analysis uses entries whose conversion and selectivities are printed, and entries read from plots are added as a sensitivity. Comparison groups contain the entries of one paper at the same pressure, H₂/CO₂ ratio and space velocity (Supplementary Table 7). Selectivities are closed as in the hand-built case, with entries below the reporting limit set to zero and both bounds of an unreported CO–methane split tested. Entries whose feed adds water or CO to CO₂ and H₂ are excluded, because the plant model takes a dry CO₂/H₂ make-up. STY per gram of catalyst defines the paper leaderboard: the printed value where every entry of a group prints it, otherwise the value derived from the mass or volumetric space velocity (bulk density 1.0 g mL⁻¹, with 0.5 and 2.0 g mL⁻¹ tested).

The plant leaderboard is the net production cost at each entry's own temperature, pressure and H₂/CO₂ ratio. Recycled CO is converted per pass to the smallest extent that keeps the reactor outlet at or below reverse-water-gas-shift and CO-hydrogenation equilibrium, the single-pass CO₂ conversion is capped at the loop's CO₂-hydrogenation equilibrium, and the purge is optimized over 0.5–40% among the levels at which the content of CO, methane and N₂ at the reactor inlet stays at or below 6.86%, the limit of the loop that anchors the cost model.[27] CO counts toward this limit because at the cost-optimal purge recycled CO is converted little further and dilutes the feed as N₂ does. A disagreement is near-infeasible when the paper leader's cost in the limited loop exceeds twice its cost without the limit or the regret exceeds 100%. In the treatment of catalyst inventory each entry may load more catalyst, raising its conversion along an approach-to-equilibrium curve calibrated on its measured point to at most 95% of equilibrium at constant selectivity.

The lower bound used to allocate the full optimization evaluates the plant economics at each purge level \(p\) on loop flows that hold for any per-pass conversion of recycled CO. Per mole of methanol the reactor inlet carries at least

$$\frac{1}{X\left[S_{\mathrm{MeOH}}+(1-p)\,S_{\mathrm{CO}}\right]}$$

moles of CO₂, with three moles of H₂ per mole of methanol and four per mole of methane, and every equipment term rises with its flow. Within each group the paper's leader is evaluated in full, then the other candidates in increasing order of the bound until the next bound exceeds the best cost found. Sampling uncertainty is a paper-cluster bootstrap with 10,000 resamples. Measurement uncertainty re-draws every entry 2,000 times with the Re/TiO₂ error basis at one, two and four times its width and rebuilds both leaderboards from the same draw.

**Comparison at the catalyst level.** Two entries of a group are different catalysts when the authors report them as different samples that differ in composition, loading of the active component, support or synthesis. Entries of one sample that differ only in reaction temperature, time on stream, activation or reactor operating mode are one catalyst at different operating points. These rules were fixed before any comparison was counted. Each catalyst enters the laboratory ranking with its highest STY and the plant ranking with its lowest plant cost, and a comparison with at least two catalysts changes catalyst when the two leaders differ. In the primary count every entry may load more catalyst as in the treatment of catalyst inventory, while the laboratory ranking keeps the measured STY. The cost gap is the lowest cost of the laboratory leader over that of the plant-cost leader, minus one. A stricter identity merges samples that differ only in synthesis.

**Second extraction.** Papers that appeared from 5 November 2025 to 13 October 2026 were taken in the relevance order of one OpenAlex search ('CO2 hydrogenation methanol catalyst') and screened against the inclusion rules of the first set until 40 were included (Supplementary Table 1b). They were extracted, grouped and costed with the same prompts, model, rules and plant model. Of 40 printed values checked at random against the papers, 39 agree, and the remaining one, a row of the Supporting Information assigned to the wrong catalyst, was corrected without changing any comparison.

### Au/TiO₂ control and catalyst life

The Au/TiO₂ control compares 2–6 nm particles under a common operating condition. The reference rate, Au loading and dispersion are anchored to Janssens et al.,[18] and the dependence on particle size is taken from the 4.5 wt% Au/TiO₂ series of Overbury et al.[19] Mass activity scales as \(d^{-(n+m)}\), with \(n\) the particle-size exponent and \(m\) the dispersion exponent, and the required catalyst mass is its inverse. In a Monte Carlo for each candidate (10,000 draws) each draw takes shared exponents \(n\sim U(0.7,\,2.0)\) and \(m\sim U(0.8,\,1.2)\) and gives every size an independent log-normal activity factor with \(\sigma = 0.25\), from the exponent uncertainty of the Overbury series. The analysis of operating freedom optimizes temperature and O₂/CO ratio for each candidate. Catalyst life enters as \(\tau\propto d^{\lambda}\), with annual catalyst cost \(m_i/\tau_i\).

### CO₂ methanation case

The inputs of Moioli and Züttel[20] (catalyst loadings, the Ni-to-Ru volume ratio of 1.89 at 99.5% conversion, the single Ru stage and the two Ni stages with intermediate condensation, and their three bases for catalyst cost) were recorded with their locations in the source before the model was written. The catalyst-dependent cost per MWh of SNG comprises catalyst inventory at the design space velocity (3,000 h⁻¹), replacement at the metal prices of the ammonia model over a 5-year life, capital of the reactor stage with the vessel correlation and capital recovery of the ammonia model, and, for Ni, a second vessel with a condensation set at κ = 1.5 times its vessel cost. Scale is swept from 0.1 to 100 MW of SNG. The Monte Carlo (10,000 draws) samples \(r_V\) (1.5–2.5), space velocity (1,500–6,000 h⁻¹), bed density (1,200–1,800 kg m⁻³), Ru and Ni price multipliers (0.5–2), catalyst life (2–10 years), Ru recovery (0–90%), the basis for catalyst cost, κ (0.5–3) and the capital multiplier (0.8–1.2).

### Adaptive Catalyst Screening Agent

ACSA collects each paper and its Supporting Information, converts them to text marked by page and page images, and extracts every catalyst entry into a fixed schema in which each number carries its value, unit, qualifier, location and page. Extraction uses one strict JSON call to a language model per paper and pass (gpt-5.5, medium reasoning effort): a main pass over text and page images, a figure pass that digitizes the points of catalyst performance in the figures of the main text, and passes over the text, tables and figures of the Supporting Information. A printed value from the main text outranks a printed value from the Supporting Information, which outranks a plot reading. Prompts contain only the paper itself.

Accuracy is scored against two curated methanol datasets[25,26] and the 21 Re/TiO₂ entries of the hand-built case. Entries are matched by paper and catalyst name, or by paper, operating conditions and elemental composition for the dataset that lists compositions,[26] never by the values being scored. Strict tolerances are ±1 K for temperature, ±1% for pressure and STY, ±0.05 for the H₂/CO₂ ratio and ±0.5 percentage points for conversion and selectivity. Every disagreement between extracted and curated values was checked against the paper, and curated values that the paper contradicts were corrected to the printed value before scoring (Supplementary Table 8). A random sample of 100 extracted values, ten from each of 10 papers, was checked by the authors against the papers and Supporting Information (Supplementary Table 9). Of these, 58 are printed in tables, 36 in the text or captions and 6 are H₂/CO₂ ratios derived from printed feed compositions, and none is read off a plot. Ninety-eight are correct. One error is a CO₂ conversion printed for a stability test and attached to a table entry of the same catalyst, and the other a methanol formation rate recorded as STY without the paper's name for it. Removing the first changes no comparison at the catalyst level and adds one disagreement to the count at the point level for the loop with the inlet limit (51 instead of 50 of 81). The second converts to the same STY and changes no comparison.

Before new candidates are scored, ACSA runs the hand-built inputs of all three systems, and the extracted Re/TiO₂ and Au/TiO₂ inputs, through the same code path and requires the results of the hand-built cases to be reproduced. The lower bound from the descriptor alone decides which ammonia candidates need the full process optimization and the plant bound by purge which methanol candidates do, and all candidates were additionally evaluated in full to verify both bounds.
