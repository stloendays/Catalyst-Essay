# Multiscale process economics can reverse catalyst rankings

## Abstract

Computational catalyst screening usually ranks materials by atomistic descriptors or intrinsic rates, whereas industrial selection depends on how those properties affect reactor operation and cost. Here we examine when atomistic rankings are preserved after process optimization and economic evaluation, and whether the catalyst improvements required to change an economic decision are physically attainable. For ammonia synthesis, the activity ranking Ru > Os > Fe becomes Fe > Ru > Os after candidate-specific process optimization. Setting the Ru metal price equal to the Fe price reverses the Fe-Ru order after reoptimization, showing that metal price and the associated change in operating conditions jointly determine the result. Across 5,000 joint cost-uncertainty samples around the baseline case, Fe remains less costly than Ru. Backward design requires a 201.22-fold increase in Ru activity to reach the Fe cost, whereas the maximum increase allowed by the scaling relation is 2.525-fold. In CO2 hydrogenation to methanol, catalyst states are reordered through changes in selectivity, recycle and purge, while an Au/TiO2 CO-oxidation control preserves the original ranking. A decision agent coupled to deterministic scientific tools also reproduces the same scientific decision at budgets below the 206-CU fixed-policy threshold. These results show that catalyst rankings can change when catalyst properties strongly affect downstream process conditions, and that backward design can distinguish economically relevant targets from physically inaccessible ones.

## Introduction

Computational catalyst discovery commonly uses adsorption energies, reactivity descriptors, scaling relations, volcano plots and surrogate models to rank candidate materials.[1–6] Descriptor-based microkinetic models have made these relations useful across catalyst families, while first-principles multiscale models have connected elementary-step energetics to rates and operating conditions.[6,7] Previous work has also linked ammonia descriptors to reactor design and catalyst selection,[32] and formulated catalyst design and process optimization as coupled problems.[33,36] Laboratory reactor studies further show that catalyst rankings can depend on the test environment.[35] What remains less clear is whether the ordering of discrete catalyst candidates is preserved when atomistic predictions are propagated through candidate-specific process optimization and economic evaluation.

This question matters most for the leading candidates. A screening model can remain well correlated across a large candidate set while changing the identity of the material that would actually be selected. Global rank correlation therefore does not by itself establish that the top of the ranking is reliable. The same principle applies to uncertainty: adaptive-sampling and Bayesian approaches show that uncertainty is most useful when interpreted in relation to the quantity or decision of interest.[8–10]

We examine the problem in both directions. In the forward direction, catalyst properties are propagated through kinetics and process optimization to an economic objective. In the reverse direction, we ask what change in a catalyst property would be required to reach a specified economic target and whether that change lies within the range allowed by the underlying scaling relations. Integrated material-and-process design has previously inverted process objectives into molecular targets,[34] and catalyst-design frameworks have optimized descriptors within kinetic models.[33,36] Here the emphasis is on candidate ranking, economic parity and physical reachability.

We apply this analysis to two reaction systems and one control case. Ammonia synthesis provides a 15-metal screen in which activity and metal price affect catalyst inventory and the preferred operating conditions.[5,11,12] CO2 hydrogenation to methanol provides a different process structure in which selectivity influences reactant loss, purge, recycle and compression.[13–15] Au/TiO2 CO oxidation is used as a control in which particle size is mapped monotonically to catalytic activity under a common process model.[16–19]

These systems allow us to distinguish three questions: whether a catalyst ranking changes after process and economic evaluation, which physical or economic variables cause the change, and whether the catalyst improvement needed to alter the decision is physically accessible. We then test whether the same calculations can be selected adaptively under a finite computational budget. The numerical physics and economics remain deterministic; the language model only chooses which predefined calculations to perform.[20–31,37,38]

## Results

### Process optimization reverses the ranking of the leading ammonia catalysts

The 15-metal ammonia screen ranks Ru, Os and Fe first, second and third in intrinsic activity (Fig. 1c), consistent with the established activity landscape for transition-metal ammonia synthesis.[11,12] After candidate-specific process optimization and economic evaluation (Fig. 1a,d,e), the order becomes Fe, Ru and Os, with costs of 15.292, 22.031 and 25.832 US dollars per tonne of NH3, respectively (Fig. 1b,h).

This change is concentrated among the leading candidates. For Ru, Os and Fe, Spearman rho is -0.50 and Kendall tau is -0.33, while the Spearman correlation across all 15 metals remains 0.929 (Fig. 1f,g). Thus the overall ranking can remain strongly correlated even when the preferred catalyst changes at the top of the list. Agreement among the leading candidates is therefore more relevant to the industrial choice than full-set correlation alone.

### Metal price and process optimization jointly determine the Fe-Ru ranking

The leading metals favor different operating conditions. Fe has an interior optimum near 425 °C and 180 bar with a 30 °C separator, whereas Ru favors approximately 450 °C and 425 bar with a 25 °C separator; Os has a broad, shallow minimum at high pressure (Fig. 2b,c). The economic comparison therefore reflects both catalyst properties and the operating conditions preferred by each catalyst.

To identify the origin of the Fe-Ru reversal, we repeated the Ru optimization after setting its metal price equal to the Fe value of 8 US dollars per kilogram. All other model inputs, constraints and costing assumptions were unchanged. The Ru optimum shifts from 450 °C and 425 bar to 425 °C and 170 bar, and its optimized cost falls to 14.712 US dollars per tonne of NH3, 0.580 US dollars per tonne below Fe (Fig. 2b–e). A full Ru-price sweep, with process reoptimization at every price, places Fe-Ru parity at 163.76 US dollars per kilogram. At this point Ru operates at 425 °C and 185 bar with a 6.24 m3 catalyst bed (Fig. 2d). Across the sweep, the Ru optimum moves from 170 bar and a 9.4 m3 bed at 1 US dollar per kilogram to 420–430 bar, a 0 °C separator and a 0.014 m3 bed at 3 × 10^5 US dollars per kilogram. Metal price therefore affects the final ranking both directly and through its effect on the preferred process conditions.

The baseline Ru-Fe cost difference of 6.739 US dollars per tonne is distributed across several cost components. Fresh-feed compression contributes +4.632 US dollars per tonne, metal inventory +1.763, compressor capital +1.181 and refrigeration +0.629, while vessel pressure, recycle compression and reactor-base terms partly offset these contributions (Fig. 2a,e). This decomposition shows where the baseline difference arises, whereas the equal-price calculation shows how the optimized process responds when the metal-price difference is removed.

Uncertainty in the catalyst descriptor and uncertainty in economic parameters affect different outcomes. Across 1,000 descriptor-uncertainty samples, Fe satisfies the 90 m3 bed-volume limit in 79.9% of samples, ranks first economically in 68.1% (681/1000), and remains within the top three in 94.0%. The atomistic winner remains the economic winner in 28.2% of samples (Fig. 2f,g).

We next varied metal price, the CAPEX coefficient, electricity price and catalyst lifetime simultaneously around the baseline case. Across 5,000 preregistered samples, full process reoptimization gives P(C_Fe < C_Ru) = 1.000, with a minimum sampled Ru-Fe cost difference of 2.382 US dollars per tonne (Fig. 2h). The required Ru activity multiplier for economic parity is 70.78-fold, 174.27-fold and 462.00-fold at the 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles, respectively (Fig. 3b). The equal-price calculation therefore identifies a condition under which the ranking reverses, whereas the Monte Carlo analysis shows that the baseline Fe preference is robust to the economic variations considered here.

### Backward design shows that the required Ru activity is outside the accessible range

The forward analysis identifies the preferred catalyst but does not show how much Ru would need to improve to reach parity with Fe. We therefore multiplied Ru intrinsic activity by a factor alpha and reoptimized the full process at each value. Under the baseline economic parameters, Ru reaches the Fe cost only after a 201.22-fold activity increase (Fig. 3a,c).

The range permitted by the scaling relation is much smaller. At 673 K, the maximum activity increase along the scaling relation is 1.090-fold, and the largest increase found over the full process-state library is 2.525-fold. Under strict scaling, the lowest Ru cost is 21.398 US dollars per tonne of NH3 at E_N = -1.215 eV, which remains above the Fe cost (Fig. 3d,e). The baseline 201.22-fold requirement is therefore far beyond the range accessible by moving Ru along the present scaling relation. Even the 5th-percentile requirement under cost uncertainty, 70.78-fold, remains well above the 2.525-fold maximum (Fig. 3b).

Thus, within the model and scaling relation used here, improving Ru activity alone cannot make Ru economically competitive with Fe over the tested range of economic parameters.

### Selectivity, recycle and purge reshape the methanol ranking

We next applied the same analysis to four literature-derived Re/TiO2 catalyst-temperature states (Fig. 4a,b).[13] Using space-time yield per gram of Re as the catalyst metric, the order is 1 wt% Re at 250 °C, 1 wt% Re at 200 °C, 5 wt% Re at 200 °C and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C. After propagation through the recycle and separation model at the 2% purge used in the reference case, the economic order becomes 5 wt% Re at 200 °C, 1 wt% Re at 200 °C, 1 wt% Re at 250 °C and 5 wt% Re at 250 °C, with net production costs of 943, 967, 975 and 1,258 euros per tonne, respectively (Fig. 4c).

The state with the highest space-time yield per gram of Re falls from first to third economically. Across the four states, Spearman rho is 0.20, Kendall tau is 0.00 and three of six pairwise comparisons invert (Fig. 4e). Methane formation consumes reactants and increases gas accumulation, purge, recycle and compression requirements. At the 5 wt% Re, 250 °C state, the local economic leverage is 0.37579 for methane suppression, 0.05883 for single-pass conversion and 0.00289 for space-time yield (Fig. 4a,g). These sensitivities are consistent with previous CO2-to-methanol process studies in which recycle, compression and separation make substantial contributions to plant performance.[14,15]

The result persists across the full purge range considered. Over 396 purge levels from 0.5% to 40%, the state with the highest space-time yield per gram of Re is never the least-cost option; Spearman rho never exceeds 0.40 and at least two of six pairwise comparisons remain inverted. Optimizing purge separately for each catalyst state changes the detailed order but does not restore the space-time-yield ranking (Fig. 4f).

The economic order is also unchanged in all 5,000 cost-parameter samples (Fig. 4d). In the reference methanol cost model, Re purchase and replacement are not included, so sampled metal price and lifetime do not affect the result. An additional calculation that includes active-Re replacement, using the Re inventory inferred from space-time yield, also preserves the same four-state order in all 5,000 samples.

### Whether rankings change depends on how catalyst properties affect the process

Ammonia and methanol show different routes by which catalyst properties affect process cost (Fig. 5a). In ammonia, intrinsic activity and metal price determine catalyst inventory and the preferred operating conditions, which in turn change compression, reactor and equipment costs. In methanol, selectivity changes reactant losses, gas accumulation, purge, recycle and compression. Absolute costs are not compared across the two reactions; the comparison concerns how catalyst properties enter each process model.

We used Au/TiO2 CO oxidation as a control case in which the mapping from particle size to catalyst requirement is monotonic. Low-temperature CO oxidation on supported Au is well known to depend on particle size and support effects.[16,17] The present calculation uses the absolute-rate anchor reported by Janssens et al.[18] and the particle-size dependence measured by Overbury et al.[19] at the closest Au loading. Candidate states differ only in Au particle size from 2 to 6 nm under the same operating condition and process model.

Under this common mapping, intrinsic activity and required catalyst mass preserve the same order, 2 > 3 > 4 > 5 > 6 nm. Spearman rho and Kendall tau are both 1.000, no pairwise comparison inverts, and all 10,000 parameter samples drawn from ranges reported in the literature preserve the complete order (Fig. 5b–d). When moderate candidate-specific kinetic and operating variations are introduced, the exact order is retained in 92.16% of 10,000 samples over 273.15–293.15 K, with mean rho = 0.99214. Extending the temperature range to 313.15 K reduces exact preservation to 72.62% while retaining mean rho = 0.96802 (Fig. 5e). Thus, adding process and economic calculations does not by itself cause a ranking inversion; inversions arise when catalyst properties alter downstream operating requirements strongly enough to change relative costs (Fig. 5f).

### A decision agent can reproduce the analysis under limited computational budgets

We then asked whether the same scientific decision could be reached without evaluating every calculation in a fixed sequence. The agent selects among 11 predefined scientific actions under a finite compute-unit (CU) budget, while all kinetic, process and economic calculations are performed by deterministic tools (Fig. 6a). A run is considered complete only when it gives the correct economic winner, Fe-Ru ordering and reachability classification; numerical accuracy of the Ru-Fe parity multiplier is evaluated separately.

The fixed deterministic policy requires 206 CU. The strongest model reaches the complete decision in 20/20 runs at a 75-CU allowance, with a median of 52.5 CU to the point from which the decision remains correct through the end of the run. At 50 CU, 13/20 runs are complete, although the economic winner and Fe-Ru ordering are correct in all 20 runs (Fig. 6c,e). The weaker models do not reach the same performance at the tested budgets. Their errors arise mainly from budget allocation and action ordering; replacing free-form tool calls with a structured interface removes the mini model's interface errors but does not restore complete decisions (Fig. 6c,g).

Two lower bounds help interpret these computational costs. If only the scored outputs are computed, the shortest legal action sequence requires 7 CU. A scientifically complete reference calculation requires 22 CU because it includes a process window, activity screening for all 15 metals, optimization of the minimum unresolved Fe/Ru/Os set, backward design and reachability analysis. Relative to this 22-CU reference, the 75-CU allowance is 3.41 times larger, the median 52.5-CU decision-stable cost is 2.39 times larger, and the 206-CU fixed-policy threshold is 9.36 times larger (Fig. 6b,f).

The qualitative decision converges before the exact parity multiplier. The baseline Ru-to-Fe parity multiplier is recovered in 9/20 runs at 75 CU and in 20/20 runs at 225 CU. A run can therefore reach the correct catalyst-selection decision before every intermediate numerical quantity has converged, provided that the remaining numerical error does not change the reachability classification.

At low budgets, the strongest model frequently reduces the process search to windows smaller than the full 14,136-state domain. Under a non-binding 5,000-CU allowance, it uses no reduced process window in any of 20 runs. Median decision stabilization rises to 566 CU, after which the agent spends a further median 148 CU before stopping, giving a median final cost of 714 CU (Fig. 6d,e). These results indicate that reduced process windows are used mainly when the computational budget is restrictive, and that the agent continues computing after the scientific decision has stabilized when the budget is non-binding.

The agent results therefore define a budget-dependent operating regime. Under constrained budgets, the strongest model can reproduce the scientific decision before the fixed policy completes. With a non-binding budget, it uses substantially more computation. The practical role of the agent is to select which deterministic calculations to run when computational resources are limited; it does not alter the underlying scientific models.

## Discussion

Our main finding is that a catalyst ranking can remain strongly correlated overall while changing among the candidates that matter for selection. In ammonia, the 15-metal ranking is largely preserved globally, yet Ru, Os and Fe reorder after process optimization and cost evaluation. This distinction matters because screening effort is concentrated on the leading candidates rather than on the full candidate set.

The equal-price analysis identifies the origin of the Fe-Ru reversal. When Ru and Fe metal prices are equal, Ru becomes less costly after reoptimization, and the price sweep places parity at 163.76 US dollars per kilogram. The preferred Ru operating point also shifts from high to lower pressure as the Ru price is reduced, changing compression and capital requirements. Metal price therefore affects the final ranking both directly and through its effect on the preferred process conditions.

The equal-price calculation and the local uncertainty analysis answer different questions. The former shows that the ranking can reverse if the Ru price is reduced sufficiently; the latter shows that Fe remains less costly throughout 5,000 sampled economic conditions around the baseline case. Descriptor uncertainty affects feasibility and ranking differently: Fe is the economic winner in 68.1% of samples but remains within the top three in 94.0%. Evaluating uncertainty against the actual selection criterion therefore provides more useful information than reporting parameter variance alone.

Backward design adds a second test. Economic parity requires a 201.22-fold increase in Ru activity under the baseline assumptions. Cost uncertainty lowers the 5th-percentile requirement to 70.78-fold, but this value still greatly exceeds the 2.525-fold maximum allowed by the scaling relation. Within the present model, incremental movement along the same Ru scaling relation cannot close the economic gap. A competitive Ru-based catalyst would therefore need a change that is not represented by activity improvement along this scaling path, such as a different catalyst class, improved stability or a different process configuration.

The methanol and Au/TiO2 results show that ranking changes are not inevitable. In methanol, selectivity affects recycle, purge and compression strongly enough to reorder the catalyst states. In the Au/TiO2 control, a common monotonic relation between particle size and catalyst requirement preserves the ranking throughout parameter ranges reported in the literature. The key determinant is the extent to which catalyst properties alter downstream operating requirements and relative costs.

The agent study shows that adaptive selection of calculations can reduce computational requirements when the budget is limited, but the effect is not universal. The strongest model reaches the complete decision at a 75-CU allowance, below the 206-CU fixed-policy threshold. With a non-binding budget, however, median decision stabilization increases to 566 CU and median final spending to 714 CU. This behavior is consistent with budget-dependent reduction of the search space rather than a general tendency to minimize computation. Keeping the numerical science deterministic while allowing the agent to choose calculations provides a practical way to automate repeated screening without changing the underlying physical models.

Together, these results suggest a practical sequence for catalyst screening: propagate candidate properties through a process and economic model, identify the variables responsible for changes in ranking, invert the model to define an economic parity target, and compare that target with the physically accessible catalyst space. When the analysis must be repeated across many candidate sets, adaptive action selection can prioritize the calculations most relevant to the final decision. This approach links catalyst screening more directly to industrial selection while retaining a clear connection to the underlying kinetics and process models.

## Methods

### Multiscale decision analysis

Each catalytic system is evaluated with its own process-level objective. The ammonia model uses catalyst-dependent NH3 production cost; the methanol model uses net production cost for the recycle and separation process; and the Au/TiO2 control uses required catalyst mass under a common process mapping. Absolute costs are interpreted only within each reaction and are not compared across systems.

Within each system, rankings before and after process evaluation are compared using Spearman and Kendall correlations, pairwise inversions and top-rank identity. Analyses of the leading candidates are reported separately from full-set statistics so that changes at the top of the ranking are not obscured by agreement among weaker candidates. Backward design varies a selected catalyst property, reoptimizes the process and identifies the value required for economic parity. This value is then compared with the range accessible under the corresponding catalyst-property or scaling relation. Causal tests change one predefined input while preserving the other model assumptions and repeating the same downstream optimization.

### Ammonia synthesis

The ammonia model contains the 15-metal activity screen, scaling and kinetic relations, catalyst productivity and inventory, candidate-specific process optimization, pressure-dependent equipment capital and seven catalyst-dependent cost components. Temperature, pressure and separator temperature are reoptimized for each candidate over the full 14,136-state process library. Descriptor uncertainty is propagated through 1,000 samples; Fe feasibility is evaluated against the 90 m3 bed-volume limit, and economic winner, preservation of the atomistic winner and top-three membership are recorded separately.

For the Ru-price test, only the Ru metal purchase price is changed. It is first set equal to the Fe value of 8 US dollars per kilogram, after which Ru is reoptimized over the full process library. The Ru-price sweep repeats this optimization at 241 logarithmically spaced prices between 1 and 3 × 10^5 US dollars per kilogram together with the two reference prices. Fe-Ru parity is located by bisection in log price. The baseline Fe-Ru cost difference is decomposed into the same seven cost components.

The preregistered economic Monte Carlo analysis uses 5,000 samples with seed 20260920. Fe and Ru metal-price multipliers are sampled independently from a log-uniform distribution over 0.5–2.0. A common CAPEX multiplier is sampled uniformly over 0.8–1.2, electricity price uniformly over 20–100 US dollars per megawatt-hour, and catalyst lifetime uniformly over 5–20 years. Fe and Ru are reoptimized for every sample. The Ru activity multiplier required for parity, alpha*, is recalculated against the sample-specific reoptimized Fe cost.

For backward design, Ru intrinsic activity is varied directly and the full process is reoptimized at each activity multiplier. Reachability is evaluated along the descriptor path defined by the ammonia scaling relation and over the full process-state library. The kinetic and scaling relations follow the established transition-metal ammonia-synthesis literature.[5,11,12]

### CO2-to-methanol

The methanol analysis uses a recycle and separation model applied to four Re/TiO2 states derived from the 1 and 5 wt% catalysts reported by Gothe et al.[13] The reference comparison uses 2% purge. Upstream rankings are reconstructed from space-time yield per gram of Re, single-pass methanol yield and space-time yield per gram of catalyst; the downstream ranking uses net production cost. Re purchase price and catalyst replacement are not included in the reference objective. Purge is varied from 0.5% to 40% over 396 levels, with an additional optimization in which purge is selected separately for each catalyst state. Local economic leverage for space-time yield, conversion and methane suppression is evaluated at the 5 wt% Re, 250 °C state. The process interpretation follows established CO2-to-methanol assessments in which recycle, compression and separation materially affect plant performance.[14,15]

The 5,000-sample economic analysis propagates the sampled CAPEX and electricity terms through the candidate-specific methanol cost model. Because Re purchase and replacement are absent from the reference objective, sampled metal price and lifetime do not affect that calculation. An additional analysis includes active-Re replacement using the Re inventory inferred from space-time yield together with the sampled Re price and catalyst lifetime.

### Au/TiO2 rank-preservation control

The Au/TiO2 control compares 2–6 nm particles under a common operating condition and process mapping. The reference rate, Au loading and dispersion are anchored to Janssens et al.,[18] and the particle-size dependence is taken from the 4.5 wt% Au/TiO2 series reported by Overbury et al.[19] The broader physical basis for low-temperature Au/TiO2 activity and particle-size effects follows the established literature.[16,17] Rank preservation is evaluated in 10,000 parameter samples drawn from ranges reported in these studies. An additional analysis introduces candidate-specific kinetic variation and independently optimizes temperature and O2/CO ratio within the specified literature-based ranges.

### Decision-agent evaluation

The decision agent operates over an 11-action scientific interface. At each step, the language model receives the remaining CU budget and the results available so far and selects an admissible action; the environment then performs the numerical calculation. The agent does not alter the physical models, process constraints, economic equations or ground-truth decision geometry. A run is counted as correct only if it identifies the economic winner, the Fe-Ru ordering and the reachability classification. Accuracy of the backward parity multiplier is evaluated separately.

The fixed deterministic policy completes the scientific decision at 206 CU. The language models are tested on the same anonymized task without access to the ground-truth outputs. The strongest model is evaluated around the fixed-policy threshold, weaker models are evaluated at selected budgets, and the strongest model is also tested with a non-binding 5,000-CU allowance. A reduced process window is counted only when it contains fewer than the full 14,136 process states and is subsequently used by a successful scoped calculation. Decision-stable CU is the cumulative spend at the first point after which the complete decision remains correct through the final action.

Two deterministic lower bounds are calculated. The shortest legal sequence that produces only the scored outputs requires 7 CU. A scientifically complete reference calculation additionally constructs a process window, computes activity for all 15 candidates, resolves the minimum Fe/Ru/Os process set, performs backward design and evaluates reachability; this sequence requires 22 CU. All reported agent costs are calculated from the retained action traces using the same CU ledger and scoring rules.
