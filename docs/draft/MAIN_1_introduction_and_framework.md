# 1. Introduction

Catalyst discovery ranks materials with atomic-scale proxies: adsorption energies, descriptors, scaling-derived barriers and intrinsic turnover frequencies. Industrial selection answers a different question — which catalyst delivers the lowest product cost once kinetics, catalyst inventory, reactor size, operating severity, recycle, separation and replacement are coupled and the process is reoptimized for each candidate. The two questions have no reason to share an answer, and in ammonia synthesis they do not. A 15-metal screen ordered on intrinsic activity places Ru first, Os second and Fe third; after candidate-specific process and economic optimization the feasible economic order is Fe, Ru, Os, at **15.292**, **22.031** and **25.832 USD per tonne NH3** (Fig. 1).

The divergence is local rather than global, and that is what makes it consequential. Across the full 15-metal set the raw activity-to-cost Spearman correlation is **0.929**, so atomistic screening remains broadly informative about which elements are irrelevant. Restricted to the three highest-activity candidates the same correlation is **-0.50**, with Kendall tau **-0.33**. Rolling the retained set outward recovers the agreement — Spearman **-0.500** at K = 3, **0.700** at K = 5 and **0.929** at K = 15 — so the screen is accurate over the part of the candidate space no one is choosing from and inverted over the part where selection actually happens (Fig. 2). A high global rank correlation is therefore not evidence that a screening metric supports the decision it is being used for.

The inversion is not a metal-price artefact. At the Fe optimum, metal inventory contributes **0.070 USD/t** of a **15.292 USD/t** total, against **9.660 USD/t** of fresh compression electricity, **3.875 USD/t** of compressor CAPEX and **0.807 USD/t** of converter-shell pressure premium. At the Ru optimum, metal inventory contributes **1.833 USD/t** of **22.031 USD/t**, while fresh compression electricity is **14.291 USD/t** — identical to the Os value, because both settle at 450 C and 425 bar. What separates the candidates is the operating point each of them drives the plant toward, propagated into equipment and utility burden. The catalyst enters the cost through its activity and the inventory that activity implies: at their own optima the reactor volume is **17.1 m3** for Fe against **0.066 m3** for Ru and **0.029 m3** for Os, a difference that the economics then reprices through vessel and compression terms rather than through the catalyst itself.

Uncertainty does not propagate uniformly through this chain either. Over 1,000 Monte Carlo draws on the descriptor distribution, Fe satisfies the 90 m3 bed-volume criterion in **79.9%** of draws, retains Top-1 rank in **28.2%**, and remains inside the actionable Top-3 in **94.0%**. The identity of the winner is genuinely uncertain while the shortlist is nearly stable, so what a further calculation can usefully change depends on which of those two questions the decision requires. An atomistic error bar alone does not say whether resolving it would change anything downstream.

The missing object is therefore not another activity model but the causal chain connecting a catalyst property to the industrial decision, together with a way to ask that chain questions in reverse. This work asks four. First, when does an atomic catalyst ranking survive multiscale propagation to the industrial decision? Second, when and why does it invert? Third, which uncertainty is worth reducing for that decision, as opposed to which uncertainty is largest? Fourth, what catalyst-property improvement does a given economic target require, and is that improvement reachable on the admissible catalyst-property manifold?

A framework that can produce an inversion must also be able to fail to produce one, or the result is an artefact of adding model layers. The falsification test is a literature-calibrated Au/TiO2 CO-oxidation control evaluated at a common fixed process condition, where candidate states differ only in Au particle size. There the activity and downstream catalyst-burden orders are identical, **2 > 3 > 4 > 5 > 6 nm**, with Spearman **1.000**, Kendall **1.000**, **0** pairwise inversions, and the complete order preserved in **10,000 of 10,000** predefined literature-envelope draws. A semi-open extension that lets each particle size search temperature and O2/CO ratio inside a literature-constrained window preserves the exact order in **92.16%** of 10,000 draws, with mean Spearman **0.99214** in the primary 273.15–293.15 K envelope. Preservation is the default behaviour of this implementation under a monotonic downstream mapping; inversion is a property of the coupling, not of the method.

This paper contributes that chain, its inverse, and a decision layer that decides where to spend the compute the chain consumes. Forward propagation carries a descriptor through scaling, microkinetics, catalyst inventory, candidate-specific reactor and process optimization over **14,136** admissible process states, and pressure-dependent economics, to an industrial ranking with propagated uncertainty. Backward inversion converts an economic target into a required catalyst-property change and tests that requirement against the scaling manifold: Fe cost parity requires a **201.22-fold** activity gain for Ru, against a scaling-consistent headroom of **1.090x** at the 673 K reference condition and **2.525x** at most over the frozen process-state library, which makes the activity-only route infeasible under the current process model rather than merely difficult. A second reaction, CO2-to-methanol over four Re/TiO2 catalyst–temperature states evaluated through an explicit recycle and separation loop at **2%** purge, reshuffles the ranking through a different channel, with Spearman **0.20**, Kendall **0.00** and **3 of 6** pairwise comparisons inverted between the intrinsic per-Re screen and net production cost. The cross-reaction comparison is stated at pathway level only — ammonia resolves an activity–inventory and reactor-demand route, methanol a selectivity–recycle route — because metric equivalence between the two historical leverage definitions could not be established from the retained source history; no cross-reaction numerical ratio is claimed here, and Fig. 9A is a qualitative pathway panel. Finally, because each link of the chain can be computed at variable resolution and a real project cannot afford all of them, we add a decision layer that allocates finite scientific compute by its value to the downstream decision and evaluate it on a frozen, closed-book benchmark whose protocol was fixed before any model was run.

# 2. Framework

## 2.1 Forward propagation

The forward chain is deterministic and runs in one direction: descriptor / DFT → scaling relations and BEP closure → microkinetics → catalyst productivity and inventory → reactor and process optimization → economics → industrial ranking. Each stage consumes the previous stage's output and adds one physically distinct constraint, and every candidate passes through the same model family and the same engineering constraints.

The entry point is the nitrogen binding energy E_N. Scaling relations and a BEP closure convert it into the elementary energetics of the ammonia mechanism, and a microkinetic model returns turnover frequency and surface coverage at a given operating state. Two distinct activity readings follow, and the distinction carries the argument. At the fixed atomic reference condition the descriptor screen orders the candidates **Ru > Os > Fe**. Once each candidate is allowed to select its own optimal operating state, the log TOF attained there is **-2.188** for Ru, **-1.562** for Os and **-4.858** for Fe: the same model, evaluated at candidate-specific optima, no longer preserves the reference ordering, because the optimiser trades intrinsic activity against the operating severity needed to realise it.

**The cost metric is a reduced, catalyst-discriminating one, and this is a design choice rather than an approximation.**
Every ammonia cost reported here is the reduced catalyst-dependent NH3 cost defined by the frozen manifest: active-metal
inventory and replacement, the reactor base, the pressure-attributable converter-shell premium, fresh-gas and recycle
compression electricity, refrigeration, and compressor bare-module capital. For the Fe optimum these pools are 0.070,
0.115, 0.807, 9.660, 0.765, 0.000 and 3.875 USD per tonne respectively, summing to the reported 15.292 USD/t. Pools that
every candidate shares in the same proportion — hydrogen and nitrogen feedstock, air separation, loop heat exchangers,
the separator vessel, piping, labour and general utilities — are outside the boundary by construction, and the manifest
records the omission and its direction.

The consequence matters for how the numbers should be read. These are **not** plant production costs, and they are one
to two orders of magnitude below a full technoeconomic estimate. They are differentials on the subset of cost that
actually depends on which catalyst is chosen, which is the only subset that can change a ranking. Adding a common-mode
pool shifts every candidate by the same amount and cannot reorder them; including such pools would inflate the absolute
level, compress every relative difference, and obscure the decision the framework exists to resolve. The lever
eligibility classification makes the same distinction operational: electricity price and discount rate are classified
common-mode precisely because they scale pools shared by all candidates. Every quantity in this paper that carries a
decision — the Fe/Ru/Os ordering, the 6.739 USD/t gap, the 1.441 cost ratio and the 201.22-fold parity multiplier — is a
differential on this declared boundary.


Intrinsic rate becomes an engineering quantity through inventory. A target production rate and a turnover frequency fix the catalyst loading and hence the bed volume, which sets reactor sizing and interacts with an engineering cap, here 90 m3. This is where the atomic and the industrial objectives first stop tracking each other: the same production duty requires **17.1 m3** of Fe bed against **0.066 m3** of Ru, although the cap is not binding for either at its optimum, with slack of **72.94 m3** for Fe and **89.93 m3** for Ru.

Process conditions are reoptimized candidate by candidate rather than compared at a common operating point, because a fixed operating point would embed a prior about which catalyst the plant was built for. The admissible domain contains **14,136** process states spanning a **10–1000 bar** pressure grid together with reactor and separator temperature. The resulting optima are genuinely different regimes: approximately **425 C / 180 bar / 30 C** for Fe, **450 C / 425 bar / 25 C** for Ru, and for Os a broad shallow high-pressure minimum in which the 220–430 bar band lies within **0.1 USD/t** of the numerical argmin at 425 bar (Fig. 4). Reporting Os as a point value would overstate the resolution of the model.

Economics then prices the operating point and the catalyst together. The cost model resolves metal inventory, a reactor volume term, a converter-shell pressure premium, fresh and recycle compression electricity, refrigeration and compressor CAPEX. The decomposition is what makes the ranking interpretable: Fe pays **0.807 USD/t** of vessel pressure premium and **3.875 USD/t** of compressor CAPEX but almost nothing for metal, whereas Os pays **4.120 USD/t** of refrigeration — against **0.629** for Ru and **0.000** for Fe — because its optimum sits at a separator temperature of 0 C. The industrial ranking is the ordering of these totals after a feasibility gate on bed volume, and it is Fe > Ru > Os.

Uncertainty is propagated through the whole chain rather than reported at its head. Drawing the descriptor 1,000 times at a frozen Fe width of **0.227 eV** gives feasibility probabilities of **100%** for Ru and Os, **79.9%** for Fe, **24.5%** for Rh and **7.6%** for Ir, and the dominant winner transitions from the atomic to the feasible economic ranking are Ru→Fe in **29.5%** of draws and Os→Fe in **27.4%** (Fig. 3). The decision-relevant statement is not the width of the descriptor distribution but the fraction of that distribution that crosses a rank boundary or a feasibility threshold.

## 2.2 Backward design

The same chain run in reverse answers a different class of question: industrial economic target → required catalyst-property improvement → descriptor and scaling manifold → reachable or unreachable. Backward design does not search for a material. It converts an economic statement into a property requirement and then asks whether the physics that generated the forward chain can supply it.

For ammonia the target is cost parity between the two candidates that define the decision. Holding the model fixed and reoptimizing the full process at every step of a Ru activity sweep, parity with Fe is reached at an activity multiplier of **201.22x**, at a break-even operating state of **425 C / 190 bar / 30 C** and an Fe parity cost of **15.292 USD/t** (Fig. 5). The requirement is well posed because the process is reoptimized underneath it; a fixed-condition sweep would report a different and weaker number.

Reachability is then tested on the manifold that produced the requirement, not against expert judgement. Moving Ru along the scaling-consistent descriptor path gives an activity headroom of **1.0899x** at the 673 K reference condition and **2.5246x** at most over the frozen process-state library, and the lowest strict-scaling Ru cost is **21.398 USD/t at E_N = -1.215 eV**, still above Fe (Fig. 6). The gap between **201.22x** required and at most **2.525x** reachable is the result. It is an activity-only infeasibility signal under the current process model, operating constraints and scaling-consistent design path, and it redirects effort away from an activity-only route rather than setting an experimental target.

Backward design also applies to levers that are not catalyst properties, and there the first step is an eligibility classification rather than a sensitivity ranking. On the canonical run, metal recovery fraction and catalyst lifetime are catalyst-discriminatory because they enter the metal cost with a candidate-dependent weight; electricity price and discount rate scale cost pools that every candidate shares and are classified common-mode; an upward relaxation of the bed cap is an inactive constraint; and the upper pressure bound, a lever in the archived FINAL-1.0 model, is no longer one because the Ru and Os optima are interior to the 10–1000 bar grid. Among the eligible levers, metal recovery is the strongest: it closes **0.685** of the Fe–Ru cost-*ratio* gap at its plausible bound of 0.95 and **0.911** at 0.99, against **0.161** for catalyst lifetime at 20 years. Raising recovery lowers both metals' inventory charge, so at a recovery of 0.99 Ru reaches **15.715 USD/t** while Fe itself falls to **15.215 USD/t** from its baseline 15.292 USD/t, and the order is preserved. A lever that closes most of a gap is still not a lever that reverses a decision.

The same inversion applies to uncertainty, which is how the third question of the Introduction becomes quantitative. Inverting the descriptor-interval condition on the frozen NH3-FINAL-1.1 run shows that raising Fe feasibility from an analytic baseline of **0.8120** to a target of **0.95** at the 90 m3 cap requires the Fe descriptor standard deviation to fall from **0.2271 eV** to **0.1223 eV**, or, at the frozen width, a bed cap of **6,480 m3**. The analytic baseline sits **1.3 percentage points** above the frozen 1,000-draw Monte Carlo value of **0.7990**, about one binomial standard error (**1.26 percentage points** at n = 1000 and p = 0.8), so the inverted values are reported on the analytic scale. Stated this way, an uncertainty-reduction request becomes a testable specification — a required error bar, or an engineering relaxation no plant would grant — rather than a general call for better numbers.

## 2.3 Decision-aware AI harness

Everything in Sections 2.1 and 2.2 is Layer A: a deterministic multiscale harness that runs frozen inputs through scaling and BEP closure, microkinetics, reactor and process optimization, economics, and ranking, uncertainty and backward design, to reproducible outputs. Layer A computes correctly whatever it is asked to compute. It does not decide what is worth computing, and under a finite compute budget that decision determines whether the chain closes at all.

Layer B is the decision layer, and it acts only at interfaces where a numerical optimizer cannot determine the scientifically appropriate next action: which uncertainty to reduce next, which catalyst lever carries the highest downstream decision value, whether a backward target is reachable, and whether computation should stop because the industrial decision is already resolved. Its cycle is to inspect the current evidence → identify the decision-sensitive uncertainty or lever → choose an admissible scientific action → execute the corresponding Layer A tool → update the ranking, feasibility and reachability evidence → stop, continue or redirect. Layer B never rewrites canonical scientific state; promotion of a result to canonical status remains a separate, explicit action outside the loop. The contribution of the AI layer is decision allocation, not workflow automation, and the framework is built so that the two can be told apart.

The decision layer is evaluated on DISCOVER V1, a closed-book, budgeted benchmark built on the frozen ammonia environment. It exposes eleven fine-grained scientific actions — `INSPECT_CANDIDATES`, `COMPUTE_ACTIVITY`, `READ_PROPERTY_UNCERTAINTY`, `BUILD_PROCESS_WINDOW`, `OPTIMIZE_PROCESS`, `READ_COST_BREAKDOWN`, `RUN_MC`, `TEST_LEVER`, `BACKWARD`, `TEST_REACHABILITY` and `CHECK_MODEL_VALIDITY` — none of which returns the answer, and the ground truth is isolated from the agent and read only by the scorer. Candidates are presented as a seeded anonymous permutation, because a zero-tool probe showed that named metals let a language model guess that Ru is highly active or that Fe is economically plausible without performing the multiscale work; winner-only accuracy is for the same reason not accepted as evidence.

Compute is budgeted in scientific units rather than tokens. One CU is 1,000 microkinetic state solves, with a measured benchmark reference of about **21.8 ms per CU**. The action costs follow from the forward chain directly: a full-domain process window over the **14,136** admissible states costs about **111 CU**, a single-candidate optimization about **29 CU**, a 100-draw Monte Carlo about **17 CU**, a backward calculation about **1 CU**, and a reachability test **2–4 CU**. A budget that cannot cover full enumeration therefore forces a genuine allocation choice between resolving the process window, resolving the uncertainty, and closing the backward and reachability steps — the same trade-off a research group faces, made explicit and measurable.

A complete decision is defined before any run and requires all four of the economic winner, the decision pair, the backward target and the reachability verdict; the scorer additionally checks the ranking inversion, the break-even value, decision completeness and budget efficiency. The comparator is a deterministic fixed-value-of-information policy D, one of the frozen non-LLM baselines A–D, whose constants were fixed before the formal evaluation, so that the benchmark tests whether adaptive allocation helps rather than assuming it. DISCOVER V1 was frozen before cross-model evaluation, and any change to its task, prompt, action schema, cost model, scorer, stopping rule or policy-D constants would define a new benchmark rather than a repair of this one. Section 3.7 reports the resulting behaviour as an operating envelope conditioned on model capability and compute budget, with a lower affordability floor and an upper boundary, rather than as a general claim about the efficiency of adaptive allocation; Section 4.6 develops that reading.
